Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat): the class completes the section on permissible (unowned) water, covering who may take it, priority in irrigation, ownership of wells and water taken into a vessel, the duty to give surplus water to people and animals, and how a shared canal is divided. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali, International Islamic Fiqh Academy, Third Session, Amman, October 1986): the class reads four resolutions: Zakat on company shares (deferred), investing Zakat funds in income-generating projects, the seven methods of artificial insemination ("test-tube babies"), and a brief opening of the resuscitation-devices resolution, which is deferred to the next class.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat

1. Permissible Water (al-Miyah al-Mubaha)

Background: water not owned by anyone: valleys such as the Nile, mountain springs, and rain torrents. People share it equally, per the hadith in Abu Dawud: "People are partners in three: water, pasture, and fire."

Ruling:

  • No one may enclose it (tahajjur), and the ruler may not grant it (iqta') by consensus. Per the gloss of 'Abd al-Hamid this covers both a grant of ownership and a grant of mere access. The teacher criticized modern practice in tourist areas where river banks and shorelines are fenced off and people barred for the sake of investment.
  • If water or its access point (mashra') is too narrow for everyone: the first arrival has priority; otherwise lots are drawn. A person in real danger of thirst comes before others, and one who needs to drink comes before one who needs to irrigate. The teacher added that human drinking comes before animals.
  • Water of unknown origin that is in the hand of one person or a group is not treated as permissible, because "the hand is evidence of ownership" (the teacher told students to underline this maxim, as it is hard to find stated so directly). Al-Adhra'i qualified this: it applies only when the source is in their own property. If the source is mawat land or a public river (like the Tigris) the water stays permissible even if it passes through their land. The teacher noted that al-Nihaya and al-Mughni adopt al-Adhra'i's view, and gave the example of flood water crossing farms in Tarim and moving on to the next farm.
  • Where the amount, timing, or manner of use is unknown, the established custom ('ada mudtarrida) governs.

2. Fatwas on Obstructing or Diverting Water

  • Someone whose land has a customary share of permissible water, and another person digs something that diverts the water away from it: the diverter is sinful, but Ibn Hajar rejects the fatwa that he also owes rent for the land's idle period. The teacher agreed that only sin applies, since the diverter never took control of the land (compared to the case of preventing someone from channeling water to his land so that the crop dies: no liability).
  • Three plots on a stream (upper, middle, lower): if the upper owner wants to irrigate through the middle one with its owner's consent, the lower owner may object, and passage of time does not prove the upper owner has a right to the middle share. If one person owns upper, middle and lower land, he may give the middle plot its own independent share so they drink together; the lower neighbor cannot object, as there is no harm and often the water arrives sooner.

3. Irrigation Priority

  • If a group wants to irrigate from permissible water and it is not enough for all, the "upper" land irrigates first (the one nearest the source), once or more, as long as it needs, even if the lower one's crop dies before its turn. If water is plentiful, everyone irrigates when they want.
  • This applies when they reclaimed together or the order is unknown. If the lower land was reclaimed first, it has priority and can bar someone from reclaiming closer to the river. The teacher explained that "upper" in the text means the earliest-reclaimed. If lands are equally near the source and the first reclaimer is unknown, draw lots. Existing owners may prevent a new reclaimer if he would narrow their supply.
  • How much: the hadith says each keeps water until it reaches the ankles. The teacher explained this illustrates the measure of need, not a fixed number, and the relied-upon position is to follow local custom and need (the hadith reflects Hijaz custom). If the land is uneven, each section is watered separately so the low part does not exceed what is needed.

4. Water Taken Into a Vessel; Wells

  • Water taken into a vessel is owned (Ibn al-Mundhir cites consensus), and returning it to the river does not make him a partner in the river. The same applies to channeling it into a pool, a sealed basin, or waterwheel buckets (Ibn al-Salah). Water that merely enters his land by a flood or by a channel he dug is not owned by entering; he only has priority over it. The teacher noted the two Shaykhs elsewhere treat it as owned, and said that should be understood only where he secured it (e.g. locked).
  • A well dug in barren land for personal use (irtifaq: drinking for himself and his animals, not for ownership): he is more entitled to the water he needs, even for crops, until he moves on. If he leaves, his right lapses even if he returns, unless he left for a need intending to return and was not gone long (al-Adhra'i). The example given was nomads digging a well for their flocks.
  • A well dug for passers-by, or with no particular intention, is like any other person's share: people share it, whether or not he declared it a waqf, and he may not fill it in even if he dug it for himself, since people's rights attached to it.
  • A well dug for ownership, or a spring emerging in his own property: he owns its water, and by the same reasoning whatever comes from his property such as oil or salt. The teacher said this includes petroleum, because it is the fruit of his ownership.
  • A tenant of a house may use the house's well water, since a lease can incidentally include ownership of an object (like milk with a nursing contract), but he may not sell it. Ibn Hajar gave a fatwa on a bathhouse tenant who wanted to sell well water: prohibited, as it reduces the water and may harm the landlord.

5. Duty to Give Surplus Water

  • Whether he owns the water or not, he need not give surplus to someone else's crops or trees, even if the crops would perish.
  • He must let animals drink under conditions: the animals' owner has permissible pasture nearby and no other permissible water, and it does not harm his own crops or animals; otherwise the owner may take the water or drive it to them. The teacher explained the owner is not obliged to water them himself, but if their owner will not water them, it becomes obligatory out of the sanctity of life.
  • In a true necessity he must give for a respected life (a human), even over his own animals or crops. A human comes before animals.
  • No duty to give water for wudu (tayammum is a substitute), just as one is not obliged to give clothes for prayer.
  • He may refuse someone whose entry would expose his household or crowd him beyond custom.
  • Ibn 'Abd al-Salam permits drinking and watering animals from a private channel when it does not harm the owner, treating customary permission as stated permission (he hesitated over orphans' and waqf property but al-Mughni says permission appears valid). He doubted 1,000 camels at a small stream. The teacher said the criterion is harm, not numbers.

6. Shared Canals

  • A canal shared by a group: neither the upper nor lower has priority; the water is divided, by force if they dispute and it is narrow. This is done by a beam or a wall with openings across the channel at the same level, equal or unequal in proportion to shares. If the openings are equal but shares differ (or the reverse), each takes his share.
  • If shares are unknown, divide according to land area (the apparent partnership follows ownership); another view says equally (al-Bulqini argued at length). Ibn Hajar distinguishes the mukatab case in al-Rafi'i, where the dispute turns on possession rather than land irrigated.
  • Per al-Rawda: land that can be irrigated from the river and shows a channel from it, with no other source, is presumed to have a share in a dispute. A person may not use his share to irrigate another plot that has no right to it. A new reclaimer is barred if it narrows the earlier owners' supply. Surplus beyond his need need not be handed on to partners; some say returning it to the wadi is forbidden waste but Ibn Hajar doubts that.
  • Fatwa: if a flood ruins a shared upper and lower plot and one owner repairs in a way that reduces the other's customary share, he must restore it; if impossible, matters wait until they reconcile. They may divide by rotation (muhaya'a), e.g. each day in turn. The teacher explained muhaya'a means taking turns by day, month or year. Al-Zarkashi says rotation is required when beam division is impossible, but not when the flow varies greatly.
  • Costs: each owner digs his own channel; repair of the main canal is shared by shares. If one repairs and gets more water, it is not exclusive to him since he was acting voluntarily.
  • The lower-plot owner may dig and plow to repel harm to himself provided it does not harm the upper; the upper may not. If the lower drinks from the upper's water, the upper cannot plant things that reduce the lower's customary share. Al-Ghazali: the lower owner may run his due water through the upper land even if it damages its crops or palms, with no liability, since the upper owner was at fault for planting in the lower's channel.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy)

Third Session, Amman, 8-13 Safar 1407 AH (October 11-16, 1986).

Resolution 14 (2/3) — Zakat on shares in companies

The Academy postponed its resolution to the Fourth Session.

Resolution 15 (3/3) — Investing Zakat funds in income-generating projects without individual ownership

Resolution: in principle it is permissible to invest Zakat funds in projects that end in ownership by those entitled to Zakat, or that are under the authority responsible for collecting and distributing Zakat, provided the recipients' urgent immediate needs are met first and sufficient safeguards against loss exist.

Class discussion:

  • The teacher read it as three conditions: (1) it ends in ownership by the Zakat recipients, not in other charitable or public projects; (2) urgent needs of the recipients are met first; (3) safeguards against loss.
  • A student asked whether "ownership by recipients" refers to the project itself or only its yield. The teacher's reading: the yield is what is distributed to the poor, because if the project itself were owned by individuals it would pass to heirs when one died. A student's alternative reading was that the capital is eventually owned by them.
  • On the Shafi'i position: it is not valid unless the Zakat is handed to the recipients or to the ruler. A workaround: hand it to them with their consent, then they return it to fund a charitable project, though they might refuse next time. The teacher recalled a passage in Asna al-Matalib that a ruler may buy property for them and bar its sale because the yield suffices them, and the class tried but failed to locate it; he said they would find it later.

Resolution 16 (4/3) — Test-tube babies (artificial insemination)

The Academy found seven methods in use.

Prohibited (five):

1. Sperm from the husband and an egg from a woman who is not his wife, with the embryo implanted in the wife.

2. Sperm from a man other than the husband and the wife's egg, implanted in the wife.

3. External fertilization of the spouses' gametes, with the embryo implanted in a volunteer woman.

4. External fertilization of a stranger's sperm with a strange woman's egg, implanted in the wife.

5. External fertilization of the spouses' gametes, implanted in the husband's other wife.

Permissible when needed, with all precautions (two):

6. Sperm of the husband and egg of his wife fertilized externally and implanted in the wife.

7. The husband's sperm injected internally into the wife's vagina or uterus.

Class discussion:

  • Who is the mother? A student noted Shabramallisi's view that the mother is the owner of the egg, because as with a clot that is placed into another woman, the first woman remains the mother. The Qur'anic "their mothers are those who bore them" was raised; the answer was that what was returned to the original woman was part of her own body.
  • Permissibility of method 6 and 7: the teacher said it is for need, because it involves exposing the awra, which is only uncovered for necessity, and here the husband and wife are distressed at not having children.
  • Source of the husband's sperm (method 7): under the school, the semen must be "respected" (muhtaram) at the moment of exit; whether it must be at the moment of entry is not required per Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli. If obtained by unlawful masturbation, it is not respected and the child is not attributed to him. So per the school it should be done by the wife's hand. The Hanbalis consider masturbation permissible on one view, likening it to expelling waste, so as a precaution this route gives a child whose lineage holds on every school.
  • A student asked whether the wife must do it by hand or with a vessel or cloth; the teacher said the essential point is that it be attributable to her, and that it be a lawful route.
  • Palestinian prisoners with long sentences smuggling out sperm for implantation in their wives: the class discussed this as a real situation. The teacher said it may be treated as necessity (the husband intends the need, not the forbidden act), citing Ibn Hajar on emission and its purpose. A student added that if the wife would otherwise seek divorce or khul', that too may count. The teacher: all of this needs further consideration, and it is not far-fetched that it counts as necessity.
