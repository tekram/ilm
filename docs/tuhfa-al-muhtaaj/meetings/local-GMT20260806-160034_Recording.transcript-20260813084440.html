Kitab Ihya al-Mawat: Land Reclamation Rules & Opening the International Fiqh Academy Compendium - August 06, 2026

📅 2026-08-06 🕐 16:00

Overview

This Thursday session opens a new book of Tuhfat al-Muhtaj: Kitab Ihya al-Mawat (Revival of Dead/Unclaimed Land), covering the conditions under which unclaimed land may be owned by reclaiming it, the status of land in Muslim territory, non-Muslim territory, and enemy territory, and the rules of harim (protective buffer zones) around reclaimed property. The class then begins reading the newly revised compendium of the International Islamic Fiqh Academy (Majma al-Fiqh al-Islami al-Dawli, based in Jeddah) — starting with its introductory forewords rather than resolutions — as the Thursday counterpart to the Tuesday readings from the Fiqh Academy of India. A closing Q&A covers natural kohl and the ruling on women's eyes, networked adhan via microphone relay between mosques, and saying "ameen" to a recorded supplication.

1. Definition and Scope of Mawat (Dead/Unclaimed Land)

Background / Question

What legally counts as "dead" or unclaimed land, and what disqualifies land from being claimed through reclamation (ihya)?

Ruling

  • Mawat is land never definitively known to have been inhabited/cultivated under Islam, by a Muslim or a dhimmi. Land whose Islamic-era habitation is merely probable, but not certain, is still treated as mawat going forward if no current sign of habitation or owner is known.
  • Land does not qualify for reclamation if it falls under the harim (buffer rights) of already-developed property, or under general Muslim public rights (riverbanks, seashores, roads, cemeteries, land needed for rites such as 'Arafah/Muzdalifah, or land the Prophet ﷺ specifically protected as a hima).
  • The basis is the authentic hadith "whoever revives land belonging to no one has better right to it," and "whoever revives dead land, it is his" — ownership requires no verbal declaration, since the Prophet ﷺ effectively granted all unclaimed land in a general dispensation (as Tamim al-Dari's grant of land in Syria, before its conquest, illustrates — objecting companions who challenged this were not deemed to have committed unbelief by such objection, only wrongdoing, absent open contempt for the Prophet's ﷺ ruling itself).
  • Reclamation is a recommended (sunna) act with its own reward, per the hadith that whatever insects, birds, or people eat from what one plants or cultivates counts as charity on one's behalf.

Referenced scholars / texts

Al-Subki (on Tamim al-Dari's grant), the hadith collections underlying the consensus (ijma') on reclamation.

2. Ownership Rules by Territory

Background / Question

Do the same reclamation rules apply in Muslim lands, non-Muslim (dhimmi) lands, and enemy territory (dar al-harb)?

Ruling

  • In Muslim lands: any Muslim, even a non-legally-responsible one (e.g. an insane person, in matters not requiring intent), may claim ownership by reclamation; seeking the ruler's permission is recommended, not required. A non-Muslim permanently resident under protection (dhimmi) or a protected transient (musta'man) may not reclaim land in Muslim territory this way (though minor customary allowances like gathering firewood or hunting are tolerated).
  • In lands belonging to dhimmi communities (their own territory under a peace treaty): the dhimmis — even non-legally-responsible ones — may reclaim it, as it is among their territorial rights; a Muslim may do so too, provided the dhimmis there are not people the Muslims must actively repel/defend against, and provided the land itself was not part of a treaty specifically confirming it as theirs (in which case a Muslim may not reclaim it).
  • In enemy territory (dar al-harb): land there may be claimed by reclamation unconditionally, since inhabited property there may be seized outright, so unclaimed land is claimable a fortiori.
  • If a state or authority (e.g. an army) restricts reclamation in some area: the discussion leaned toward the view that reclamation done despite the restriction still results in ownership if it actually occurs, though it may be sinful to have defied the restriction where the restriction serves a genuine public interest (comparable to reclaiming land under an existing claim-in-progress, or tahajjur — sinful to seize, but still effective once done). A government agency (e.g. a ministry of defense) that physically fences and occupies land this way is understood to have reclaimed it on behalf of that specific government body, not the state's treasury generally, similar to how earmarked waqf funds cannot be redirected to other purposes.

Referenced scholars / texts

Al-Kurdi (on the timing of "before being overtaken" for enemy abandonment of land).

3. Previously Inhabited but Abandoned Land

Background / Question

What is the rule for land that shows signs of prior habitation but is now in ruins, or whose owner or era of habitation is unknown?

Ruling

  • If it is known to have been inhabited and its owner (even a dhimmi or enemy national) is known, it remains that owner's property — unless enemy nationals abandoned it before being overtaken by Muslim forces (treated as relinquished, hence reclaimable), as opposed to abandoning it after being overtaken (which counts as spoils of war, fay').
  • If the owner is unknown but the property clearly dates to an Islamic-era settlement (defined as: a city built by Muslims like Baghdad or Basra; a town whose people accepted Islam collectively, like Medina or Yemen; a town conquered by force, like Khaybar; or one whose underlying land title was ceded to Muslims by treaty) — it is treated as lost property, entrusted to the ruler's discretion (preserve it, sell it and hold the proceeds, or borrow against it) until the true owner appears, otherwise it reverts to the public treasury, which may then re-grant it.
  • If the settlement dates to the pre-Islamic (jahili) era, or its era is genuinely unknown, it is treated as ordinary mawat and may be reclaimed, since pre-Islamic ownership carries no protected status — unless it lies in enemy territory under an existing treaty confirming it as theirs.
  • Land conquered by Muslims but later overrun by disbelievers (e.g. Tarsus, or historically al-Andalus/Spain) does not revert to being enemy territory; it remains legally Islamic territory, its mosques remain mosques, and their sale is invalid — pending its eventual return to Muslim rule.

4. Harim (Buffer Zones) of Developed Property

Background / Question

What kind of surrounding land counts as protected harim of an already developed property or settlement, and can such land be owned or sold separately?

Ruling

  • Harim is land needed to fully benefit from the adjoining developed property, even if the property could technically exist without it; it cannot be owned via reclamation (since it belongs to the property's owner) and, per the stronger view, cannot be sold separately from the property itself (though Ibn al-Rif'a held it could be, analogous to anything that merely diminishes another's value if severed).
  • For an entire revived village, its harim includes: the communal gathering place (nadi), horse-tethering areas (for equestrian communities) and camel-resting areas (for camel-herding communities) — with a dispute between Ibn Hajar/al-Khatib (conditioned on the community actually having horses/camels) and al-Ramli (unconditional, since needs can change) — ash/refuse disposal sites, sheep pens, the threshing floor, children's play areas, water drainage channels, and village roads, per longstanding, consistently applied custom. Grazing land also counts as harim if it is customarily used for that purpose and either near the village or, even if farther, genuinely needed by residents (even only seasonally) — though villagers may not bar travelers from grazing their own animals on land that remains fundamentally open/public.
  • Riverbank harim (e.g. along the Nile) covers whatever space is needed for full use of the river, including areas needed for dredging — building there, even a mosque, is invalid and must be demolished, per a reported consensus of all four Sunni schools (though modern practice of temporarily storing goods or building simple shelters there, without intending permanent ownership, is tolerated if it doesn't harm others). Coastal resort developments that block public beach access were raised as a contemporary example of the same principle being violated.

Referenced scholars / texts

Ibn Hajar al-Haytami, al-Khatib al-Shirbini, Shihab al-Din al-Ramli, Ibn al-Rif'a.

5. Opening the International Islamic Fiqh Academy Compendium

Background / Question

The class begins reading the newly revised compendium of resolutions from the International Islamic Fiqh Academy (based in Jeddah, an organ of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation), as the Thursday counterpart to the Tuesday readings from the Fiqh Academy of India.

Notes

  • This session covered only the compendium's introductory forewords — by Dr. Yousef bin Ahmad Al-Othaimeen (OIC Secretary-General) and Sheikh Dr. Saleh bin Abdullah bin Humaid (the Academy's Chairman) — not yet any actual resolutions.
  • The forewords describe the Academy's four-decade history, its collegial method (combining jurists across schools with subject-matter experts), and this new edition compiling 238 resolutions and recommendations from its 2nd through 24th sessions (spanning 1406–1441 AH / 1985–2019 CE), up from 185 in the prior edition — with translations into English and French now complete, and further languages underway.
  • In discussion, the group noted the Academy continues to meet roughly annually and is currently studying (without yet issuing resolutions on) cryptocurrency and lab-grown meat, and has previously discussed donor human-milk banking.

Referenced scholars / texts

Dr. Yousef bin Ahmad Al-Othaimeen, Sheikh Dr. Saleh bin Abdullah bin Humaid, and past Academy leadership commemorated in the foreword (Sheikh Dr. Bakr Abu Zayd, Dr. Muhammad al-Habib Ibn al-Khoja, Dr. Abd al-Salam al-Abbadi, Dr. Ahmad Khalid Babiker, and others).

6. Closing Q&A

Natural Kohl and Women's Eyes

A recurring question about "authentic" kohl marketed for its supposed Prophetic-era benefits: on the mu'tamad (relied-upon) position, a woman's eyes are themselves considered 'awra (to be covered) — she may only uncover them out of necessity, such as to see while walking. Wearing kohl while going out with eyes visible is permissible if she is confident no one will look at her with desire; this parallels the stronger opinion that permits looking at (not merely allowing exposure of) a woman's face when there is no fear of temptation — though the mu'tamad position treats eye exposure as impermissible outright. The same logic (whether it draws undue attention or desire) extends to anklets that make noise, tight or colorful outer garments, and other adornments like blush or eyebrow tinting — all judged by whether they invite attraction, not by any inherent prohibition in the item itself.

Networked Adhan Across Mosques

Some mosques now link a single caller's adhan live via microphone/speaker to multiple mosques ("unifying the call"). While permissible in principle, the stronger view on the Shafi'i school is that this does not fulfill the sunna obligation for each individual mosque/congregation, since each congregation is meant to have its own call — a position also taken by the (Mecca-based) International Islamic Fiqh Academy referenced by name in the discussion, treating this practice as an innovation. The key distinction drawn is between a live relay (still intentional, transmitted call — not yet a recording) and prior recordings (which lack the necessary present-moment intent and are likened to an echo or a parrot's mimicry, which does not trigger the recommended prostration for a recited verse either).

Ameen to a Recorded Supplication

Saying "ameen" upon hearing a recorded supplication (e.g. broadcast or on audio) is unobjectionable and potentially rewarded, since supplication (unlike ritual markers like the call to prayer) is not itself a "symbol of the time" requiring live, present intent — its essence is the heart's attentiveness to the meaning, which can be engaged even through a recording.

Referenced scholars / texts

Muhammad bin Salim bin Hafiz (cited on live broadcast vs. recorded recitation), the (Mecca-based) International Islamic Fiqh Academy (referenced regarding networked adhan).
