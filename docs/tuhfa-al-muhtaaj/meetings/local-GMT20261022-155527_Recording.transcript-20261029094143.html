Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf): the class continues the conditions on the beneficiary: waqf on a dhimmi, why not on an apostate or harbi, why a waqf on oneself is invalid and which stipulations amount to it, the ways to make a benefit return to the endower lawfully, a waqf for a sinful purpose (churches), a waqf restricted to male children, and categories of beneficiary that can be counted (the poor, scholars, mosques). Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf

1. Waqf on a Dhimmi, an Apostate and a Harbi

Ruling:

  • A waqf by a Muslim is valid on a specific dhimmi (one or several), just as charity to him is valid, unless the choice shows intent to sin, such as a waqf on the servant of a church for worship, or on the church's lamps.
  • It is not valid on what the dhimmi cannot own (a Muslim slave, a mushaf, books of fiqh), nor on an apostate or a harbi, because waqf is a continuing charity and neither has continuity (they face the death penalty). The teacher noted that charity such as giving water may still be given to them, but waqf resembles a continuing charity.
  • If the beneficiary later becomes a harbi (the dhimma ends), the waqf becomes "broken in the middle" or "broken at the end": if he is last in the chain it is broken at the end; if named before others ("on so-and-so the dhimmi, then the poor") it is broken in the middle. In both cases, while he lives the income goes to the endower's nearest relative; when he dies it passes to the next named. The teacher compared it to a waqf on a named animal.
  • Why the apostate and harbi differ from a married adulterer: the first two can protect themselves by Islam, whereas a convicted adulterer's execution cannot be averted, yet the waqf on the adulterer is valid since the waqf on the first two is a defiance of Islam's honor. The jurists hesitated about a treaty-protected foreigner and one granted safe passage (al-Ghazzi: like the dhimmi; al-Damiri: like the harbi); al-Subki considered the one whose death penalty is mandatory for banditry like the married adulterer.
  • Textual point: the teacher connected this to the usurped-land waqf from the previous class: Ibn Hajar says a waqf on a building on usurped land is valid in principle (the object is present, removal is an external matter), while al-Ramli, al-Khatib and the Nihaya/Mughni say it is not valid because it lacks continuity.

2. Waqf on Oneself

Ruling: invalid on the sounder view, since one cannot own one's own property or its benefits; it is acquiring what is already acquired, and waqf by the endower differs from ownership only in name.

  • This includes stipulating that his debt be paid from the waqf or that he benefit from it. Stipulating that he drink from a waqf well, read from a waqf book, cook in a waqf pot, and so on: a commentator said this is valid, but Ibn Hajar says it is not, likely because of 'Uthman's statement about Bi'r Ruma ("my bucket is like the buckets of Muslims"), which was a report not a condition. The teacher added that a man in their town endowed a mosque with a stipulation that he and his descendants have priority at the ablution place, and no one voided it, but it may make a difference.
  • Exceptions: stipulating that a sacrifice or hajj be done for him from it is valid, since only reward returns to him. Stipulating that he benefit by praying in a mosque he endowed is not valid, as it brings him an obvious worldly benefit.
  • Abu Zur'a's fatwa: a man endowed a building and a garden stipulating that the income pay first for their upkeep and the remainder go to him, then to his children: valid. The surplus is kept during his life in case upkeep needs it, and what remains at his death goes to his children. It is not void for what he assigned himself, because it is unknown, unlike a waqf "on Zayd and himself" (valid for Zayd's half), and not like a waqf broken in the middle, since the amounts are not a second tier.
  • If he later becomes poor: a waqf on the poor, then he becomes poor (or was poor at the time), he may take from it (al-Kafi, al-Subki). He may stipulate himself as administrator (nazir), even for a fee, if it does not exceed the customary fee; more than that voids the waqf if it is for himself (it is fine if the larger fee is for someone else).
  • Lawful devices (hiyal): (1) endow it on his father's descendants or on a tribe and state qualities found only in himself, such as "the most learned of Banu Rif'a" (Ibn Rif'a did this himself); al-Isnawi, al-Ghazali and al-Khawarizmi disagree because it ends in himself, but the teacher said the first is relied upon, as it takes the wording, not the outcome. (2) Lease the property for a long term and then endow it, so the rent is his; and then he may rent it back from the lessee for safety. The teacher compared it to selling a property that has been rented out: the rent belongs to the seller.
  • Ibn al-Salah's fatwa: a Hanafi judge ruling that a waqf on oneself is valid does not stop a Shafi'i from selling it inwardly (as the judge's ruling covers only the outward). Others reject this since the sounder view in al-Rawda is that a judge's ruling in a matter of disagreement takes effect inwardly and ends the dispute. The teacher noted that a ruling must come from a judge on a case (claim and answer), not an officially issued rule or opinion (like a fatwa), which does not end disagreement.

3. A Waqf for a Sinful Purpose

  • Invalid for a Muslim (or any) to endow for building or repairing churches used for worship, their lamps, or copying the Torah, since it is aiding sin. What a dhimmi does among themselves is not annulled unless they bring it to us and their judge has ruled. A church used as a hostel for passers-by, or for specific people to live in, is valid, since it is not for worship.
  • Discussion on the wall: the teacher recalled an earlier discussion about being hired to build a wall around a church used for worship, and said it seemed not a sin because it is protection, not the worship itself; Shaykh Muhammad al-Khatib disagreed and counted it as aiding sin. Test: Ibn Hajar (in Kitab al-Nikah) says aiding sin means the sin depends on the helper's participation, like playing chess with someone who believes it forbidden, since chess needs a partner. A wall is not required for worship to occur.
  • Is building a church by a Muslim kufr? The sounder view is that it is a sin, not kufr. Al-Shawbari reports it is kufr as it magnifies another religion; the gloss of 'Abd al-Hamid says the nearer view is al-Shawbari's, unless accompanied by an evident necessity. The teacher stressed how serious it is to declare a Muslim a kafir.
  • Cases raised: (a) a baker asked in America to make a cake for a same-sex wedding: the cake is separable from the contract, so it is not aiding sin; but if he writes a congratulatory inscription or draws the couple, it becomes endorsement. (b) Selling a car, gold or grapes to someone likely to resell in a forbidden way (e.g. gold sold on instalment): the teacher hesitated and asked the students. Conclusions: if there is a standing arrangement (e.g. returning what is unsold) it is more like aiding; a one-off sale with no tie, where the buyer might also sell lawfully, is separated; a seller of grapes to a wine factory is different since the use is almost exclusively for wine. A student added online stores in non-Muslim countries that sell gold without handover; the teacher left it for further discussion.

4. Waqf Restricted to Male Children

Many scholars endow on their sons only (e.g. a small house in Hud used a few days a year) intending to deprive females. Some have given fatwas that it is void; Ibn Hajar says it is plainly valid: (1) deprivation is not conceded, since the scholars agree that favoring some children with all or part of one's property is not forbidden even without excuse; (2) even if forbidden, the sin is external to the act of waqf, like buying grapes to make wine.

5. Categories That Can Be Counted

A waqf on a category of piety that can be counted is valid: the poor (meaning here the poor of Zakat, except that someone whose earnings suffice but has no property can be given from a waqf on the poor), scholars (of tafsir, hadith, fiqh and the like), mosques, schools, the Ka'ba, bridges, and shrouding the dead (for those with no estate or supporter), by the general evidence for waqf; it is not objectionable that the beneficiary is an inanimate thing since the benefit returns to Muslims, and scholars may die out but the poor do not, since permanence is in each thing according to its nature.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy)

Resolution 23 (11/3) — Inquiries of the International Institute of Islamic Thought, Washington (continued)

Question 4: a woman converts while her husband stays on his religion; they have children who may be lost, she hopes he will convert, or he treats her well and she fears not finding a Muslim husband. Answer: upon her conversion with his refusal, the marriage is annulled and it is not lawful for him to be intimate with her; she waits out the 'idda, and if he converts during it she returns to him by the earlier contract; if the 'idda ends, the bond is cut, and if he converts later they need a new contract. His good treatment does not make continuing the marriage lawful. The teacher noted this is by consensus, and as in the preceding resolution, the hope of his conversion changes nothing.

  • A student asked about someone who uttered a word of kufr and then prays. The teacher: he must renew the testimony of faith and repent explicitly, saying "I retract what I said," since a verbal sin needs verbal retraction; praying alone doesn't suffice. He mentioned a widely recited creed in Hadramawt mosques (after the evening supplications of Imam al-Haddad's ratib) that includes "if I said anything contrary to consensus I withdraw it," as a way of renewing it. If a long time passes without repentance, the marriage is annulled by passing of the 'idda, and there is no inheritance between them. These should be reminded in letters, lectures, and Friday sermons.

Question 5: burying Muslims in non-Muslim cemeteries (where burial elsewhere is not allowed). Answer: permitted by necessity; but if Muslims can be placed in a separate section it is better, since those cemeteries are places of the curse of the unbelievers. The teacher added that if a Muslim cemetery exists even two or four hours away and the body would not spoil, transport is obligatory (the place of non-Muslim burial is a place of punishment), particularly if the deceased left an estate or a waqf for burial exists. He suggested Muslims in non-Muslim countries pool shares to buy a cemetery, one of the most important charitable works there.

Question 6: selling a mosque abandoned by Muslims who moved, or taken over by others, and replacing it. Answer given in the resolution: permitted to sell a mosque from which benefit has ceased, to buy another to be a mosque. The teacher flatly rejected this as incorrect: a mosque is never sold; it stays a mosque until the Day of Judgment, and the supposed benefit does not outweigh its sanctity. He noted that mosques in Andalusia are still mosques and may one day return to Muslims, which would be impossible if sold. He attributed the contrary position to Ibn Taymiyya as one of his slips. A student read a fatwa from the Egyptian Dar al-Ifta (Shaykh Hasan Ma'mun, c. 1969) giving the Hanafi positions: Muhammad b. al-Hasan (it returns to the endower or heirs; if unknown it may be sold and the price spent on another mosque) versus Abu Yusuf (it is a mosque forever; the majority of Hanafis follow him, Ibn al-Humam preferred it; though some report he permits transferring the debris and furnishings or selling them with the judge's permission). The teacher found it odd that Muhammad would say this about a mosque and asked the student to check Muhammad b. al-Hasan's own words.

  • A mosque in Spain with a church inside: a student asked whether one may enter. The teacher: if there are images or sinful rites, entering is forbidden by the sin present; if it is only a church without images, one may enter and needs no permission of the non-Muslims, since it remains a mosque; a menstruating woman may not stay in it in any case. Parts of the building far from the church are not affected by the sin where prayer still takes place.

Question on women living alone or with non-Muslim women abroad for work or study. The resolution's answer says it is not permitted to reside alone in a foreign country. The teacher said that is incorrect on the Shafi'i view: what is forbidden to her is traveling without a mahram or group, not residing; if she is safe, even alone, it is fine. It becomes forbidden only where there is real danger of being preyed upon.

Question 9: the limits of a woman's covering at work/study (many say they can only cover everything but face and hands, and employers forbid covering the head). Answer: the majority of scholars hold covering all but face and hands; if fitna is feared these must be covered too. The teacher said he recalled a Hanafi allowance (attributed to Muhammad or Abu Yusuf) for the exposed part of the forearm near the hand at work, a thing many working women find hard to avoid, but he could not confirm the source.

Question 11: Muslim students working in restaurants that serve alcohol or pork. Answer: if no lawful work is available, work in non-Muslim restaurants is permitted provided he does not himself serve, carry, make or trade alcohol, and likewise for pork. The teacher added: if the part where he works is separate from where alcohol is served, it is permitted even when not forced (the custom treats him neither as an aider nor as approving, like eating at a table far from where pork is served). If he does not handle alcohol but it is present, it is permitted even if he found other work, so long as he would be significantly affected by losing this one (e.g. needing a car for a real need). Serving or carrying alcohol is only permitted when necessary to survive.

Question 12: medicines containing alcohol (cough and cold syrups; about 95% of such medicines contain some). Answer: permitted for the patient if no alcohol-free equivalent is available and a trustworthy doctor prescribes it. The teacher explained the distinction: a liquid that has started to ferment and contains a trace of alcohol without reaching intoxication (e.g. fermenting grape juice or yogurt) is not forbidden and not impure, whereas adding even a drop of real alcohol to a liquid makes it impure. A student cited a fatwa of the Syrian mufti Shaykh Usama al-Rifa'i who set a threshold by the hadith "what intoxicates in quantity, a handful of it is forbidden," at about 5 parts in 10,000 or less; the teacher said that fits with his point and that the test is whether drinking all of it would intoxicate.
