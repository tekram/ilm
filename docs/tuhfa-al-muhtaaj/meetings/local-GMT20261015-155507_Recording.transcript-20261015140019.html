Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf): the class continues the conditions on the beneficiary of a waqf (mawquf 'alayh): that it be a specific party able to own at once, why a waqf on someone or something not yet existing is invalid, the cases that are valid by following an existing beneficiary, and who or what cannot be a beneficiary (a fetus, a slave for himself, an animal, wild animals). Book 2 (Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali, International Islamic Fiqh Academy, Third Session, Amman, October 1986): Resolution 22 (10/3) on muqarada bonds and development/investment bonds (referred for further study), and Resolution 23 (11/3) on the inquiries of the International Institute of Islamic Thought in Washington, with the class discussion on a Muslim woman marrying a non-Muslim and on a woman who converts while her husband stays on his religion.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf

1. Conditions on the Beneficiary (Mawquf 'Alayh)

Background: the text says a waqf may be on a specific beneficiary (one or a group) or on a category (covered later). The teacher explained "specific" includes one person or several (e.g. Zayd, 'Ubayd and Hind), and cited a note that the dual and plural are treated alike ("two make a group" as in the rules of congregation).

Ruling (conditions):

  • No sin in the beneficiary: a waqf on a church for worship, or to build one, is invalid.
  • Specified: the waqf object must be specific.
  • Able to own immediately: the beneficiary must exist and be eligible to own at the time, since waqf is a transfer of benefit. Textual note: the sentence should read "by being existent, eligible to own"; the teacher corrected the printed wording.
  • Therefore invalid: a waqf on a mosque that will be built, on one's child when he has none, on "the poor among my children" when none is poor, or on feeding the poor from its income at one's grave or one's father's grave (the latter said of a father still alive, which makes it a break at the start; the teacher clarified a missing "and" in the text and the gloss that this applies "even if he is alive").
  • Government grants: the teacher discussed the common case where the state allots land for a mosque. If the state declares it a waqf as the representative, it may be valid; if it merely grants the plot for use, it is not a waqf on a future mosque.
  • Ibn al-Salah's fatwa: a waqf "on whoever reads over my grave after my death" is void if he dies and his grave is not known; so it would be valid if the grave is known. Ibn Hajar's explanation, with some hesitation ("it seems the difference is"): reading at the grave is intended by the Shari'a, so valid on condition of knowing the grave, unlike feeding at the grave. A later section revisits recitation at the grave. The teacher added that the benefit of reciting at a grave, even without gifting the reward, is the descent of tranquility, and the dead are like the living in benefiting from it.

2. When a Waqf on Non-Existent Persons Is Valid

  • If he has a child, or a poor one among them, the waqf is valid and the income goes to the one who comes into existence or becomes poor (in the first and second cases respectively), since a waqf is valid for the non-existent as a follow-on to the existent. "I endow it on my child, then my grandchild" when he has no grandchild is valid. Also "on such a mosque and any mosque built in that locality."
  • Extended discussion: a vow or bequest "to my children and whoever will be born to me": the teacher singled this out as important and easily forgotten (it is in Bughyat al-Mustarshidin, and in Fatwas of Ba Fadl and Muhammad b. Salim b. Hafiz). Opinions:

1. Valid in full, with the not-yet-existing included as followers of the existing (understood from al-Tuhfa on bequests; the vow has the bequest's ruling), held by Ibn Hajar, followed by al-Ramli and the author of al-Muqaddima al-Hadramiyya.

2. Invalid in full: 'Abd Allah b. 'Umar Ba Makhrama (the grandfather), al-Nashiri, Abu Zur'a, and a view of 'Abd Allah b. Ahmad Ba Makhrama in one report.

3. Suspended (tawaqquf): Ba Makhrama (the grandson, author of the 'Adan and Hajran fatwas): if children are later born, validity is clear for the existing ones' share; otherwise the vow and bequest are void, and meanwhile the heirs may deal with the item.

4. Valid for half: Ibn Ju'man and Muhammad b. 'Afif, which Shaykh Muhammad Ba Sudan says should be relied upon.

The teacher noted the scholars of his teachers' circle tend to the first, Ibn Hajar's, and clarified that one of his citations was a form of the vow written by Shaykh Fadl, not a fatwa of his.

  • A student corrected the teacher: the passage in the text is not the case of children not yet born but "on my child (who does not exist)" with the clause "if he has a child". The teacher accepted this.

3. Fetus and Others Who Cannot Be Beneficiaries

  • A waqf on the dead or on a fetus is invalid, because waqf is an immediate transfer of control (unlike a bequest). A fetus is also not included in a waqf "on my children" but is held in reserve: if born alive, and the wording did not name or count the existing children, he enters as a follower. The teacher explained that Ibn Qasim and Shabramallisi differ on whether "holding" means suspending the ruling of inclusion; the teacher agreed the better reading is that the ruling of inclusion is suspended, and that wording like "descendants" includes the fetus (al-'Ubab, al-Rawd).
  • Slave: a waqf on a slave for himself (even a mudabbar or umm walad) is invalid since he cannot own. If the waqf is on a pious purpose such as the service of a mosque or ribat, it is valid; likewise on the free portion of a part-free person, and on a mukatab validly contracted, since he owns. The teacher: a waqf on one's own slave is effectively for oneself. If he says "on the slave" without specifying, it is understood as a waqf on his master, like a gift or bequest to him. For a part-free person, the rule depends on whether there is a rotation schedule.
  • Animals: an unqualified waqf on a privately owned animal is void (an animal cannot own), though some say it is a waqf on its owner like the slave; the difference is that a slave can own. If the waqf is on its fodder, or on it with the owner as the intended beneficiary, it is valid. Also valid for animals dedicated in the way of Allah at a frontier (jihad horses, mules, camels). Al-Mutawalli: not valid on wild beasts and free birds. Al-Rafi'i and al-Nawawi transmit it but it is disputed; the objection that the condition is "no sin" is answered that no one intends a waqf for these by custom. By contrast, the doves of Makkah are customarily intended, so a waqf on them is valid (the teacher recalled land in Tarim endowed on the Makkah doves). A specific wild animal (this particular cat or the bird on that palm) is invalid for certain, though some dispute it.
  • A contemporary question: the teacher recalled a question from a woman in Britain with unowned lost money, asking whether it may fund shelters for stray cats that would otherwise die. The teacher and two other jurists on the fatwa council answered yes: out of respect for life, the money is spent on public interests and the rich would be obliged to spend on it anyway.
  • Waqf on the Magian / harbi: the text notes that a waqf on a harbi non-Muslim appears valid because he can own, but later text shows the condition is the continued existence of the beneficiary; Kurdi: the reference is to the beneficiary's continuity, which resolves the apparent objection. (The class also discussed a related point on endowing a usurped property.)

Textual points

The class discussed the vocalization of "al-mahall" versus "al-hilla" (the camp or encampment) and a printing discrepancy in the text.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy)

Third Session, Amman, 8-13 Safar 1407 AH (October 11-16, 1986).

Resolution 22 (10/3) — Muqarada bonds and development/investment bonds

The Academy asked its General Secretariat to commission studies on the topic, for decision in the Fourth Session. The teacher only explained the terms: muqarada bonds (as understood from Jordanian fatwa-house materials) are bonds where the holder shares profit and loss like muqarada (qirad), versus development bonds, issued by the state to fund a project of local benefit (e.g. a dairy factory). A student clarified that the state sells the bonds to raise money and then invests it; "investment" covers more forms than muqarada.

Resolution 23 (11/3) — Inquiries of the International Institute of Islamic Thought, Washington

The Academy asked its Secretariat to relay the approved answers.

Question 3: marriage of a Muslim woman to a non-Muslim, especially in hope of his converting (many Muslim women claiming they lack suitable Muslim men): Answer: forbidden by the Qur'an, sunna and consensus; if it occurs it is void and has none of the legal effects of marriage; children born of it are not legitimate; and hoping the husband will convert does not change the ruling.

Class discussion:

  • A student asked about "illegitimate children" if the woman was ignorant (e.g. far from scholars in a non-Muslim country) and whether the husband's ignorance can make them legitimate, mentioning the three kinds of doubt (shubha) in the husband, in the act, and in the place. The teacher: the criterion is the man's intent; if he did not intend zina and believed it was a valid marriage, the children are attributed to him and are legitimate in lineage, since the Shari'a is keen to preserve lineage. If he knew it was forbidden by Islamic law but followed only the court, they are not.
  • A student asked about civil marriage in court: the teacher said it is not valid by itself; if the couple both believe it is valid, the child is attributed. If the man understands it as contradicting Shari'a and proceeds anyway, the children are illegitimate. Effects of the invalid marriage are limited: she owes a waiting period ('idda) and the child is attributed to him; they must be separated and she must not give herself to him.
  • A Mauritanian source: a student quoted 'Abd Allah b. Bayyah's Sina'at al-Fatwa as mentioning an irregular view. The teacher: it needs verification as to the source and the exact point; mention of an anomalous opinion does not break consensus, and a book on the craft of fatwa is not a specialized fiqh text. The student added that Shaykh Bin Bayyah said in a workshop that Muslims should avoid such views, though a person in such a situation may take them. The teacher said he would check it in the coming class.
  • Distinction: the consensus is on a Muslim woman marrying a non-Muslim at the outset; a woman who converts while married to a non-Muslim is a different question. The Qur'an (60:10, "they are not lawful for them, nor are they lawful for them") and the Prophet's words to Zaynab are explicit.

A Woman Converts While Her Husband Stays on His Religion

(Read from a second resolution on this subject; the transcript does not clearly name the issuing body.)

1. If both convert together, and she is not forbidden to him (e.g. by lineage or suckling), they remain married.

2. If the husband alone converts and the wife is of the People of the Book, they remain married.

3. If the wife converts and the husband stays: if before consummation, separation is immediate; if after consummation and he converts before her 'idda ends, they remain married; if 'idda ends she may wait for his conversion however long, and if he converts they remain married without a new contract. If she chooses to marry someone else after 'idda she must seek an annulment through the court.

4. Under the four schools she may not stay with him after 'idda ends or let him have her; some scholars allow her to stay with full marital rights if it does not harm her religion and she hopes he will convert, so as not to deter women from Islam, relying on a report from 'Umar about a woman in al-Hira, and on 'Ali's view that the Christian husband is more entitled to her.

  • The student's quotation from the same discussion mentioned the hadith of Zaynab being returned to Abu al-'As by the first marriage; Ibn 'Abd al-Barr said even if sound, it is abrogated or abandoned by all and that al-Nakha'i's view was isolated, followed only by some Zahiris. The teacher: the Zahiris' disagreement does not count for consensus, and the Prophet likely returned her without a new contract because her 'idda had not ended. He also noted other isolated views (e.g. lending slave girls for intercourse; Ibn al-Musayyib on a triple-divorced woman returning with a marriage to another man without consummation) that scholars condemned strongly.
  • The teacher stressed that the ruling is "illegitimate" in the sense of lineage only when the man knew, otherwise it is a case of shubha with lineage attributed, and that the Shari'a rules by outward appearance.

Closing exchange: Is a secular Muslim a kafir?

A student asked whether Muslims who believe in secularism (religion private, the rest worldly) are excused. The teacher: no takfir unless they deny what is consensus and known by necessity from the religion; if they hold a doubt (such as thinking inheritance rules or banking interest do not suit the times, or interest in banks is not riba), they are not declared kafir, though they may be disciplined (ta'zir). Declaring a ruling unjust or mocking it would be kufr. The aim is to avoid takfir of a Muslim.
