Ijāra for Nursing & Childcare, Physician Liability, Landlord Duties & Talfīq Q&A — June 25, 2026

📅 2026-06-25 🕐 16:00

Overview

This Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj session continues the chapter on ijāra (hire/lease), moving from the hire of persons for intangible services into the specific rulings on nursing contracts (ijārat al-riḍāʿ), the distinction between nursing (riḍāʿ) and large childcare (ḥaḍāna kubrā), what materials and supplies the hired party must provide, liability of the hired physician (al-ṭabīb al-ajīr), and landlord vs. tenant obligations. The session closes with a Q&A on lending to a riba debtor, combining the rulings of two madhhabs (talfīq) across separate acts, and whether a woman switching madhhabs mid-menstrual cycle owes makeup prayers.

1. Ijāra for Nursing and Childcare

Validity and Conditions

A woman — whether free or enslaved, Muslim or non-Muslim — may validly be hired for nursing (riḍāʿ) and childcare if she is trustworthy (amīna). The contract must specify three things: (1) duration (mudda), (2) location (makān), and (3) identification of the child (taʿyīn al-mursaʿ) — all three are required for the contract to be valid.

Small vs. Large Ḥaḍāna

Small ḥaḍāna (ḥaḍāna ṣughrā): Holding the infant for nursing — this is subsumed within the nursing contract and need not be contracted separately.

Large ḥaḍāna (ḥaḍāna kubrā): Comprehensive childcare — washing (ghasl), oiling (tadhin), swaddling (taqmīṭ), and attending to the infant's bodily needs. This is a separate and entirely distinct contract from nursing; the hire of a wet nurse does not include large ḥaḍāna.

Obligations of the Wet Nurse

The wet nurse must eat foods that increase milk production and avoid foods or conduct that would harm the milk. Intercourse with her husband is suspended (mahrūm) during the nursing period if it would harm the infant, since the welfare of the infant is the objective of the contract. If the milk dries up mid-contract: only the nursing portion (jihat al-riḍāʿ) of the contract fails; the childcare component survives independently.

2. Mandatory Materials in Hire Contracts (Al-Āla fī ʿAqd al-Ijāra)

The Stronger Opinion

The stronger view: auxiliary materials are not owed by the hired party (al-āla laysat ʿalā al-ajīr) — ink is not owed by the scribe, thread is not owed by the tailor, kohl is not owed by the eye-doctor, and so on. The hired party's obligation is the labor (ʿamal), not the inputs.

The Role of Custom (ʿUrf)

Al-Rāfiʿī clarified: custom (ʿurf) governs this question. In any given trade and locale, if it is customary for the hired party to supply the materials, this custom is incorporated into the contract. If custom is disputed or ambiguous, the contract must explicitly specify who provides the materials; failure to do so renders the contract void for gharar (uncertainty regarding what is owed). This applies to dhimma hire (contracting for an outcome): in ʿayn hire (contracting for a specific person's time), only the labor is owed regardless.

3. Physician Liability (Ḍamān al-Ṭabīb)

The Hired Physician (Ajīr Mushtarak or Khāṣṣ)

A skilled physician (ṭabīb māhir, even if self-taught) who treats a patient in accordance with his skill owes no liability for failure to cure — because the contracted benefit (manfaʿa) in a valid medical hire is the treatment itself, not the cure. The cure cannot be guaranteed; the deliverable is proper medical care. The physician earns the agreed wage even if the patient does not recover.

Condition as a consideration in the contract: If cure (shifāʾ) is explicitly stipulated as a condition, the contract is void (fāsid), because it makes the wage contingent on an outcome outside the physician's control.

Alternative: Jiʿāla on recovery. A jiʿāla (reward contract: "I will pay you X if I recover") is valid — payment is made only upon recovery, and no gharar problem arises because the jiʿāla tolerates uncertainty.

Unskilled physician: An unskilled practitioner who causes harm bears full liability (ḍamān), earns no wage, and must return the cost of any medications that he provided.

4. Landlord vs. Tenant Obligations

Landlord's Duties

  • Deliver the key (taslīm al-miftāḥ) and enable access.
  • Undertake major repairs (tamkīn al-intifāʿ / repairs that enable the contracted use).
  • Clear snow from the outer roof (saqf khārij / jamlun) if relevant.
  • Reclaim the property from usurpers; defend against fire or plundering.
  • Deliver with the cesspit (bāliʿa) empty.
  • Note: the landlord cannot be compelled to repair, but if he refuses to repair a defect that prevents use, the tenant acquires a right of cancellation (khiyār).

Tenant's Duties

  • Clear snow from the courtyard (fināʾ) and the usable roof (saqf yustaʿmal).
  • Remove ordinary refuse (qumāma ʿādiyya).
  • Clean the cesspit during the period of occupancy.
  • Wind-blown soil: neither party is responsible.

5. Q&A

Lending to a Riba Debtor

Question: Is it permissible to lend money to a person who is not repentant and intends to open another interest-bearing bank account with the borrowed money?

Ruling: Not permissible. Lending to someone who will use the loan to facilitate a clear and ongoing sin (maʿṣiya mustamirra) constitutes assistance in that sin (iʿāna ʿalā al-maʿṣiya), which is itself prohibited.

Talfīq Across Separate Acts: Witr as One Rakʿa After Shortened ʿIshāʾ

Question (Tashfeen Ekram): A traveler performs ʿIshāʾ shortened to two rakʿas following the Ḥanafī madhhab (relying on a Ḥanafī-permissive view of his travel situation), then performs witr as a single rakʿa following the Shāfiʿī madhhab (for which one rakʿa suffices). Is this talfīq (combining two madhhabs) permissible?

Discussion: This appears permissible, because the two acts are separate — each with its own beginning, intention, and completion. The talfīq that is problematic is combining the two madhhabs in a single act such that the act would be invalid under both (e.g., praying with a Ḥanafī wuḍūʾ to a Shāfiʿī prayer where the wuḍūʾ would be invalid under the Shāfiʿī rules). Here ʿIshāʾ was completed fully under Ḥanafī rules and witr was completed fully under Shāfiʿī rules — analogous to performing wuḍūʾ under the Ḥanafī school then praying under the Shāfiʿī school.

Woman Switching Madhhabs Mid-Cycle for Menstrual Rulings

Question: A woman follows the Shāfiʿī madhhab and therefore treats days 1–10 as menstruation (ḥayḍ). On day 11, she switches to the Ḥanafī madhhab, under which day 11 would be considered istiḥāḍa (irregular bleeding, not menstruation). Does she owe makeup prayers (qaḍāʾ) for the days she omitted prayers while on the Shāfiʿī ruling?

Ruling: No makeup prayers are owed for the days she was following the Shāfiʿī madhhab — she was legitimately following a recognized legal opinion at the time. There is no conflict between the two applications: she validly omitted prayers for days 1–10 under the Shāfiʿī school, and she validly resumes prayers from day 11 under the Ḥanafī school. The two rulings operate on non-overlapping periods.
