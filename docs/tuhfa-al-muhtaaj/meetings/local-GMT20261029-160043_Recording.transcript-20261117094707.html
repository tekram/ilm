Kitab al-Waqf: Beneficiary Categories & Waqf by Deed or Act (Tuhfa) + Fiqh Academy: Mosque Weddings, Churches & Handshakes - October 29, 2026

📅 2026-10-29 🕐 16:00

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf): the class continues the beneficiary conditions (categories that can be counted, waqf on everyone, on the wealthy, on dhimmis and sinners), then the wording of waqf (when deeds or acts stand in for words): building in the form of a mosque, the moment building materials leave ownership, money collected to build a zawiya, a road made a waqf by use, mutes and writers. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali, resolution 23 (11/3), answers to the Washington institute): gelatin and yeast from pigs, holding weddings in mosques (and singing and the daff there), student "temporary" marriage, eyebrows and kohl, handshaking, and renting churches for prayer. Before the lesson there was also a general conversation about a news clip concerning events in Yemen, and a short Q&A on pausing in al-Fatiha closes the session.

Pre-Class Conversation (brief)

A student asked about a circulating clip criticizing a political-religious group in Yemen and listing alleged abuses. The teacher's advice, in summary: reliable news sources and full context are lacking, so avoid taking sides or being drawn into either camp; pray in general terms for the peace of Yemen without naming a wrongdoer; and not trust claims from partisan sources (he cited the saying "between truth and lies are four fingers": what you saw yourself versus what you heard). On the religious points raised: reviling the Companions by itself does not make it lawful to fight a group (unlike the Khawarij whose armed rebellion harms others); a Shi'i who reviles the two Shaykhs may still have testimony accepted (as in the gloss of 'Abd al-Hamid), unlike a Sunni who does so, because the former was raised on it; and reviling 'A'isha in other than what Allah cleared her of does not, on the apparent texts, amount to kufr, though the teacher personally finds it very serious and noted the Qur'anic verse about the pure for the pure. He also said he knew of nothing to suggest the group has left its Zaydi doctrine.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf

1. Beneficiary Categories That Can Be Counted

  • A waqf on a pious category that can be counted: the poor (meaning the poor of Zakat, except that here someone whose earnings suffice but who owns nothing may receive, unlike Zakat); scholars (those of the Shari'a sciences, as in a bequest: tafsir, hadith, fiqh), who receive even if wealthy, as an honor to knowledge; mosques, schools, the Ka'ba, bridges/water channels (qanatir); and shrouding the dead, limited to someone with no estate and no one obliged to pay (washing, shrouding, carrying, prayer, burial).
  • It is valid by the generality of the evidence for waqf; it does not matter that the beneficiary is an inanimate thing since the benefit returns to Muslims, nor that scholars may die out while the poor do not, since permanence is relative to each thing.
  • Excluded: a waqf on all people, since they cannot be counted. Al-Mawardi and al-Rawyani say it is void; al-Subki disputed. Al-Ramli (following his father) holds it valid; Ibn Hajar follows al-Mawardi, so this is a point where they differ. The teacher noted the text's wording "all people" and left open whether "all Muslims" is the same.

2. A Category Where Piety Is Not Evident

  • A waqf on the wealthy is valid on the sounder view, as charity to the wealthy is permitted (even recommended if the gift gladdens them; a trivial sum that does not move them is only permitted). The only condition is no sin in the category. The text's example "a category where piety is not apparent" was challenged because charity to the rich is pious; the answer is that it is "not apparent," which differs from "absent."
  • Dhimmis and the immoral: the two Shaykhs (al-Rafi'i and al-Nawawi) held waqf on a dhimmi and on the immoral invalid, as aiding sin, but others disputed them in both transmission and reasoning. Al-Mawardi explicitly validated waqf on Jews and Christians. The teacher said the relied-upon view (and al-Ramli's) is that it is valid on dhimmis and the immoral, against the two Shaykhs, and that this matches the earlier discussion where the two are not always adopted.
  • Birds: as before, a waqf on birds is valid only where custom intends the beneficiary (as with the Makkah doves).
  • Wealthy relatives: a waqf on "the wealthy among my relatives" is valid without dispute, since charity to relatives is obedience. "Wealthy" here means one for whom Zakat is not permitted (al-Zubayri; al-Nihaya has al-Ziyadi in his place); al-Adhra'i's suggestion of using custom is doubted.
  • Tombs: a waqf for building or ornamenting graves is invalid, with an exception suggested for the tombs of prophets, scholars and the righteous, whose visiting is sought (cf. "He made me blessed wherever I am").
  • A waqf on levies the sultan imposes on a town is valid. A waqf of a cow on a ribat, "to drink its milk" or "to sell its offspring" for its needs: al-Qaffal said it must be specified, but the relied-upon view is that it is valid even without specifying.

3. Wording of Waqf and Acts Standing in for Words

  • The speaker must use the wording of waqf; a mute person by gesture, a writer by writing with intent. A written deed alone is not relied upon (fatwa of Shaykh Salim: a document saying "endowed on so-and-so" is not adopted without proof of intent). The mu'ata (exchange by act, as in sales) does not apply in waqf.
  • A building in the form of a mosque or graveyard with permission to pray or bury does not leave his ownership; but if he permits i'tikaf there it becomes a mosque, since i'tikaf is only valid in a mosque.
  • Building a mosque on barren land: the intention suffices, since the land itself does not leave his ownership in fact or by assumption so no strong word is needed.
  • Materials: ownership of building materials ends when they are set in their place, not before, unless he says "this is for the mosque" (al-Mawardi, with al-Fariqi's weak dissent). Objections were raised (al-Qamuli, al-Bulqini: it requires acceptance by the administrator), answered that for a place that is not yet a mosque there is no administrator, so necessity treats it as the mosque's.
  • If he says to the mosque's caretaker "mold bricks from my land for the mosque" and the caretaker builds with them, the mosque's ruling applies and the owner may not withdraw them. Al-Isnawi extended this (from al-Rafi'i) to schools and ribats; al-Bulqini to wells dug for travelers and a plot revived as a cemetery.
  • Money collected from people to build a zawiya or ribat becomes a waqf by the act of building (Shaykh Abu Muhammad). Some objected that he built on a weak view; but al-Ramli accepted it, and 'Umdat al-Mufti lists it among cases of waqf without wording, so the teacher took it as relied upon. The teacher noted that Ibn Hajar also uses the phrase "someone objected" to introduce a view he does not reject.
  • A road becomes a waqf by use (the sons of Imam al-Haramayn's report); but a plot that was his own needs wording, and in barren land the intention to make it a road plus passing through it, even once. Ibn Hajar adds that the intention is of waqf as a road.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy)

Resolution 23 (11/3) — Inquiries of the International Institute of Islamic Thought, Washington (continued)

(Question 15 is not in this recording.)

Question 13: yeast and gelatin containing traces of pig-derived elements. Answer: not lawful for Muslims to use pig-derived yeast and gelatin in foods, and they must use the plant-based or properly slaughtered animal sources. The teacher added it applies even if no alternative is available, since there is no true necessity.

Question 14: weddings held in mosques, with singing, in places lacking other space. Answer: not permitted if men and women mix, women dress immodestly, and there is dancing and singing. The teacher clarified:

  • The text's ban is where there is a prohibited element such as mixing in a way that disrespects the mosque; women alone in a mosque who dress up is not forbidden by the mosque's sanctity (though forbidden for them in other ways). He noted the case of non-Muslim women at al-Azhar being asked to cover their heads.
  • Dancing is not an issue unless it disrespects the mosque (the Abyssinians played in the mosque before the Prophet). Singing as verse is not forbidden; the forbidden thing is music. A student quoted his grandfather's fatwa setting conditions for songs (permissible if it replaces sensual songs, avoids effeminacy and imitation of the immoral, and is free of instruments such as strings, flutes and the "kuba"/darbuka, which was a stated example).
  • The marriage contract in the mosque is recommended; holding gatherings or lessons there is not forbidden unless disturbing (disliked if a little, forbidden if much, per the school; al-Nawawi differs in some points).
  • Daff in a mosque: the teacher said that in Tarim the daff and a "mizmar" were played in some old mosques (al-Saqqaf, al-Mihdar, Suwayh) and the elders accepted it as inherited practice, though some, including Ibn al-Muqri in al-Rawd, said "our mosques have become places of play," and a student said his grandfather's circle kept it outside in the courtyard. The teacher said a prohibition needs a text; the hadith says to announce the marriage in the mosque and beat the daff; he would not himself introduce it in a mosque where it is not customary, and would object in his heart and not attend.

Question 16: a student's marriage with the intention of ending it after study. The resolution says marriage is by nature continuous. The teacher noted the answer avoided stating the ruling clearly: such a marriage is valid if the intention is not stated as a condition, but it is disliked (it does not suit a Muslim, since marriage aims at continuity), and he criticized the use of "the default" ("al-asl") where the real point should have been stated plainly, for the sake of those seeking clear guidance.

Question 17: a woman's appearance at work after plucking eyebrows and using kohl. Answer: kohl is lawful for men and women; plucking some of the eyebrows is not permitted unless the hair deforms her appearance. The teacher found the answer misleading since the question is about appearing before men: kohl is lawful if it does not show, but if it shows to men it becomes adornment that draws attention, turning permitted looking into forbidden. For a man trimming his eyebrows: disliked on the school's view (not forbidden), and plucking for beautification is forbidden for Ibn Hajar's circle only on some readings; Ibn Hajar leaned to no prohibition, while al-Ramli and al-Khatib lean to it when for beautification; removal for a deformity or hair so long it obscures sight is fine, and trimming is lighter than plucking. Bughyat al-Mustarshidin says trimming the length is disliked as altering Allah's creation, though al-Hasan and others allowed it (and said the Prophet did), and the teacher said a reason such as harm removes the dislike.

Question 18: handshaking with the opposite sex at work/study. Answer: shaking hands with an adult foreign woman is forbidden (and vice versa). The teacher retold a story of a Mauritanian delegation to France in which a young student kissed the fingers of a French woman's hand as the others did; when asked about it he answered "she held out her hand and I couldn't pull it back," and they laughed.

Question 19: renting churches for prayers (five prayers, Friday, the two Eids), given statues and images. Answer: no objection when needed, avoiding praying toward statues and images and covering them with a screen if they are in the qibla direction. The teacher disagreed: it is disliked even without statues, and with statues entering is not permitted at all (entering churches needs two conditions: no venerated images and the owners' permission); covering does not cure it since the duty is to remove them. A student said some rooms in a church may be statue-free; the teacher said that if one must, a room with no statues may be used though it is still unbecoming, as the church is a place where the Creator is insulted, and the Muslim should find a classroom, office or stadium. He added the point that passing statues in the entrance does not harm, as in the rule that the sin lying on the way does not stop one from answering a wedding invitation. Staying in such a place is endorsing sin, a sin and not kufr.

Q&A

A student asked about someone who pauses in al-Fatiha at "Malik Yawm al-Din" without intending to cut the recitation. The teacher: if the pause does not change the meaning and is short and not intended to cut it, the recitation is valid, though not the better practice.
