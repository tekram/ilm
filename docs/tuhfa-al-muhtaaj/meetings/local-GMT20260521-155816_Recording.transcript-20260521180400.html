Ijarah of Land and Mounts: Use-Specification, Carried Loads & Trespass on Property - May 21, 2026

📅 2026-05-21 🕐 15:58

Overview

Continuation of the Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj chapter on the conditions of the leased benefit (manfaʿa), beginning where last week stopped at the words "and when the land is fit (ṣaluḥat)". The session covers what must be specified when leasing land for building, planting, or sowing; what the lessee owes in a year of drought when the land cannot be cultivated; the related question of liability when someone places his property on another's land or vehicle without permission; the conditions for leasing a riding mount (knowledge of rider, howdah, daily food, attached gear); the rules for leasing a described (dhimma) mount for riding; the rules for leasing for carriage (knowledge of the load by sight, weight, measure, species, container); and the question of when the species and description of the mount must be specified for carriage.

1. Specifying the Use When Leasing Land

Background / Question

When land is fit for building, planting trees (ghirās), and sowing crops (zarʿ), or for two of the three — what must be specified in the lease for it to be valid?

Ruling

  • The type of use must be specified in the lease, because the harm caused by each use differs.
  • It is sufficient to say "for sowing" (li-l-zirāʿa) or "for you to sow it," without specifying what is sown — in the strongest view (al-aṣaḥḥ). The lessee may sow whatever he wishes, because varieties of crops do not differ much. The contract is therefore not restricted to the least-harmful crop.
  • The Shaykhayn extended the same rule to "for you to plant" and "for you to build": the kinds of plants or building materials (stone, mud, cement) need not be specified; the lessee plants or builds as he wishes. Objections that these vary widely are dismissed; the Tuḥfa's author warns the reader not to confuse this with the parallel rule for sharecropping (muzāraʿa).
  • If the land is suited to only one of the three uses, no specification is needed.
  • Modern question: do zoning permits (residential / commercial / agricultural) issued by the state suffice as a specification, such that anyone using the land outside its permitted purpose is a transgressor? The Shaykh's view: probably not; the type of manfaʿa must be stated in the contract itself; custom or municipal regulation does not substitute, wa-Llāhu aʿlam.

Year of Drought (sanat al-jadb)

  • If the land was leased only for sowing, and a drought year intervenes such that nothing can be grown, the lessee still owes the rent of the like (ujrat al-mithl) for the period of possession, since some benefit was still possible (e.g., tying animals there).
  • Whoever ruled to the contrary on the basis that "there is no rent at this time" was mistaken — and equally so the analogy that some draw to the houses of Mina (which are not rented outside the Hajj season). What matters in penalizing the usurper (ghāṣib) is possibility of benefit, not actual use.
  • A nuanced second view: building implements placed in Mina are not subject to rent at all, because their owner is himself a transgressor in placing them where he ought not — to charge him rent would imply approving his blocking of others from the common public benefit.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, the Shaykhayn, Ibn al-Ṣabbāgh (forthcoming), Ibn al-Rifʿa.

2. Application: Property Placed on Another's Property Without Permission

Background / Question

A side discussion arose from the ālāt Minā case: if someone places his property on yours without permission — a tray of eggs on your car, or a motorcycle parked inside your courtyard after you've locked up — what may you do?

Ruling

  • The placer is a transgressor (mutaʿaddī bi-l-waḍʿ). The owner of the place is essentially a ṣāʾil defending his own property and may remove the trespassing item, depositing it at the nearest suitable place.
  • No liability falls on the remover if the item is then damaged or stolen, because he is not an entrusted person (muʾtaman), not a borrower (mustaʿīr), and not a hired agent (ajīr). He is merely defending his property.
  • The remover may also charge rent for the period during which his property was occupied by the trespasser, parallel to the ghāṣib who owes both the value of the destroyed item and the rent of the period. Combining the right to remove with the right to charge rent is licit: the rent is for the past period of occupation; removal is for ending the trespass — the two concerns are different.
  • A discussant raised an objection: if rent is owed for the occupation, is the occupier not implicitly a tenant? The reply: he is a trespasser, not a tenant; the owner is owed compensation for past occupation just as the ghāṣib of a house owes both rent and value if the house is destroyed.
  • Even if the owner of the place keeps the item (does not remove it), he may still charge rent for the past period, because his silence does not equal consent — silence is not assent for an active transgression.
  • Contrast: a prayer-mat placed in a mosque by someone else is not the mover's to displace, because the mosque is not owned. There the mover may be liable if he removes it.
  • The Shaykh's framing: the obligation to charge rent does not negate the right to recover the value of the lost or destroyed item; the ghāṣib owes both. In the same way, the trespasser owes for occupation and remains liable if the item is damaged in his trespass; but the defender's removal is not negligent action.

Referenced scholars / texts

Drawn from the Tuḥfa's framing of the ālāt Minā case; the chapter on ghasb.

3. "Use It as You Wish": Land, Animal, Human

Background / Question

What is the rule when the lessor says "I have leased it to you for you to use it as you wish"?

Ruling

  • For land that is fit for building, planting, and sowing: "ājartuka li-tantafiʿa bi-hā bi-mā shiʾta" is valid; the lessee may use it for any of the three because he has accepted the most harmful use. (The reading of the text li-yantafiʿa bi-hā al-muʾjar — i.e., the lessee himself — is what is established in the commentaries of al-Ramlī.)
  • For a riding animal (dābba), Ibn al-Ṣabbāgh stipulated for agricultural lease that the lessee must rest the animal when custom requires; abusing the animal is forbidden even on its owner. The land, by contrast, has no analogous prohibition of abuse. Still, Ibn al-Ṣabbāgh's point about agricultural land — that it should be rested when custom demands, to avoid the soil becoming saline (sabikha) — has merit.
  • For a human, this open-ended formulation is invalid. A human is unlike land or animal in that his capacities are highly varied and bounded; one cannot say "use this man for anything you wish," because that exposes him to building, sowing, planting — many of which he is not fit for. The ambiguity (jahāla) is too great.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, al-Ramlī, Ibn al-Ṣabbāgh, the Rashīdī/Sharwānī gloss.

4. Choice Between Sowing and Planting

Background / Question

What of the formulation "if you wish sow, if you wish plant"?

Ruling

  • "In shiʾta fa-zraʿ wa-in shiʾta fa-ghris" — valid, and the lessee chooses between the two; he may do either, because he has accepted the more harmful (planting trees).
  • "Li-tazraʿa wa-taghris" or "li-tazraʿa-hā wa-tughrisa-hā" — invalid, because the proportion of each is not specified.
  • Al-Qaffāl: "Sow half and plant half" — invalid until the lessee specifies which half is for which; the spatial division must be set.
  • "Li-tazraʿa-hā aw tughrisa-hā" — invalid likewise, on the Tuḥfa's reading, because it makes one of the two binding without specification. Even if the intent is "whichever you wish," the explicit wording governs over the unstated intent.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, al-Qaffāl, al-Rawḍ (in the Sharḥ), al-Taqrīr.

5. Leasing a Mount (Dābba) for Riding

Background / Question

What must be known when one leases a riding mount, whether by ʿayn (a specific animal) or dhimma (an animal described in the contract)?

Ruling

  • Knowledge of the rider is required, by sight or by full description sufficient to estimate his weight — body size and build (heavy or thin).
  • Al-Bulqīnī's view that "weight together with description" is required is weak. The decisive factor is weight; modern scales now make this directly knowable.
  • Description and weight are required jointly only for the howdah (maḥmal), because once a specific howdah is set, it does not change, but a rider's weight may change with fattening or weight loss. So weight alone suffices for the rider; for the howdah, both description and weight (or seeing it and testing it by hand) are required.
  • The relied-upon view requires the howdah to be seen AND tested by hand when possible — because to the eye it may appear hollow, but to the hand it may turn out to be solid or weighty.
  • This same rule applies to whatever the rider travels with: saddle (sarj), packsaddle (īkāf), companion-mount (zāmila) — when the variation among them is significant and there is no settled custom. If a stable custom regulates them, the contract is carried to that custom.
  • If the rider brings his own equipment, this is fine; the lessor's only obligation is to provide what is suitable for the animal.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, al-Bulqīnī, al-Adhraʿī, Ibn al-Rifʿa.

6. The Maʿālīq (Hanging Items) and Daily Food

Background / Question

If the rider stipulates that the mount also carry the maʿālīq — the small items hung along on a journey (water-skin, food, cooking pot, basin, jug, mat, pillow, etc.) — what is the rule?

Ruling

  • Stipulating maʿālīq in the absolute, without seeing them and testing them by hand or describing them with their weight, voids the contract on the strongest view, because people vary widely in how much they bring.
  • The rider's daily food along the journey need not be specified by amount; he eats his customary portion.
  • If on a given day the rider happens not to eat — because of hospitality from another, illness, or distraction — he is not obliged to discharge what he would have eaten from the animal's load. Such occurrences are common.
  • If the maʿālīq are not stipulated in the contract at all, the rider has no right to load them — not even a light item like a small water-skin customarily carried, because people vary in this too.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, al-Māwardī.

7. Specific (ʿAyn) vs. Described (Dhimma) Leases of Mounts

Background / Question

What specifications are required in each form of mount lease?

Ruling — Leasing a specific animal (ʿayn)

  • The animal must be specified (not left ambiguous). One of "specified-vs-ambiguous" or "known-vs-unknown" descriptions does not substitute for the other.
  • Whether seeing the animal is required follows the disagreement on sale of an absent object (bayʿ al-ghāʾib); the relied-upon view requires it.
  • The animal must be capable of the load or ride for which it is hired.

Ruling — Leasing in dhimma

  • The contract must state:
  • Species (camel, donkey, etc.);
  • Kind (e.g., bukhtī — a specific variety of camel; though some species names indicate kind too);
  • Male or female — the male is stronger, the female is easier to ride;
  • Mode of gait: baḥr (long stride), qaṭūf (slow), or muhamlij (smooth but quick). The lessee specifies this because slowness causes delay, speed causes hardship and possibly nausea for the rider.

Ruling — Both forms

  • The distance per day must be specified.
  • Whether travel is by day, by night, or both.
  • Where the party stops — settled places (where food and water can be bought) or desert (where neither is available, so provisions must be carried).
  • Exceeding or falling short of the stipulated route is permitted for a specific feared harm (e.g., gunfire on the route between Sana'a and elsewhere): the time of delay caused by such fear is not counted against the lessee.
  • If the road has customary stations, the rider descends at them in measure; if not, stations must be specified or the contract is set by time alone.
  • If the road is dangerous and unpredictable, time-based pricing is also not feasible. Al-Adhraʿī's response (paraphrasing al-Shāmil): from a specified city to another by necessity is allowed.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, al-Shāmil, al-Adhraʿī, the discussion of bayʿ al-ghāʾib.

8. Leasing for Carriage (Ijārah li-l-Ḥaml)

Background / Question

What must be known about the load when leasing for carriage, and what about the animal itself?

Ruling — Knowledge of the load (maḥmūl)

  • If the load is present, the lessor sees it; if it is hidden in a container or in darkness, he tests it by hand to estimate its weight.
  • If absent (or present but in a container), it is specified by measure (kayl) or weight (wazn); weight is preferred for everything because it is more precise.
  • The load's species (genus) must be known because its effect on the animal varies. Even when measure is constant — salt vs. millet — weight and heaviness vary; even when weight is constant, density varies.
  • "Carry on it whatever you wish" — invalid (unlike "sow on it whatever you wish" for land, because land bears anything).
  • If priced by weight (e.g., 100 raṭl of wheat) or measure (e.g., 10 qafīz of whatever you wish), the container is not included in the weight; its weight or description must be separately known. Sacks (gharāʾir) of comparable size satisfy custom; otherwise the container's species must also be specified.
  • If priced "100 manns including container" — the species of the container must be stated, or "100 manns of whatever you wish."
  • If "100 raṭl" alone is said, the container is excluded from the weight.

Ruling — Knowledge of the animal

  • The species and description of the animal need NOT be specified in a carriage lease in dhimma, because the lessor is committed only to transporting the load, and the load arrives unaffected by which animal carries it.
  • Exceptions that require specifying the animal's species and description:
  • The road has mud (waḥl) and not every animal can pass it;
  • The load is fragile — glass, pottery, etc. — and animals vary in stability;
  • For riding lease, where the rider's experience varies — always required.
  • For mere carriage with no special concern, the speed of the animal is not detailed because customary stations bring the caravan together. A weak animal would be considered a defect (ʿayb) the lessee can complain about.
  • Al-Zarkashī held that the animal must be specified when the lease is time-based, because actual distance varies with the animal. The Shaykh's preferred view is that for carriage by amount of distance, this is unnecessary except in the cases just listed.

Modern application: car rental

  • Car rental companies typically leave the use to custom and do not stipulate. Cars are not like animals in this respect — they tolerate substantial variation in load without significant harm. Custom can substitute for strict specification only where it actually prevents disputes; where it leads to disputes, the strict rules return.
  • The Shaykh's view: for ordinary car rental, custom is sufficient; for an unusual or extraordinary use, the rules of the load and animal must be applied.

Referenced scholars / texts

Tuḥfa, al-Sharḥ of al-Ramlī, al-Zarkashī, Ibn al-Rifʿa.
