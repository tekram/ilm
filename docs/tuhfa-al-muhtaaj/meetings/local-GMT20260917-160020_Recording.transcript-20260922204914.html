Ihya al-Mawat: Minerals & Shared Property (Tuhfa) + Fiqh Academy Fatawa - September 17, 2026

📅 2026-09-17 🕐 16:00

Overview

This session opens with Tuhfat al-Muhtaj's Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat (reviving

barren/dead land), covering the section on jointly-owned/shared property

(al-a'yan al-mushtaraka) -- apparent minerals (ma'din zahir) and hidden

minerals (ma'din batin), and their modern application to oil and mining rights.

The second half of the session leaves Tuhfa and moves to reading and discussing

resolutions (fatawa/qararat) of the Majma' al-Fiqh al-Islami al-Duwali (the

International Islamic Fiqh Academy) -- covering the Islamic calendar, letters

of guarantee, and several rulings issued in response to the Islamic

Development Bank's inquiries.

1. Apparent Minerals (al-Ma'din al-Zahir) -- Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat

Background

Apparent minerals are those extracted without labor beyond the initial find --

naphtha, sulfur, tar (qar), mummiya, millstone rock, kohl stone, salt (natural

water/mountain salt), and stray gold nuggets exposed by a flood.

Ruling

  • Not owned merely by land reclamation (ihya'), nor by staking a claim

(tahajjur), nor by a ruler's grant (iqta') -- these remain shared among

Muslims and non-Muslims alike, by analogy to the Prophetic hadith

forbidding exclusive grants over water-access points (mashari' al-ma').

  • Exception: if someone reclaims land for habitation/farming without knowing

it contains such a mineral, he owns the reclaimed plot and whatever mineral

is later found in it.

  • If the mineral requires some processing to extract (e.g., salt made by

digging a basin and channeling seawater), it is ownable by reclamation, and

the ruler may grant it.

  • Where the yield (nayl) is insufficient for multiple claimants: whoever

arrived first is given priority, but only to the extent of his customary

need (as judged by 'urf); if he demands more than his need while it harms

others, he may be forcibly restrained. If several arrive together, or

priority is unknown, they draw lots.

  • Similarly barred from exclusive grant: land claimed merely to take its

firewood or hunting, ponds claimed for their fish, wild trees/thickets

(al-ayka) growing on unowned land, sea/land game, and ambergris cast up by

the sea (belongs to whoever finds it -- rulers have no claim to it).

Discussion note

The class extended this to modern petroleum: since oil requires effort

(hafr/processing) to extract, land is owned by reclamation, and companies

that develop a field acquire real rights over what they extract -- not "the

oil belongs to all Muslims" as some claim.

2. Hidden Minerals (al-Ma'din al-Batin) -- Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat

Ruling

  • Gold, silver, iron, copper, turquoise, ruby, and similar buried minerals

are never owned merely by digging/labor at the site, nor by reclaiming

barren land generally.

  • The extracted yield (nayl) itself becomes owned upon actual taking, not

merely upon discovery, and no ruler's permission is required to take it

(though the ruler may still grant rights of usufruct, not ownership, over

the site).

  • If someone reclaims barren land not knowing it contains a hidden mineral,

he owns both the plot and the mineral extracted from it. If he knew

beforehand and built something there anyway, he owns only the mineral

extracted, not the land (since the site is unsuited to habitation and his

reclamation intent was invalid for that purpose).

  • The buried yield may not be sold before extraction, since its quantity is

unknown (jahala); after extraction the specific find may be sold.

Discussion note

Applied to a real-world istifta' (fatwa request) about a mining company that

surveys land with geological equipment, marks out plots (awtad) with

estimated gold content, and wants to sell rights to those plots. Conclusion:

the company may only sell/transfer its right to search and extract in a

plot it has already staked (a "waiver" of its priority), not the gold itself

(which remains unknown/jahala) nor the land.

3. Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy Resolutions)

Resolution 10/2 -- Unifying the start of lunar months

Tasked the Academy's General Secretariat with commissioning documented

scientific studies from experts in astronomical calculation and

meteorology, and placed the issue on the agenda of the next session for

further technical and juristic study.

Resolution 12/6/2 -- Letters of guarantee (khitab al-daman)

  • An unsecured (bila ghita') letter of guarantee is a suretyship (daman/

kafala); a secured one, where the requester has covering funds, is a

wakala (agency), which may be paid or unpaid, alongside a kafala for the

beneficiary's benefit.

  • Kafala is a gratuitous contract; charging a fee for it is not permitted,

since it resembles a loan that draws a benefit to the lender (forbidden).

  • Ruling: charging a fee for the guarantee operation itself is impermissible;

but actual administrative costs of issuing the letter are permissible,

provided they do not exceed the customary fee (ajr al-mithl) for such

services.

  • Class discussion: on the Shafi'i view this may be tolerated as a shubha

(doubtful matter) rather than outright riba unless explicitly stipulated

as a condition of the guarantee itself.

Resolution (IDB inquiries) -- Loan service fees

Charging fees for loan-related services is permitted only within the bounds

of actual expenses; any amount beyond actual cost is forbidden as riba.

Class discussion distinguished "actual transfer costs" (e.g., wire fees)

from general administrative/staff overhead, which the class leaned toward

excluding on the Shafi'i method (a loan may not carry any conditioned

benefit to the lender).

Resolution -- Leasing operations (ijara muntahiya bi-tamlik)

  • A bank's promise to lease equipment to a client after acquiring it is

permissible.

  • The bank appointing the client (or, preferably, someone else) as its agent

to purchase specified equipment on the bank's behalf, intending to then

lease it to the client, is a valid agency.

  • The lease contract must follow real transfer of ownership to the bank and

be a separate contract from the agency/promise.

  • A promise of a gift (hiba) of the equipment at lease end is valid via a

separate contract; a promise cannot validly be delegated as a vow (nadhr).

  • Risk of loss/defect lies with the bank as owner, barring tenant misuse;

insurance costs on the equipment fall on the bank, ideally through Islamic

(takaful-style) insurance companies.

Resolution -- Deferred sale with installments / foreign trade financing

Same principles as the leasing resolution apply: a promise to sell after

the bank takes ownership is valid; agency to purchase on the bank's behalf

is valid; the sale contract must follow actual possession. The same

framework applies to foreign trade financing.

Resolution -- Disposal of interest earned on foreign bank deposits

The Islamic Development Bank may not protect the real value of its funds

via interest earned on deposits in foreign banks; such interest must be

spent on public benefit purposes (training, research, relief, financial and

technical assistance to member states, educational institutions, and

propagation of Islamic knowledge) rather than retained.
