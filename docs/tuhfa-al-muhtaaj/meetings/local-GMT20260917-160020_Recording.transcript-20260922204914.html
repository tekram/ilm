Ihya al-Mawat: Apparent & Hidden Minerals (Tuhfa) + Fiqh Academy Resolutions - September 17, 2026

📅 2026-09-17 🕐 16:00

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat): the session covers the section on jointly-owned property (al-a'yan al-mushtaraka) — apparent minerals (ma'din zahir) and hidden minerals (ma'din batin) — and its modern application to oil, gas, and mining rights. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali, the International Islamic Fiqh Academy): the class reads and discusses six resolutions covering the Islamic lunar calendar, letters of guarantee, loan service fees, Islamic-bank leasing and installment-sale financing, and the disposal of interest earned on foreign bank deposits.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat

1. Apparent Minerals (al-Ma'din al-Zahir)

Background: apparent minerals are those extracted without labor beyond the initial find — naphtha, sulfur, tar (qar), mummiya, millstone rock, kohl stone, natural/mountain salt, and stray gold nuggets exposed by a flood.

Ruling:

  • Not owned merely by land reclamation (ihya'), by staking a claim (tahajjur), or by a ruler's grant (iqta') — these remain shared among Muslims and non-Muslims alike, by analogy to the hadith forbidding exclusive grants over water-access points (mashari' al-ma').
  • Exception: if someone reclaims land for habitation/farming without knowing it contains such a mineral, he owns the reclaimed plot and whatever mineral is later found in it. If he knew beforehand and built a dwelling anyway, his reclamation intent is invalid (the site is not suited to habitation), so he owns neither plot nor mineral.
  • If the mineral requires processing to extract (e.g., salt made by digging a basin and channeling seawater in), the site is ownable by reclamation, and the ruler may grant it.
  • Where the yield (nayl) cannot support multiple claimants: whoever arrived first has priority, but only up to his customary need ('urf); if he demands more while it harms others, he may be forcibly restrained. If several arrive together, or priority is unknown, they draw lots — except that a Muslim is given precedence over a dhimmi.
  • Similarly barred from exclusive grant: land claimed merely for its firewood or hunting, ponds claimed for their fish, wild unowned trees/thickets (al-ayka), sea/land game, and ambergris cast up by the sea (belongs to whoever finds it — rulers have no claim to it).

Discussion note — modern application to petroleum: since oil requires effort (hafr/processing) to extract, it falls under "minerals requiring processing" — a company that reclaims/develops a field (drilling infrastructure, refining plant, housing for workers) thereby "revives" the site and acquires real ownership of what it extracts, not merely a right of taking. This is distinguished from the claim that "the oil belongs to all Muslims" — that principle applies to minerals extracted without labor, not to petroleum.

2. Hidden Minerals (al-Ma'din al-Batin)

Ruling:

  • Gold, silver, iron, copper, turquoise, ruby, and similar buried minerals are never owned merely by digging/labor at the site, nor by reclaiming barren land generally — because such digging is destructive (kharab) rather than constructive of the site, unlike ordinary land reclamation.
  • The extracted yield (nayl) becomes owned only upon actual taking, not upon discovery; no ruler's permission is required to take it, though the ruler may grant rights of usufruct (irfaq), not ownership, over a site (unlike apparent minerals, where no such grant is recognized).
  • If someone reclaims barren land not knowing it contains a hidden mineral, he owns both the plot and the mineral extracted from it. If he knew beforehand and built a dwelling there anyway, he owns only the mineral extracted, not the land.
  • The buried yield may not be sold before extraction, since its quantity is unknown (jahala); after extraction the specific find may be sold.

Discussion note — modern mining survey: applied to a real istifta' (fatwa request) about a mining company that surveys land with geological equipment, marks out numbered plots (awtad) with an estimated gold content, and wants to sell rights to those plots. Conclusion: since the company staked its claim knowing gold was there, it owns neither the land nor the gold (both jahala); it may only sell/transfer its priority right to search and extract in a plot it has already staked — a waiver of that right, paid for even in gold, since the transaction is a waiver of a right, not a sale of the (still unknown) gold itself.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy Resolutions)

Resolution 10/2 — Unifying the start of lunar months

Tasked the Academy's General Secretariat with commissioning documented scientific studies from experts in astronomical calculation and meteorology, and placed the issue on the next session's agenda for further technical and juristic study.

Resolution 12/6/2 — Letters of guarantee (khitab al-daman)

  • An unsecured (bila ghita') letter of guarantee is a suretyship (daman/kafala); a secured one, where the requester has covering funds, is a wakala (agency) — paid or unpaid — alongside a kafala for the beneficiary's benefit.
  • Kafala is a gratuitous contract; charging a fee for the guarantee operation itself is impermissible, since it resembles a loan that draws a benefit to the lender (forbidden).
  • Actual administrative costs of issuing the letter are permissible, provided they do not exceed the customary fee (ajr al-mithl) for such services.
  • Class discussion: the teacher held that neither a secured nor unsecured letter of guarantee is riba per se, since the guarantee itself is not a loan; only an explicit stipulated condition tying payment to the guarantee would corrupt the contract (and even then, only invalidate that condition/reduce it to a minor sin, not necessarily riba), and mere administrative fees — even if generous — are tolerated on the Shafi'i principle that contracts are judged by their stated wording, not their economic substance.

Resolution (IDB inquiries) — Loan service fees

Charging fees for loan-related services is permitted only within the bounds of actual expenses; any amount beyond actual cost is forbidden as riba. Class discussion distinguished true pass-through transfer costs (e.g., wire/transfer fees, which may be charged to the borrower without turning the loan into interest-bearing) from general administrative/staff-overhead costs (document processing, credit checks, signatures) — the teacher leaned toward permitting the bank to recover its actual per-loan costs but not general institutional overhead, while flagging that the Academy's own resolution text is broader and does not clearly draw this line.

Resolution — Leasing operations (ijara muntahiya bi-tamlik)

  • A bank's promise to lease equipment to a client after the bank acquires it is permissible.
  • The bank appointing the client (or, preferably, someone else) as its agent to purchase specified equipment on the bank's behalf, intending to then lease it to the client, is a valid agency.
  • The lease contract must follow real transfer of ownership to the bank and be a separate contract from the agency/promise — the class flagged a Shafi'i concern here where the client is both the one who "receives" as agent and the one who "receives" as lessee (ittihad al-qabid wal-muqbid); the safer structure is for someone other than the client to act as purchasing agent.
  • A promise of a gift (hiba) of the equipment at lease end, or a lease-ending-in-transfer-of-ownership, is valid via a separate contract; a promise cannot validly be delegated as a vow (nadhr), since a vow is a devotional act not open to agency.
  • Risk of loss/defect lies with the bank as owner (barring tenant misuse/negligence); insurance costs on the equipment fall on the bank, ideally through genuinely Islamic (takaful-style, not merely "Islamic-labeled") insurance companies.

Resolution — Deferred sale with installments / foreign trade financing

Same principles as the leasing resolution apply: a promise to sell after the bank takes ownership is valid; agency to purchase on the bank's behalf is valid; the sale contract must follow actual possession ("do not sell what you do not yet own"). The same framework governs foreign trade financing, where the bank finances a client's purchase of goods from abroad rather than owning them itself.

Resolution — Disposal of interest earned on foreign bank deposits

The Islamic Development Bank may not protect the real value of its funds via interest earned on deposits in foreign banks; such interest must be spent on public-benefit purposes (training, research, relief, financial and technical assistance to member states, educational institutions, and propagation of Islamic knowledge) rather than retained or returned to the foreign bank, since practically speaking the funds have become "lost property" (mal da'i') once absorbed into an interest-bearing system with no realistic path to return them to their true, unidentifiable original payers.
