Ihya al-Mawat: Apparent & Hidden Minerals (Tuhfa) + Fiqh Academy Resolutions - September 17, 2026

📅 2026-09-17 🕐 16:00

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat): the session covers the section on jointly-owned property (al-a'yan al-mushtaraka) — apparent minerals (ma'din zahir) and hidden minerals (ma'din batin) — and its modern application to oil, gas, and mining rights. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali, the International Islamic Fiqh Academy): the class reads and discusses six resolutions covering the Islamic lunar calendar, letters of guarantee, loan service fees, Islamic-bank leasing and installment-sale financing, and the disposal of interest earned on foreign bank deposits.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab Ihya' al-Mawat

1. Apparent Minerals (al-Ma'din al-Zahir)

Background: apparent minerals are those extracted without labor beyond the initial find — naphtha, sulfur, tar (qar), mummiya, millstone rock, kohl stone, natural/mountain salt, and stray gold nuggets exposed by a flood.

Ruling:

  • Not owned merely by land reclamation (ihya'), by staking a claim (tahajjur), or by a ruler's grant (iqta') — these remain shared among Muslims and non-Muslims alike, by analogy to the hadith forbidding exclusive grants over water-access points (mashari' al-ma').
  • Exception: if someone reclaims land for habitation/farming without knowing it contains such a mineral, he owns the reclaimed plot and whatever mineral is later found in it. If he knew beforehand and built a dwelling anyway, his reclamation intent is invalid (the site is not suited to habitation), so he owns neither plot nor mineral.
  • If the mineral requires processing to extract (e.g., salt made by digging a basin and channeling seawater in), the site is ownable by reclamation, and the ruler may grant it.
  • Where the yield (nayl) cannot support multiple claimants: whoever arrived first has priority, but only up to his customary need ('urf); if he demands more while it harms others, he may be forcibly restrained. If several arrive together, or priority is unknown, they draw lots — except that a Muslim is given precedence over a dhimmi.
  • Similarly barred from exclusive grant: land claimed merely for its firewood or hunting, ponds claimed for their fish, wild unowned trees/thickets (al-ayka), sea/land game, and ambergris cast up by the sea (belongs to whoever finds it — rulers have no claim to it).

Discussion note — modern application to petroleum: since oil requires effort (hafr/processing) to extract, it falls under "minerals requiring processing" — a company that reclaims/develops a field (drilling infrastructure, refining plant, housing for workers) thereby "revives" the site and acquires real ownership of what it extracts, not merely a right of taking. This is distinguished from the claim that "the oil belongs to all Muslims" — that principle applies to minerals extracted without labor, not to petroleum.

2. Hidden Minerals (al-Ma'din al-Batin)

Ruling:

  • Gold, silver, iron, copper, turquoise, ruby, and similar buried minerals are never owned merely by digging/labor at the site, nor by reclaiming barren land generally — because such digging is destructive (kharab) rather than constructive of the site, unlike ordinary land reclamation.
  • The extracted yield (nayl) becomes owned only upon actual taking, not upon discovery; no ruler's permission is required to take it, though the ruler may grant rights of usufruct (irfaq), not ownership, over a site (unlike apparent minerals, where no such grant is recognized).
  • If someone reclaims barren land not knowing it contains a hidden mineral, he owns both the plot and the mineral extracted from it. If he knew beforehand and built a dwelling there anyway, he owns only the mineral extracted, not the land.
  • The buried yield may not be sold before extraction, since its quantity is unknown (jahala); after extraction the specific find may be sold.

Discussion note — modern mining survey: applied to a real istifta' (fatwa request) about a mining company that surveys land with geological equipment, marks out numbered plots (awtad) with an estimated gold content, and wants to sell rights to those plots. Conclusion: since the company staked its claim knowing gold was there, it owns neither the land nor the gold (both jahala); it may only sell/transfer its priority right to search and extract in a plot it has already staked — a waiver of that right, paid for even in gold, since the transaction is a waiver of a right, not a sale of the (still unknown) gold itself.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy Resolutions)

Resolution 10/2 — Unifying the start of lunar months

Tasked the Academy's General Secretariat with commissioning documented scientific studies from experts in astronomical calculation and meteorology, and placed the issue on the next session's agenda for further technical and juristic study.

Resolution 12/6/2 — Letters of guarantee (khitab al-daman)

  • An unsecured (bila ghita') letter of guarantee is a suretyship (daman/kafala) — the guarantor merely joins his own liability (dhimma) to the debtor's for what is owed now or later; a secured one, where the requester has covering funds on deposit, is instead a wakala (agency) — paid or unpaid — combined with a kafala for the beneficiary's benefit.
  • Kafala is a gratuitous contract (tabarru') meant for irfaq/ihsan (mutual aid); the resolution's stated reasoning is that charging a fee for it resembles a loan that draws a benefit to the lender (forbidden), since if the guarantor ever has to pay out on the guarantee, that payment functions like a loan to the debtor, and receiving a fee for standing ready to make it looks like being paid to lend.
  • Ruling: no fee may be charged for the guarantee operation itself (regardless of whether the letter is secured or unsecured); but actual administrative costs of issuing the letter are permissible, provided they do not exceed the customary fee (ajr al-mithl) for such services — and where full or partial cover is provided, the admin fee may reflect the actual cost of processing that cover.
  • Class discussion — teacher's own view diverges from the resolution's stated reasoning: he holds that a letter of guarantee, secured or not, is not riba as such at all, because the guarantee contract itself is not a loan (qard) — it only risks becoming problematic if a fee is explicitly stipulated (shart) as a condition inside the guarantee contract itself; even then, the defect only invalidates that specific condition (fasid ash-shart), not necessarily the whole arrangement, and doesn't automatically make it riba. He invoked the legal maxim "ma kana sarihan fi babihi wa wajada nafadhan fi mawdi'ihi la yakunu kinayatan fi ghayrihi" (what is explicit in its own domain and finds legal effect there is not construed as an implicit reference to something else) — i.e. an explicit daman contract is not implicitly reclassified as a qard (loan) merely because payment might occur. He added that, out of caution, he does not stress this looser position publicly/to laypeople, since other schools do treat it as invalid, and normalizing the leniency risks people falling into genuine riba through carelessness — better to treat it as a doubtful matter (shubha) to be avoided than to publicize the permission.

Resolution (IDB inquiries) — Loan service fees

Charging fees for loan-related services is permitted only within the bounds of actual expenses; any amount beyond actual cost is forbidden as riba. Extended class discussion on where the line falls:

  • Genuine pass-through/transfer costs — e.g., a wire-transfer fee, or paying a third party (courier, exchange house) to physically move the money to the borrower — are real, itemizable costs distinct from the loan itself; the teacher affirmed these may be charged to the borrower (e.g., "I lend you $1,000, but the $10 wire fee is on you") without this constituting a conditioned benefit to the lender, since the fee compensates an outside service, not the loan.
  • General administrative/staff overhead — the manager's signature, photocopying documents, the clerk who carries the paperwork between desks, archiving copies — the teacher was more skeptical: from the bank's own internal accounting, these functions are usually booked as general administrative overhead, not as a direct/marginal cost of any one loan, so treating them as a chargeable "actual expense" of that specific loan is questionable; he raised the analogy of a wealthy merchant who employs a bookkeeper, doorman, and guard and then tries to bill a portion of all their salaries onto anyone who owes him money — that clearly should not be allowed, since these are the lender's own overhead, not a service rendered to the borrower.
  • A tangential question was raised: may a lender charge a prospective borrower for the cost of investigating his creditworthiness before agreeing to lend (asking around, checking his standing) — analogized to paying $10 of one's own time/effort to make inquiries about someone before lending to him? The teacher's tentative answer: this fee is not obligatory on the borrower unless he specifically committed to it in advance (e.g., by a vow-like undertaking), since the inquiry is undertaken for the lender's own protection, not requested as a service by the borrower — so it cannot simply be added as a precondition of the loan itself, even though the underlying due-diligence activity (checking solvency, litigation history) is legitimate.
  • Final synthesis: the resolution's "actual expenses" is best read as covering only real amounts paid out to third parties for the specific transaction (transfer/remittance costs, exchange fees), not the bank's general payroll/administrative cost of running its lending operation — though the class noted the Academy's own wording is broad enough ("ujur khadamat," fees for services) that it does not explicitly draw this line itself.

Resolution — Leasing operations (ijara muntahiya bi-tamlik)

  • A bank's promise to lease equipment to a client after the bank acquires it is permissible.
  • The bank appointing the client (or, preferably, someone else) as its agent to purchase specified equipment on the bank's behalf, intending to then lease it to the client, is a valid agency; the resolution itself prefers a purchasing agent other than the eventual lessee where feasible.
  • The lease contract must follow real transfer of ownership to the bank and be a separate contract from the agency/promise. The class raised a Shafi'i-specific concern: if the client himself is appointed as purchasing agent, he ends up "receiving" the goods twice over — once as the bank's purchasing agent, and again as the eventual lessee taking possession for his own use — risking a scenario of ittihad al-qabid wal-muqbid (the same person acting as both the one who hands over and the one who receives) in the chain of transactions; the safer structure is for the bank's own employee or a third party to act as purchasing agent, take possession on the bank's behalf, and only then hand the goods to the client as lessee.
  • A promise of a gift (hiba) of the equipment at lease end (i.e., an ijara-ending-in-ownership structure) is valid, but must be executed via its own separate contract at the time of transfer; a mere promise cannot be validly delegated as a vow (nadhr) instead, since a vow is a devotional act (qurba) not open to representation/agency — just as agency does not validly apply to formal worship acts.
  • Risk of loss/defect (halak, ta'yib) lies with the bank as owner unless caused by the lessee's misuse or negligence (ta'addi/taqsir), in which case liability shifts to the lessee.
  • Insurance costs on the equipment fall on the bank, "as far as possible through Islamic companies" — but the class flagged this phrase as genuinely ambiguous and left it unresolved: does "Islamic companies" mean companies that are Shari'a-compliant/cooperative (takaful) in their actual insurance structure, or merely companies that are nominally "Islamic" while still selling conventional (non-takaful) insurance products, which almost none of the "Islamic insurance companies" in the region actually do? The teacher noted the Academy elsewhere does not endorse conventional commercial insurance, so the phrase should be read as referring specifically to genuine takaful/cooperative arrangements — but agreed the point in the resolution's own text remained unclear enough that it was flagged for the class to mark and revisit rather than settled definitively on the spot.

Resolution — Deferred sale with installments / foreign trade financing

Same principles as the leasing resolution apply: a promise to sell after the bank takes ownership is valid; agency to purchase on the bank's behalf is valid (preferably not the eventual buyer himself, for the same qabid/muqbid concern); the sale contract must follow actual possession by the bank ("do not sell what you do not yet own"), and must be a separate contract from the agency/promise. The same framework governs foreign trade financing — where the bank finances a client's purchase of goods from abroad rather than acquiring the goods for its own use — the same conditions on ownership-before-sale and separation of contracts apply equally there.

Resolution — Disposal of interest earned on foreign bank deposits

The Islamic Development Bank, when obliged to deposit funds in foreign (interest-bearing) banks, may not use the resulting interest to protect the real value of its own capital against currency fluctuation — such interest must instead be spent entirely on public-benefit purposes (training, research, relief efforts, financial and technical assistance to member states, and institutions/schools propagating Islamic knowledge), not retained by the Bank for its own use. Class discussion probed why the interest is not simply returned to the foreign bank that paid it (the ordinarily preferred disposal of ill-gotten gain where the source is known and reachable): the teacher recalled having previously argued the interest should, in principle, be returned to the paying bank if a mechanism exists; a participant countered that return is not practically feasible, since Islamic financial institutions operating in those host countries typically hold only non-interest-bearing accounts with no established channel to redirect interest income back to the origin, and the funds have effectively become commingled and untraceable to a specific counterparty. Given this practical impossibility of return, the class concluded the money is properly treated as "lost property" (mal da'i') — money whose rightful owner cannot realistically be identified or reached — which is why it must be redirected to public-benefit spending rather than kept or refunded.
