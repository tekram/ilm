Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf, the wording): the class covers explicit wordings of waqf (waqaftu, habastu, sabbaltu and their derivatives, and phrases added to "sadaqa"), why "tasaddaqtu" alone is not a waqf, implicit wordings, how a mosque is created by words, the acceptance and rejection of the beneficiary, and the effect of a beneficiary's refusal. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali, resolution 23 (11/3), Washington institute's inquiries): slaughter of the People of the Book (Q20), attending gatherings where alcohol is served (Q21), and sighting the crescent versus astronomical calculation (Q23), with a long discussion of attending a non-Muslim religious festival as a politician. The Q22 answer is not in this recording. The class ends before the section on aiding sin.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Waqf

1. Explicit Wording

Ruling: the explicit words are those derived from waqf: "waqaftu kadha 'ala kadha," "my land/properties are waqf (mawquf) on so-and-so," and also "habastu" and "sabbaltu" and their derivatives ("my properties are hubus on him," "musabbala"), on the sounder view, because they are well known in Shari'a and custom (al-Mutawalli: nothing is reported from the Companions except with them). An acknowledgement ("bear witness I endowed such") also counts.

  • "Tasaddaqtu bi-kadha sadaqatan muharramatan / mu'abbadatan / mawqufatan" (charity forbidden, perpetual, or endowed): scholars found the dispute here puzzling against the clarity of "my land is mawquf," with the answer that there is a dispute there too; Ibn Hajar's answer: "mawquf" in "my land is mawquf" is the intended predicate (the 'umda), while in "sadaqa mawquf" it is a qualifier (na't) that is secondary, which weakens its clarity. The teacher spent time reading the sentence so the students followed this grammatical distinction.
  • Also explicit (on the sounder view): "musabbala," "muhabbasa," "sadaqa hubus," "hubus muharram," "sadaqa thabita" or "batla" (Ibn Khayran), or "sadaqa that is not inherited / not sold / not given" (the "wa" there means "or," as the sounder view is that one suffices, as held by al-Bahr, Ibn Khayran, Ibn al-Rif'a, though al-Subki disputed), because a charitable expression with these indicators allows only waqf. By contrast, "you are irrevocably (ba'in) divorced, forbidden to me ever after" is not explicit divorce, as it could mean separation by annulment or suckling.
  • "Tasaddaqtu" alone is not explicit (nor a kinaya), since it is between obligatory and voluntary charity and waqf; so no waqf results. If he adds a general category ("I gave this in charity to the poor") and intends waqf, it becomes an implicit expression (as al-Rawda, al-'Aziz and al-Zarkashi), and waqf results. If added to a named person or group, it is not an implicit expression of waqf since it is explicit for ownership without return: if the recipient accepts and takes possession he owns it, otherwise not. Al-Zarkashi reports from a group that if he intended waqf, it is a waqf between him and Allah.
  • "Harramtuhu" or "abbadtuhu" is not explicit (it is not used alone but as emphasis), but implicit; the text's "aw" is to avoid implying one alone is non-implicit.
  • "Ja'altu hadhihi al-buq'a masjidan" (I made this plot a mosque) makes it a mosque even without intention, since a mosque can only be a waqf; if he intends waqf or adds "for Allah" it certainly becomes a mosque. "I endowed it for i'tikaf" is explicit for a mosque, since i'tikaf is only in a mosque. "For prayer" is explicit for waqf generally but implicit for a mosque: with that intention it becomes a mosque, otherwise a waqf for prayer (like a school) that is not a mosque.

2. Acceptance and Rejection by the Beneficiary

  • Acceptance: for a specific person or group, acceptance is a condition on the sounder view if they are capable (otherwise by their guardian, right after the offer or on receiving word), like a gift; this is what Imam al-Haramayn and his followers held, and al-Muharrar; relied upon by al-Nihaya and al-Mughni. In al-Rawda the stated preference is that it is not required, since waqf is closer to acts of piety than contracts. The teacher worked out the agreement and disagreement: a bequest needs acceptance by agreement; a vow does not (by agreement of Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli); only waqf is disputed; the text's wording (acceptance required) is relied upon by al-Ramli and al-Khatib, while Ibn Hajar apparently leans to al-Rawda's view.
  • Al-Mutawalli: the dispute applies if waqf is owned by the beneficiary; if it is Allah's, it resembles manumission (objected: manumission is not undone by rejection or by a void condition; answered that the comparison is only for one ruling).
  • If the first generation is the beneficiary, later generations need not accept (though they take from the endower on the sounder view per later scholars). Heirs who inherit all (the case "a waqf on heirs within the third"): acceptance is not required; it is binding as an act of piety seeking permanent reward, since the endower could remove a third from the heirs entirely, so he may endow it on them. If he endows to his sons in proportion to their shares and then to male grandchildren excluding females: some said he is limited; the scholar notes the waqf or bequest conditions apply, so the condition stands.
  • A general category or a pious purpose such as a mosque: no acceptance, by agreement; the administrator's acceptance is not needed, unlike gifts.
  • If the beneficiary rejects: if the specific beneficiary of the first generation (or all or some of those after) rejects, his right is void whether or not acceptance is required, like a bequest. If the heir is the beneficiary within a third, the rejection does not void it (as al-Baghawi: as with manumission). If the first generation rejects, it is a waqf "broken in the middle" for those after. Al-Subki: rejection voids it as with the first generation. A later acceptance after rejection is not counted unless a judge ruled the rejection void (the text: no rejection before the right begins, like rejecting a bequest in the testator's lifetime).
  • A story: Shaykh Fadl 'Irfan said a house endowed by their ancestor stood unused for a long time because the heirs disagreed; he suggested they agree to reject it and revert the property; a student asked if there is a way out for jointly inherited property with many heirs (majority vote). The teacher said waqf and inheritance don't work by vote since each has a right; a rejection only voids that person's share. For a vow, a rejection also voids (though acceptance is not needed), since one cannot force someone to accept a property he dislikes (e.g. out of scruple).

Book 2: Fatawa al-Majma' al-Fiqhi al-Islami al-Duwali (International Islamic Fiqh Academy)

Resolution 23 (11/3) — Inquiries of the International Institute of Islamic Thought, Washington (continued)

Question 20: slaughter of the Jews and Christians and food in their restaurants without knowing if Allah's name was mentioned. Answer: permitted if slaughtered by an acceptable Shari'a method, even if Allah's name is not mentioned; the Academy recommended a deeper study at its next session. The teacher said the intended meaning is slaughtered by the Islamic method (not stunned or struck on the head), since the verse says "your food is lawful to them and theirs to you" and the question's point is doubt.

Question 21: attending public occasions where alcohol is served and sexes mix, without taking part, since withdrawing may isolate Muslims. Answer: it is not lawful for a Muslim to attend gatherings of sin. The teacher distinguished:

  • Aiding sin is forbidden except in necessity; approving it by being present is permitted when there is a strong need, in the sense that he would otherwise lose something with a significant effect by custom (the text's blanket answer is unqualified). The criterion is that the sin does not depend on his participation, only his presence.
  • Examples: a research supervisor who can only be met at that event, without which a term's fees or defense date is affected; a religious festival, a drinking gathering, or other events fall under the same rule.
  • If it is a separate part of a large hall (a man drinking alone in a university hall, a large restaurant), where he is not seen as sitting with the drinkers, it is not even counted as attending the sin.
  • A Muslim politician attending a Hindu festival (Diwali): the teacher said: if he attends in order to endorse the festival, it is forbidden and not out of necessity; if he is present in the place for another need (voters are there and he would lose their support) it is approval of sin permitted only for strong need; and taking part in their rites is certainly forbidden and graver. Muslims should advise him and support him in other matters, not on the festival. A student suggested the community's approach should be not to justify or evaluate his religious reasoning since he did not consult them, but to guide our own decisions (such as voting); the teacher accepted that, adding that the right of a Muslim over a Muslim is advice, ideally privately or through his advisers, and that people confuse a politician with a scholar or representative of Islam. He told the student to record the discussion's distinctions with the date.

Question 23: sighting the crescent for Ramadan/Shawwal where sighting is difficult in America and calculation is possible; may calculation be used, and observatories run by non-Muslims; following other countries causes disputes. Answer: reliance is on sighting and astronomical calculation and observatories are used in support; when sighted in a country, Muslims everywhere must follow it, not heeding differences in horizons.

  • The teacher: this is the Hanbali (and some Hanafi) position, now widely adopted in Saudi influence; the Shafi'i position is that each locality has its own horizon, supported by hadith and reason (e.g. in Indonesia the crescent sets before others see it). The majority hold that differing horizons count; so the stated resolution's last point is not relied upon in the school.
  • A student from the US said the crescent is easy to see there and the question's description was exaggerated; another said sighting needs training and community reports from individuals are unreliable, which the first answered by pointing to local committees; the teacher said the school's rule on witnesses is strict.
  • On calculation: the teacher said the Shafi'i internal disagreement exists: if the crescent is sighted to the east, the lands further west would, scientifically, also have it; but the Shafi'is mostly do not follow this. Al-Ramli accepts calculation based on birth of the crescent, not requiring that it be visible, which Shaykh 'Abd al-Hamid criticized strongly (the teacher marked this as unusual for him). Ibn Hajar's view is stricter. In practice, the teacher said, where Muslims in a place agree, following the group is acceptable, particularly for unity or hardship, as the difference between Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli is a minor one, but one should not take whichever one wants by whim.

The class stopped before the section on aiding sin, which the teacher said needs discussion next time.
