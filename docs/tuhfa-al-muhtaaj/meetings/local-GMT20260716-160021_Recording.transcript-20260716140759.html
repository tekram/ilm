Kitāb al-Ijāra: Post-Lease Obligations, Overloaded Cargo, Tailor Disputes & Wages for Teaching — July 16, 2026

📅 2026-07-16 🕐 16:00

Session Overview

This session continued the study of Kitāb al-Ijāra (Book of Hire and Lease) from the Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj of Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī with Shaykh Ibn Qāsim's commentary. Topics spanned the nature of the lessee's liability, post-lease obligations, overloaded cargo, tailor-client disputes, and an extended discussion on the permissibility of taking wages for teaching Quran and religious knowledge.

1. The Lessee's Hand: A Hand of Amāna (Trust)

Background. The text (Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj) establishes that the lessee's (mustāʾjir's) hand over the leased item (al-ʿayn al-muʾajjara) is a yad amāna — a hand of trust, not a hand of liability. This applies to both a lease of a specific item (ijārat al-ʿayn) and a lease of a guaranteed service (ijārat al-dhimma).

The Tuḥfa notes the difference from a sale where the buyer holds the item during the majlis al-khiyār (option period): the buyer's hand is a hand of liability because he takes possession for his own purposes (li-gharaḍ nafsihi). The lessee's hand, by contrast, is not exclusively for his benefit — it is also for the benefit of the owner (the leased item is being used to provide a service both have agreed upon).

Ruling. The lessee does not owe any compensation for damage to the leased item that occurs without transgression (taʿaddī) on his part.

2. Travel with the Leased Item

Ruling. The lessee may travel with a leased item (e.g., an animal or a tool) wherever he wishes, as long as there is no unusual risk from the travel. The basis: he owns the manfaʿa (usufruct) for the duration of the lease and may exercise that usufruct wherever he chooses. This applies both to leases of specific items and leases tied to a person's labor (ijārat al-dhimma).

If the lessee travels with the item after the lease period has expired, his liability shifts: he is governed by the same rules that govern one who keeps a wadīʿa (deposit) beyond the original arrangement — specifically the question of whether the Shāfiʿī position of al-Subkī (that the depositor must actively return or notify) applies, or the position the Tuḥfa adopts (that takhlia alone suffices).

3. Post-Lease Obligations: Takhlia (Making Available) vs. Active Return

Background. After the lease period ends, what does the lessee owe?

The Tuḥfa adopts — against al-Subkī — the position that the lessee owes only takhlia (relinquishing hold; ceasing use; not preventing the owner from retrieving). He is not required to proactively deliver the item back or notify the owner of its availability.

Key distinction — the ḥānūt (shop) vs. the dābba (animal):

Al-Baghawī brings a well-known example: someone leases a shop (ḥānūt) for one month, then locks it and disappears for two months. He owes the named rent (ajr musammā) for month 1 and ajrat al-mithl (market-rate rent) for month 2. The Tuḥfa cites al-Qaffāl's contrasting ruling on a leased animal: if the lessee ties it up and neither uses it nor prevents the owner from retrieving it, he owes nothing for the second day.

Ibn Ḥajar (al-Haytamī) analyzed the distinction:

  • Animal: The owner can access and retrieve the animal at will even without the lessee's cooperation. No key is required. Therefore mere non-use by the lessee, without active obstruction, does not create liability.
  • Shop: The Shaykh's commentary notes that if the lessee has locked the shop and left, the owner literally cannot access the shop — the locked door is itself a ḥabs (detention), regardless of whether a key was formally handed over at the start of the lease. The case of absence (ghayba) is what creates liability, not the lock itself in isolation; Ibn Ḥajar accepted that al-Baghawī's ruling on the ḥānūt with absence is sound (the ghayba prevents the owner from exercising control over his property).

Implication. If the lessee remains reachable and the owner could ask for return, the lessee's obligation is only to comply. But if the lessee disappears with the key and makes the property inaccessible, he becomes liable for ajrat al-mithl for the period of that effective ḥabs.

4. Lease for Building or Planting: Options at Expiry

Background. If someone leases land to build upon or to plant in, and the lease expires with structures or crops still on the land, what happens?

Ruling. The lessee may choose to remove what he has built/planted (qalaʿ). If he does not choose removal, the landlord (mūjir) is given three options (the three choices established in the waqf/ʿāriya parallel texts):

1. Take ownership of the structure/crop by paying its qīmat mustahaqq al-qalaʿ (the value of the item as if removal had been required — i.e., after accounting for removal costs).

2. Demand that the lessee remove it and pay arsh al-nuqs (compensation for the damage caused by removal to the land).

3. A third option mentioned in some texts, though not the primary relied-upon position.

5. Liability of the Ajīr (Hired Worker) for Loss of Entrusted Items

Background. The text distinguishes types of ajīr:

  • Ajīr mushtarak (shared laborer): one who commits to a specific task in his own dhimma (e.g., a tailor who takes in work from multiple clients, charged per job). Examples: a tailor hired to sew a garment, a dyer hired to dye cloth.
  • Ajīr munfarid (exclusive hire / ajīr khāṣṣ): one who hires himself out exclusively to a single employer for a period of time.

Three positions on the ajīr mushtarak's liability for loss:

1. Aẓhar (strongest): No liability without transgression — he is like the ʿāmil al-qirāḍ (muḍāraba partner) and the mustāʾjir, who are not liable absent transgression.

2. Second position: Liable (analogized to the mustaʿīr/borrower, whose hand is a hand of liability).

3. Third (al-Māwardī): The ajīr mushtarak is liable, at the item's value on the day of loss.

Special case — the watchman (ḥāris al-sikka / al-ghafīr). Al-Qaffāl is cited: if a watchman hired to guard a street or neighborhood fails to prevent a theft, he is not liable (qatʿan — categorically). The reason: the items were never in his possession; he is in the position of a guard (ḥāris), not a bailee. The Zarkashī extracts from this that the khafīr (security guard) generally owes no ḍamān.

Transgression creates liability. If the ajīr acts beyond the scope of the hire — e.g., hires out an animal entrusted to him to a third party, or a teacher strikes a student past the bounds of tāʾdīb (correction), or a husband strikes his wife beyond the permitted bounds — he is liable for harm resulting from that transgression.

6. Overloading a Hired Animal

Background. A detailed analysis of liability when a lessee overloads a hired animal.

Ruling. If someone hires an animal to carry 100 raṭl of wheat (ḥinṭa) but instead loads 100 raṭl of barley (shaʿīr): since barley is lighter per raṭl, it distributes differently across the animal's back, causing a different pattern of stress. The two loads differ in their effect (ḍarar), and loading barley when wheat was specified creates liability for any resulting harm.

The Tuḥfa presents several sub-cases:

  • Exact weight, different substance (e.g., wheat vs. barley): liability depends on whether the difference in effect is meaningful.
  • Heavier material in smaller volume (e.g., iron vs. cotton at same weight): cotton spreads across the entire back while iron concentrates its weight, causing greater localized pressure — different ḍarar.
  • Overload by 10 raṭl extra (hired for 100, loads 110): owes ajrat al-mithl for the additional 10. If the animal perishes, he bears liability as a yad ḍamān (hand of liability) for the extra load — paying proportional qīma if the owner was present, full compensation if the owner was not present.
  • If the owner weighed and loaded himself without the lessee requesting it: no liability on the lessee for the extra, and the owner cannot even recover the extra load — he must return it to its original location.
  • If the owner delivered 110 raṭl to the animal-owner who loaded without knowing: the lessee (muttajir) bears liability for the proportional extra by the madhhab position.

7. Dispute Between Tailor and Customer Over Garment Type

Background. A tailor cuts a garment as a qabāʾ (robe; open at the front, maqshūq min jihāt al-amām) and the customer says he ordered a qamīṣ (shirt; closed). The garment has been cut — there is no returning to the original state.

Ruling. The Aẓhar position: the customer (the owner/mālik) is believed with an oath. He receives no obligation to pay the tailor's wage, since it has been established — through his oath — that no permission to cut the garment in that form was given. The tailor must pay arsh al-nuqs (the difference in value between the garment at its current cut and what it would have been worth if cut as ordered).

Disagreement. A second position: the two parties should perform mutual oaths (yataḥālfān). The Isnawī criticized the majority view, noting that the tailor's oath would establish non-liability for the wage (since the wage is tied to the customer's authorization), and the tailor should bear arsh al-nuqs for the confirmed difference between the current cut and the ordered one.

Sub-case: Dispute before cutting. If the dispute arises before the garment is cut, both parties take oaths (by agreement of all positions). Mutual oath applies uniformly to all cases where the item still exists and can be returned to its original state.

Application to copyists and similar trades. The same rules extend to other cases of deviation from specification: if a copyist rearranges chapters in a book without authorization, he earns only the proportional wage for what can still be used; if it cannot be incorporated, he earns nothing.

8. Taking Wages for Teaching Quran and Religious Knowledge

Background. The final and most extended discussion in this session addressed the ḥadīth often cited against taking wages for religious teaching: "He used the Quran as a means of livelihood" (ittakhadha Qurānahu mutākallan). Some have understood this as prohibiting wages for teaching Quran.

Ruling. The Shaykh explained that this ḥadīth is better understood as referring to a scholar who corrupts his fatwa or teaching for the sake of money — who "sells" Quranic rulings by affirming what his patron wants, not what the Quran says. It does not refer to taking a wage for the act of teaching.

Historical practice: scholars throughout Islamic history received stipends, salaries, and fees for teaching — whether for teaching Quran, fiqh, or other religious sciences. Al-Nawawī himself commended a teacher who teaches with a sound intention and accepts fair compensation, noting that such a person receives double reward: reward for the teaching itself, and reward for earning a ḥalāl livelihood (since seeking ḥalāl income is a religious obligation — farīḍa).

The Shaykh went further: a person who is primarily a doctor or businessman but accepts a teaching wage in order to teach may receive even greater reward than a full-time scholar who teaches for free, because he combines two noble intentions — the intention of teaching and the intention of pursuing ḥalāl employment.

Practical application in the American context. The participants discussed the reality that in the US, qualified scholars are few, and for many, teaching would require leaving lucrative employment. The Shaykh affirmed: a teacher who accepts payment and has sound intention for the teaching earns both rewards — for the teaching and for the legitimate pursuit of income.

One participant shared an observation: students who pay a fee for religious lessons attend more consistently than those who attend for free. A teacher who charges partly to enforce discipline (and not primarily for the money) was analyzed — the Shaykh indicated such a person likely earns reward for the teaching in any case, since the fee serves the legitimate pedagogical purpose of commitment.

Referenced Scholars & Texts

  • Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī: Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj fī Sharḥ al-Minhāj — the primary text being studied throughout
  • ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd ibn Qāsim: Ḥāshiyat Ibn Qāsim on the Tuḥfa — extensively cited for rulings and commentary, including the dispute with Shabrāmallisī on whether the lease contract is valid after Friday prayer time begins
  • Shabrāmallisī: cited in disagreement with Ibn Qāsim on the lease-after-adhan question
  • Al-Baghawī: cited for the ḥānūt (shop) case — the foundational narration on post-lease liability when the lessee disappears
  • Al-Qaffāl: cited for the dābba (animal) case and the watchman's exemption from liability
  • Al-Isnawī: cited for the critique of the Aẓhar position on tailor-customer disputes
  • Al-Zarkashī: cited for the principle that the khafīr (watchman) generally bears no ḍamān
  • Al-Subkī: cited for the minority position that a depositor-equivalent must actively notify the owner
  • Al-Nawawī: cited for his commendation of teachers who take wages with correct intention
  • Al-Rāfiʿī (al-Anwār): referenced on the shop-key question
