Opening of Kitab al-Nikah & Indian Fiqh Academy's 21st Session Resolutions - August 04, 2026

📅 2026-08-04 🕐 15:57

Overview

This session marks a change in the Tuesday curriculum: the class now opens

the reading of Tuhfat al-Muhtaj's Kitab al-Nikah (Book of Marriage),

alongside continuing the Indian Fiqh Academy resolutions that have been read

on Tuesdays. The first part covers the linguistic and legal definition of

nikah, whether it is a contract of ownership or mere license, and the five

rulings (recommended, obligatory by cause, disliked, etc.) that attach to it,

including several branch questions raised by the students. The second part

reads three resolutions from the 21st session of the Fiqh Academy of India

(Takaful insurance, marital discord/khula, and narcotics), followed by a

closing Q&A on the rules of greeting (salam) between men and women.

Part A — Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah (opening)

1. Definition of Nikah

Background / Question

What does nikah mean linguistically and legally, and why do jurists use

this term rather than "marriage contract" ('aqd al-zawaj)?

Ruling

  • Linguistically, nikah means "joining" (damm) and "intercourse"

(watʼ) — as in "the trees intertwined" (tanakahat al-ashjar). Legally,

it is defined as a contract that permits intercourse by means of a

specific wording.

  • The word is literal (haqiqa) for the marriage contract and figurative

(majaz) for intercourse — proven because negation of the word is valid

for intercourse ("he did not consummate her" can be truly said even if he

fornicated with her, but could not be truly said if it referred to the

contract) and because it would be indecorous for the term to be literally

about the act itself; hence it is used euphemistically for the contract.

  • Qur'anic usages such as "until she marries another husband" (*hatta

tankiha zawjan ghayrah*) are shown by the hadith "until you taste her

little honey" ('usaylatuha) to refer to consummation, not merely the

contract — an example of the figurative sense being intended by context.

  • Regarding "the fornicator marries none but a fornicatress" — Ibn al-Rif'a

held the intended meaning is "has intercourse with," while others held the

reverse (literal in intercourse, figurative in contract), and still others

held it is used literally in both senses — though the last view was

rejected as contrary to the accepted principle that treating a word as

figurative in one place is preferable to positing pure ambiguity

(ishtirak).

  • Consequence: if someone swears not to "yankih" (marry), he breaks his

oath only by contracting a marriage, not by consummating one. Likewise,

if a man fornicates with a woman, no relationship of muharram-by-marriage

(musahara) is established with his son through her.

  • Ibn Hajar notes he compiled over a hundred proofs (Qur'anic verses and

hadiths beyond the consensus) on this topic in his own work titled

al-Ifsah 'an Ahadith al-Nikah. Marriage is legislated from the time of

Adam and continues even in Paradise — unlike every other contract, which

ends with this worldly life.

2. Benefits of Marriage & Ownership vs. License

Background / Question

What purposes does marriage serve, and is the marriage contract a form of

ownership (tamlik) of the wife's intimate right, or merely a license

(ibaha) to enjoy it?

Ruling

  • Three benefits are named: preservation of lineage, relief of what is

harmful to withhold, and the attainment of pleasure and enjoyment — the

last being the only one of the three that persists in Paradise.

  • The correct (mu'tamad) position is that marriage is a license, not

ownership. The practical difference appears if a husband swears "I own

nothing" while having a wife: on the ownership view he would break his

oath (since he would be "an owner of the right to benefit, not owner of

the benefit itself" — meaning he could not lease or lend that right, since

true ownership of the benefit would permit that); on the license view, no

violation occurs.

  • If a wife is had by a man through a mistaken-identity intercourse (*wat'

bi-shubha), the dower (mahr*) belongs to her by unanimous agreement

regardless of which of the two views is taken, because even under the

"ownership" reading the husband merely owns the right to benefit, not the

benefit (i.e., the dower) itself.

  • A husband is not obligated to have intercourse with his wife, since it is

his right, not a duty — though some hold it recommended once, to satisfy

her desire and to make the dower fully due (since the dower is only fully

secured, rather than halved, once consummation or death occurs).

3. The Five Rulings of Marriage

Background / Question

Is marriage recommended, obligatory, permissible, or disliked, and under

what conditions does each ruling apply?

Ruling

  • Recommended for one who is in need of it — defined as having desire

(tawq) for intercourse, even if latent — and who possesses "the means"

(al-ahba): the dower, clothing for the season of consummation

(kiswat fasl al-tamkin), and one day-and-night's maintenance. This

applies even if he is occupied with worship or study, per the hadith

"Whoever among you is able to marry, let him do so, for it lowers the gaze

and guards chastity" — since neglecting marriage for study could itself

cause other harms.

  • The hadith's term al-ba'a means intercourse together with the financial

means for it (per the parallel hadith "whoever among you has the means,

let him marry"); hence "whoever cannot" refers to one who lacks the

means despite being physically capable — since it is this person, not

someone lacking desire altogether, who is directed to fast instead

(fasting curbs desire, which would make no sense to prescribe to someone

without desire in the first place).

  • Marriage is not obligatory merely from this hadith, since "marry

whom is good for you" (ma taba lakum) in the Qur'an indicates

permission — though the stronger answer given is simply that no scholar

has ever taken the apparent wording of the hadith to mean unqualified

obligation (an implicit consensus), rather than relying on the "*ma

taba*" verse alone (which really refers to which women are lawful to

marry, not to voluntariness).

  • One student raised an objection from usul al-fiqh: a command tied to an

innate natural inclination (jibla), like "eat and drink" in the fasting

verse, does not indicate obligation because the inclination itself

already motivates the act — the same reasoning was affirmed as applicable

here, since desire for a spouse can even exceed desire for food.

  • Marriage becomes obligatory by extrinsic cause: (1) if vowed (since a

vow can only attach to something recommended, and marriage is

recommended when need and means coincide) — though al-Ramli, his son in

al-Nihaya, and al-Khatib held a vow to marry does not bind, since its

underlying default status is mere permission, not recommendation; (2) if

one contracted (without a vow) to buy and free a slave, buying the slave

becomes obligatory as a means; (3) discussed but contested — whether a

husband who improperly (in a bid'i, Sunna-violating manner) divorced a

wife he was wronging in the marital rotation (qasm) must remarry or

take her back to fulfill her due turn; Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli differ on

characterizing this as a genuine exception to "resuming is only Sunna, not

obligatory" versus a distinct case caused by the need to redress the

wrong done to her.

  • Marriage may be obligatory to avoid fornication: one view holds it

obligatory unconditionally for anyone fearing zina (since chastity cannot

otherwise be secured); another holds it obligatory only if he does not

wish to instead take a slave-concubine (tasarri).

  • On tasarri (concubinage): a group's objection that one cannot lawfully

buy a slave-woman today because the mandatory one-fifth (khumus) share

cannot be properly allocated is answered as follows — the impediment

applies only where it is confirmed that she was captured by a Muslim (in

which case a fifth belongs to the state/mentioned parties in the Qur'anic

verse) or confirmed that a disbeliever bought her from a disbeliever who

captured her legitimately from a hostile disbelieving state with no truce

or treaty (in which case, since the second disbeliever "captured" her by

purchase from an owner with no valid title, buying her from that second

disbeliever incurs no khumus obligation on the Muslim buyer). Where the

chain of custody is genuinely unknown, the default (lawfulness) applies.

  • Marriage in the land of disbelief (dar al-harb): recommended to be

avoided altogether (not prohibited), out of fear that one's children

would be raised in the captors' religion or enslaved if captured in war

(lineage would be legally established, but freedom from bondage would

require formal legal proof) — unless he fears falling into zina if he

does not marry, since a certain harm (zina) outweighs a merely feared,

uncertain future harm. The same reasoning was extended to concubinage and,

by analogy, to marrying into innovation-steeped communities (e.g., where

children risk being raised into takfiri or Shi'i beliefs), where avoiding

marriage is likewise preferred unless zina is feared.

  • If the means are altogether lacking: it is recommended to forgo

marriage, per "let those who cannot marry maintain chastity" — al-Rafi'i's

wording ("preferable not to marry") was judged by some to be a weaker

recommendation than al-Nawawi's ("recommended to forgo"), though the

session concluded there is no real difference between the two

expressions.

  • A student's earlier query about whether the "one day and night" of

maintenance should be interpreted flexibly for salaried (monthly-paid)

workers versus day-laborers was addressed at the start of the reading:

the ruling's wording is unqualified (mutlaq) and is not restricted by

employment type, so a monthly-salaried man lacking ready cash for

remaining days of the month, despite having today's due, would not yet

meet the threshold for recommended marriage on the literal wording of the

jurists — though this was noted as a point potentially open to

reasoning-by-cause (ta'lil) rather than mere literal transmission.

Referenced scholars / texts

Ibn Hajar al-Haytami (author of Tuhfat al-Muhtaj and of *al-Ifsah 'an

Ahadith al-Nikah*), al-Baijuri, Shihab al-Din al-Ramli, al-Nawawi, al-Rafi'i,

Ibn al-Rif'a, al-Subki, al-Zarkashi ("Bahr al-Zarkashi"), al-Shirwani and

Ibn Qasim al-'Ibadi (in the commentary/hashiya being read alongside the

main text), al-Ghazali's Ihya, Abu Ishaq al-Marwazi, Abu Hanifa (cited

regarding contraception opinions).

Part B — Indian Fiqh Academy, 21st Fiqh Symposium Resolutions

4. Resolution 89/1/21 — Takaful (Cooperative Insurance)

Background / Question

What is the Shariah-compliant model for Takaful insurance, and what steps

should the Fiqh Academy and Indian government take regarding it?

Ruling

  • The ideal Takaful structure is one based on mutual donation (tabarru')

and cooperation, without tying participants' entitlement to benefits to

investment returns.

  • Islamic Takaful may be structured on any of three bases: a gift-for-

consideration (hiba bi-'iwad), a binding commitment to donate or a

(non-binding) promise to gift, or a waqf (endowment) — any one of these

may be chosen depending on circumstances and applicable law.

  • The Academy resolved to form a committee (five qualified scholars plus

three insurance/financial/legal experts) to study existing Takaful models

in light of Indian law and produce detailed reports and guidance, and to

call for the establishment of a Shariah supervisory body with binding

authority over Takaful companies' practices.

  • The resolution also calls on Muslims to establish charitable endowments

and mutual-aid associations for the poor and disaster-affected, and urges

the Indian government to support the establishment of riba- and gambling-

free Takaful companies by removing legal obstacles.

  • The scholars in the session likened the underlying spirit of Takaful to

the mutual pooling practiced by the Ash'ari tribe praised by the Prophet

ﷺ ("They are of me and I am of them"), who would pool resources during

hardship and redistribute equally.

5. Resolution 90/2/21 — Marital Discord (Shiqaq) and Khula

Background / Question

What is the Shariah view on preserving the marital bond, and what should

happen when a wife wants a divorce but the marriage cannot be salvaged?

Ruling

  • Marriage is a noble, pure bond in Islam that should be kept as durable

and firm as possible; a husband should not divorce without valid reason,

nor should a wife seek khula (mutual release) without valid reason. In

disputes, both should return to the Qur'an and Sunna and adopt mutual

forgiveness and forbearance.

  • If the relationship's basic goals (tranquility, mutual affection) are no

longer met and the wife requests divorce, the husband should grant it

without intending to harm her by delay; if he does not wish to divorce

but she offers khula, he should release her.

  • Shiqaq (discord) is defined as bitterness in the marital relationship

that resists reconciliation.

  • If discord arises and the wife categorically refuses to remain married,

the judge should first attempt reconciliation; failing that, he pursues

khula; family members of both spouses should also try to resolve the

dispute; if all this fails after genuine sustained effort, annulment of

the marriage according to any of the four Sunni schools' criteria (even

if not the couple's own school) becomes permissible, given its

conditions are met.

  • Q&A — psychological harm as "darar"/"shiqaq": verbal abuse or

repeated threats of taking another wife count as harm/discord only if

they actually induce a diagnosable psychological state in the wife (per

expert assessment) — mere dislike of such talk, without resulting

psychological harm, does not qualify. A case cited from *Lughat

al-Mustarshidin* (al-'Allama al-Jifri): a wife who suffered visible

distress, anxiety, and screaming whenever her husband entered, and who

calmed down only once he left, was ruled not obligated to submit to him

physically (though her maintenance entitlement would lapse), while the

husband was likewise not obligated to leave his own home nor to divorce

her — since the marriage contract itself remains binding; the recommended

(but not obligatory) course for him is to release her via khula in

exchange for compensation, since Shafi'i doctrine does not empower a

judge to compel a husband into khula (unlike some other schools) — she

would otherwise need to seek relief through a court following a

different school's ruling on compulsion.

6. Resolution 91/3/21 — Narcotics

Background / Question

What is the Fiqh Academy's position on alcohol versus other narcotics, and

what obligations follow from that?

Ruling

  • Treating alcohol as categorically different from other narcotics (as

some modern legal systems do) is rejected as dangerous and inconsistent

with human welfare; a firm stance should be taken against alcohol just as

against other intoxicants, banning its trade.

  • The Indian constitution's provisions aiming to curb intoxicant use should

be given full legislative effect.

  • All of humanity, and Muslims especially, are urged to avoid narcotics,

since the human body and mind are a trust from God, not one's own

property to damage.

  • Alcohol's prohibition applies regardless of name or the substance from

which it is manufactured; other narcotics are prohibited specifically

insofar as they are intoxicating, whether liquid or solid, and regardless

of method of consumption (including injection).

  • Cultivating, trading in, or facilitating the production of narcotics such

as cannabis and opium (for non-medical purposes) is impermissible, as is

their purchase or sale; anyone who consumes them is deserving of

punishment, and Muslims are obligated, as able, to forbid this by every

possible means; those who spread the habit through cultivation or trade

deserve especially severe punishment.

  • Taking preventive measures to protect addicts from the habit is a

religious and moral duty; if voluntary treatment fails and the case

becomes one of dire necessity, gradual medical treatment under expert

physician supervision (which may itself involve controlled use) is

permitted. The body is a divine gift to be safeguarded from all harm,

including tobacco and its derivatives.

7. Closing Q&A: Greetings (Salam) Between Men and Women

Background / Question

If speaking to an unrelated man is permitted for a woman when there is a

genuine need, does that permission extend to an initial greeting (salam),

and how does this apply to phone calls, television appearances, and similar

modern situations?

Ruling

  • The governing principle is not "speech is permitted for need, so greeting

is a subset of permitted speech," but rather: wherever seclusion

(khilwa) is not present, greeting is likewise permitted (from either

party) — e.g., two trustworthy women together with a man who observes

modesty with them are not in khilwa, so mutual greeting is unobjectionable;

likewise, a gathering with a mix of men and women where custom rules out

any likelihood of impropriety is not khilwa, so greeting is permitted.

  • By this reasoning, a woman greeting a shaykh appearing on a television

broadcast with many viewers is not prohibited, since there is no seclusion

and no real suspicion, given the large audience.

  • By contrast, a phone call placed to a business (e.g., ordering from a

restaurant) is not a context of permitted greeting — she should state her

order directly without an opening greeting, since her need there

justifies only the minimum necessary speech (the transactional request),

not an unrelated pleasantry; the underlying rule is that non-essential

speech beyond the need — even a greeting — is not permitted wherever a

suspicion-inducing privacy (rather than genuine publicness) is present.

  • Reference was made to Nihayat al-Zayn's discussion (under obligations

related to jihad) and to I'anat al-Talibin for supporting textual

citations on the same principle.

Referenced scholars / texts

Al-'Allama al-Jifri (Lughat al-Mustarshidin), Nihayat al-Zayn,

I'anat al-Talibin, and the Fiqh Academy of India's 21st Fiqh Symposium

proceedings (held at the Islamic University in Indore, attended by

delegates from Egypt, Mauritius, the US, Canada, Britain, South Africa,

and across India).
