Kitab al-Nikah: Fasl al-Khitba & Proposing to Women in Iddah (Tuhfa) + Surrogacy Resolution (Fatawa al-Hindi) - October 6, 2026

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, Fasl al-Khitba): a new section begins, on the engagement proposal (khitba, with a kasra on the kha'): whom one may propose to, explicit proposal (tasrih) versus allusion (ta'rid), the position of women in 'idda of every type, and whether the proposal itself is recommended. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Hindi): the class reads Resolution 105/23 of a seminar of Indian scholars on womb rental and lending (surrogacy), which prohibits it, with discussion of lineage, whether it counts as zina, and artificial wombs. Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, Fasl al-Khitba

1. Definition and Whom One May Propose To

Background: khitba is seeking marriage from the woman (the suitor asks for marriage).

Ruling:

  • Proposing is permitted to a woman free of marriage and of 'idda, both explicitly and by allusion; proposing to a married woman is forbidden in both forms. Both rulings are by consensus.
  • The teacher noted that the text implies further conditions, taken from later text and the commentary: she must also be free of every other impediment to marriage (such as being a Magian), and free of another man's existing proposal.
  • Apparent objection: a woman in 'idda from doubtful intercourse (shubha) seems permitted to be proposed to although she is not free of 'idda. Answer: only allusion is permitted to her, not explicit proposal, as the later text ("no explicit proposal to a woman in 'idda") shows, and "free" means free of all impediments. A triple-divorced woman may not be proposed to by her former husband until she marries another man and completes that 'idda.
  • A slave woman used as a concubine (amah mustafrasha): the wording might suggest she may be proposed to even if her master has not turned away from her. Ibn Hajar: it is forbidden in general unless a clear sign shows the master has lost interest or wants her married, because it harms him: she is in the position of a wife, and even his knowledge that another man's eye is on her harms him. The teacher read this as: the default is to treat her as a wife unless a sign shows otherwise. Signs include a long time with him or having borne him children.
  • Harm to the husband versus the wife: a student asked about harm to the wife. The teacher said harm to the husband (jealousy) is the usual reason jurists give, but the ruling is not restricted to it: even if the wife loves her husband and he does not care, proposing to her can spoil her against him, as the husband may let her go once someone else wants her.
  • Al-Mawardi's objection (by analogy a man could not propose to his wife's sister) is answered: al-Bulqini says it is lawful if his intent is that when she agrees he will separate from the wife, and Ibn Hajar calls this the tenable view. A student raised the modern case of a man proposing to his wife's friend. The teacher: it is his right, he may marry another woman after his wife, and the damage to their friendship does not make it forbidden. This differs from proposing to a married woman, where the proposal itself is meant to break an existing marriage.
  • A proposal to a minor or a virgin with no compelling guardian is not forbidden: al-Bulqini's suggestion that it is forbidden is weak, unless the suitor intends an invalid contract.
  • Proposing to a Magian woman whom he would marry if she becomes Muslim is permitted.

2. Is the Proposal Recommended?

  • The word "permitted" implies it is not recommended, which is what al-Rafi'i and al-Nawawi transmit from the early scholars. Al-Ghazali: it is sunna, citing the Prophet's practice and people's custom.
  • Some argued it follows the ruling of marriage, since means take the ruling of ends. The objection: that would make it obligatory when marriage is obligatory, which seems far-fetched. Ibn Hajar: there is no problem if it truly is a means. He ties this to the proposal while in ihram: stating a proposal is only disliked, but if he proposes in order to marry during ihram it is forbidden (and likewise a lawful man proposing to a woman in ihram). It differs from the woman in 'idda because the end of 'idda depends on her own word, which she may falsify, whereas the end of ihram does not depend on her.
  • He then rejects that it is a means in an absolute sense, because marriage does not depend on it: marriage often occurs without a proposal, so "means take the ruling of ends" cannot be applied across the board.

3. Women in 'Idda

Married woman: proposing is forbidden both explicitly and by allusion, and also anything expressing his desire for her, even without intending marriage, e.g. "What a pity, your husband beat me to you, I wanted you." The teacher said this may hurt the husband and so be forbidden, and compared the sales rule about undercutting someone who has already bought.

Explicit proposal (tasrih): forbidden to every woman in 'idda, except to the man whose 'idda it is. This covers 'idda from death, from shubha, from irrevocable or revocable divorce, and from annulment (fasakh because of a defect, or automatic annulment through li'an or suckling). It is forbidden by consensus because she may desire him and lie about her 'idda ending. The teacher stressed:

  • This is a wisdom (hikma), not a binding cause: even for a woman known to be truthful, or one counting by months where lying is impossible, the ruling remains, as it is a matter of worship rather than reasoned.
  • The man whose 'idda it is may propose to her if he may marry her. If he cannot (a triple divorce during her 'idda, or a man who had intercourse with a woman in 'idda by shubha and she conceived), he has no right: his 'idda comes first, then she completes the remainder of the earlier 'idda, so he may not propose.

Allusion (ta'rid):

  • Forbidden to a revocably divorced woman, even with the husband's permission, since she is in the position of a wife. Also forbidden to a woman in 'idda from apostasy of the husband, as both can return to the marriage (by ruj'a or by Islam). If the woman herself apostatized, she cannot be married at all, so a proposal is invalid; al-Rashidi: proposing to an apostate woman in order to marry her once she returns to Islam is not forbidden.
  • Permitted: allusion (without alluding to intercourse) to a widow in 'idda, even if pregnant, per Qur'an 2:235 ("no blame on you for what you allude to in proposing to women"); the fear that she might abort the child to remarry is rare. Also to an irrevocably divorced woman (ba'in), whether her 'idda is by cycles or months, on the preferred view, by the verse's generality. For women separated by triple divorce, suckling, or li'an, there is no dispute that allusion is permitted, though some extended the dispute to them.

4. Explicit vs. Allusive Speech

  • Tasrih: what indicates for certain a desire to marry, e.g. "When your 'idda ends I will marry you."
  • Ta'rid: what could mean that or not, e.g. "You are beautiful," "Who could find one like you?", "Allah will drive good to you," "Many a man desires you," and "I desire you" (as al-Isnawi transmitted from al-Umm and relied upon).
  • Allusion to intercourse ("I can have intercourse with you") is forbidden. Allusion in other respects is merely disliked. The teacher noted that saying "you are beautiful" to a woman can be flirtation and a cause of fitna even without intending marriage (e.g. a colleague who complains about her husband), which is a separate matter.
  • Al-Rawda and al-Mughni: allusion to intercourse with a woman one has proposed to is disliked for its ugliness, and becomes forbidden if it amounts to explicit mention of intercourse. Referring to it by "touching" does not count as explicit. Explicit speech about intercourse is not disliked to one's own wife or slave woman, since they are the place of his enjoyment.
  • Rhetoric note: rhetoricians say allusion is more eloquent than direct speech; that is a rhetorical point, while jurists follow ordinary speech. The teacher illustrated with the story of the woman who told the ruler "I have no mice in my house," saying she is poor without saying so, which is stronger than saying "I'm poor."

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi

Resolution 105/23 — Womb rental and lending (surrogacy)

A seminar of Indian scholars; the class read from p. 250 of their copy.

Introduction: keeping to the natural disposition (fitra) secures success in the hereafter and in this world, and Shaytan urges man to revolt against it (Qur'an 4:118-119). The resolution criticizes modern Western civilization for treating religion and morals as having no place in life: permitting unlawful relations in place of marriage, legalizing same-sex marriage, and treating indecency as a basic right. Allah placed sexual drives in all animals for procreation, but gave humans the distinction of covering and chastity: the drive is confined to spouses, which builds families, lineage and mutual rights and duties, unlike animals (Qur'an 4:1, 49:13, 25:54).

Methods described:

1. The husband's sperm and a stranger woman's egg are fertilized, then placed in the wife's womb.

2. The husband's sperm and the wife's egg are fertilized in the womb of a stranger woman.

3. A stranger man's sperm is fertilized in another woman's womb for a woman who wants children.

4. A stranger man's sperm with a woman's egg, fertilized in the womb of the same woman (as worded in the reading).

The common element: the woman who wants children uses a stranger man's sperm, a stranger woman's egg, or a stranger woman's womb. The resolution says all of these lead to many moral and religious harms.

Reasons given:

  • Moral and psychological harm, and treating a woman's body as a commodity for sale and purchase, contrary to the dignity of motherhood.
  • Severe grief for the surrogate when the child is taken from her (Qur'an 46:15: "his mother carried him with hardship"), and the commissioning woman lacks the bond of one who endured the pregnancy.
  • Doubt about the man's role: whether a man who accepts a stranger's sperm for his wife will treat a daughter born of it as a father should (guarding honor).
  • Conflict over custody between the egg's owner and the carrier. A student told of a doctor-teacher's account of a woman who rented a womb for $10,000, then on seeing the baby refused to give him up, returned the money and took the case to court.
  • The loss of genetic identity, which is a source of pride and whose loss brings humiliation; a person doubting which woman is his mother lives in distress. Lineage also connects through the mother, and some families continue through daughters.

Seminar resolutions:

1. A woman carrying a stranger man's sperm or another woman's egg in her womb, whether for commerce or as a loan, is definitely forbidden.

2. A man may not give his sperm to be fertilized in a stranger woman's womb or mixed with her egg.

3. Physicians may not assist in this process.

4. The Indian government should enact a law ending it.

5. Fellow citizens should call on the government to stop it.

6. The seminar asks the government not to legalize any act contrary to moral values recognized by all religions.

Class discussion:

  • Is it zina? A student asked. The teacher: no, not in the sense of the penalty or the sin of zina, which requires insertion of the glans. Insertion of semen without that is not zina; at most ta'zir for the woman if she did it deliberately. The phrase that she "does what a prostitute does" is a comparison by outcome (mixing of lineage). If the husband's semen was respected (muhtaram) at the moment of exit, as Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli require, and entered by mistake, the child is attributed to him.
  • Artificial wombs: a student asked about devices being developed (partly for women who want to protect their bodies and careers). The teacher: it removes the objection of renting another woman's womb, and the mother would be the egg's owner. The remaining question is obtaining the egg, which requires uncovering the awra, allowed only for need (e.g. real psychological harm from childlessness or health risk), and the child is attributed to the father provided the semen was obtained lawfully. A student noted that operations performed through imaging screens avoid direct viewing of the awra. The teacher accepted this as a lighter route, since looking at an image is not looking at the awra, and a doctor working daily is thinking about work, not desire. In principle an external womb has no apparent legal problem if there is no harm to the child; the only issue is later teasing as "child of the tube." The teacher also recalled a clip of someone raising a chick in an opened egg.
