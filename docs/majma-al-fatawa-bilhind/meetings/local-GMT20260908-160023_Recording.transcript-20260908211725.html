Looking at Mahrams & Children, Sukuk & Women's Protection Resolutions - September 08, 2026

📅 2026-09-08 🕐 16:00

Overview

This Majma' al-Fatawa Bilhind session continues the Tuhfat al-Muhtaj chapter on looking (nazar) — this time toward mahram relatives, slaves, and pre-pubescent children — then moves to readings from Islamic Fiqh Academy (India) resolutions on sukuk and on international "women's protection" recommendations, closing with several fiqh Q&A items (adornment/nail decoration, ihram and eyeglasses/perfume).

1. Looking at a Mahram, a Slave, and a Child

Background / Question

The text addresses what a man may look at on a mahram relative (someone permanently unmarriageable to him by blood, breastfeeding-kinship, or marriage-affinity), then extends to a slave looking at his/her owner, and finally to looking at and touching young children.

Ruling

  • Between mahrams, the region between navel and knee is forbidden to view; the navel and knee themselves are subject to disagreement — Ibn Hajar treats them as also forbidden out of caution (paralleling their status as 'awra in prayer, unlike in ordinary looking where they are not), while al-Ramli and al-Khatib permit looking at the navel/knee points themselves, restricting the prohibition strictly to what lies between them.
  • Elsewhere on a mahram's body, looking without desire is permitted, even across a difference of religion (e.g., a non-Muslim mahram), except where that person's own religion permits marriage to close relatives (e.g., Zoroastrians) — in that case the ruling reverts to full prohibition, including seclusion, since the protective legal reasoning (that mahram status itself blocks marriage) no longer applies to them.
  • A slave/servant's permitted viewing of an unrelated woman not his owner (or an owner viewing a non-owned man) is restricted to what is customarily visible in service/labor (al-mihna): the head, neck, hands up to the upper arm, and legs up to the knee — not the chest, even the upper chest.
  • A trustworthy male slave may look at his female owner (and vice versa) under conditions: the slave must be characterized by full uprightness ('adala, not mere chastity from fornication), and must not be jointly owned, partially manumitted (mub'ad), or under a manumission contract (mukataba) — in which case the ordinary stranger rules apply instead.
  • A castrated/emasculated man (mamsuh) is treated like a mahram for viewing purposes under the same uprightness conditions, provided no residual inclination toward women remains; a man with a cognitive disability who shows no interest in or awareness of women is likewise given mahram-like treatment for viewing (though not for nullifying ablution by touch), per a position attributed to al-Nawawi.
  • Neither an adult mahram nor an adult slave/eunuch needs permission to enter at all hours (unlike a non-mahram adolescent, who — per the verse on seeking permission — technically still must ask at the three specified times, though this is now considered a matter of etiquette rather than an obligation, since closed doors and locks have superseded the original need).
  • Regarding children: the private area of a girl, even in infancy, is treated with more caution than a boy's — a nursing/caregiving woman (not necessarily the actual mother) may look at and touch a female infant's private area only for the necessity of care and hygiene (cleaning impurity, applying medicine), not for affectionate play; for a boy, viewing his private area is permitted more broadly until he reaches the age of discernment (tamyiz), per Ibn Hajar, though al-Ramli and al-Khatib prohibit it even before discernment. The weak hadiths about the Prophet playing with and kissing Hasan's and Husayn's private parts were noted as inconclusive either way (not strong enough to establish a ruling, whether to permit or forbid).
  • The age/onset of when a girl becomes "desired" (and thus subject to viewing restrictions) is not fixed strictly by a set age but by whether she has reached a build/appearance that would arouse desire in people of sound temperament; a disfigurement or illness delaying that threshold delays the ruling accordingly, and conversely early physical development (build, attractiveness) can bring a girl under the ruling earlier than a fixed age.
  • On requiring young girls to wear hijab: this is a matter of prudent upbringing and social custom (with regional practice varying between roughly age 7, 10, or 12, e.g., custom in Tarim ties it to around 12), not a strict fiqh cutoff — the operative fiqh criterion remains whether she has reached the point of being "desired," which can occur earlier or later than any of these customary ages.
  • The correct position on menstrual puberty is completion of 15 lunar years by default; a marginal note claiming average onset by age 20 (cited to argue no contradiction with the practice of annulling a marriage for a wife who hasn't menstruated by 20) was firmly rejected as unsubstantiated and self-contradictory — since if 20 is merely "average," failing to menstruate by then would not itself be a defect.
  • On dress/adornment: displaying one's zeenah (adornment — nice clothing, generally acceptable grooming) is distinguished from displaying mafatin (alluring features — underarm, chest, or lifting a garment to reveal the leg near the knee), the latter being impermissible as it provokes desire; ordinary event make-up falls under permissible adornment, though applying bridal-style decorative markings to unmarried girls before marriage was described as locally frowned upon.

Referenced scholars / texts

Ibn Hajar al-Haytami (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj), al-Ramli, al-Khatib al-Shirbini, al-Adhra'i, al-Mawardi, al-Nawawi (Rawdat al-Talibin / Sharh al-Muhadhdhab draft), Ya'qut al-Nafis, al-Bajuri.

2. Fiqh Academy Resolutions

Sukuk

The symposium judged the question of sukuk needed further study and deferred it to the next gathering. In discussion, sukuk were distinguished from conventional bonds (sanadat, which are interest-bearing debt and prohibited) and from company shares (ashum): sukuk represent ownership in a specific asset or project (e.g., a leased property) rather than in a whole company, so unlike shares they don't carry governance/voting rights, and tradeable sukuk must be asset/usufruct-based (e.g., ijara or musharaka) rather than murabaha-based (which, being debt, may only be transferred at face value).

International "Women's Protection" Recommendations

Reading a resolution responding to international (UN/CEDAW-style) draft recommendations — equal divorce rights and court-only divorce, banning polygamy while permitting non-marital relationships, equal inheritance, shared maternal guardianship, uniform marriage age of 18, criminalizing non-consensual marital relations as rape, and rights to contraception/abortion — the assembly characterized these as conflicting with the natural order (fitra) and nearly every religious tradition, called on the Indian government not to sign such recommendations (citing constitutional religious-freedom protections), and offered its own counter-recommendations: address root causes of sexual crime (alcohol, drugs, indecent media) rather than only penalties, promote modesty in dress, encourage single-sex education, strengthen inheritance and anti-harassment enforcement, and avoid changing marriage-age law in a way that conflicts with Shariah norms. Extensive audience discussion followed on whether Muslims should participate in international forums whose recommendations are non-binding but reputationally significant, with a comparison drawn to Syria's post-liberation government joining an anti-terrorism coalition with the US — accepting practical coordination and having many of its officials' names removed from terrorism watchlists — despite the underlying compromises such coalitions can create.

3. Q&A

  • Nail/hand adornment when going out, based on the hadith of Umm Sinan: rejected as a misapplied hadith, since the narration concerned an elderly woman among other women in a private setting, not general public adornment — this doesn't override the explicit Qur'anic distinction between zeenah (permitted, modest) and mafatin (forbidden), nor the verse commending "elderly women past marriage" for choosing modesty ("though to be modest is better for them").
  • Terminology check: whether "musawwadat sharh al-Muhadhdhab" refers to the extant Majmu' or an unfinished, longer draft of al-Nawawi's commentary — left open pending further checking.
  • Eyeglasses during ihram: no clear-cut ruling either way — glasses (prescription or sunglasses) rest on the nose without fully contacting/covering the face in the customary sense, so the default (no clear prohibition established) permits wearing them cautiously.
  • Perfume around ihram: scenting the body before entering ihram is the sunnah and any lingering scent afterward is not held against the pilgrim; the same latitude was extended informally to scented garments already worn before ihram.
