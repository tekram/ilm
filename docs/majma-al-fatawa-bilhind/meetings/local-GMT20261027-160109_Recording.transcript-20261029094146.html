Arkan al-Nikah: Offer & Acceptance Wording (Tuhfa) + Children's Rights Resolution (Fatawa al-Hindi) - October 27, 2026

📅 2026-10-27 🕐 16:01

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, Fasl fi Arkan al-Nikah wa Tawabi'iha): the class begins the section on the pillars of marriage (four: the two spouses, the guardian, two witnesses, and the wording) and starts with the wording (sigha): the offer (ijab) and acceptance (qabul), what exact wordings are valid, present-tense wording, the need to refer to the bride, whether mispronunciation by the layperson or the learned harms, and whether the husband's acceptance may precede the offer. Before the lesson the teacher also corrects an earlier answer on a conditional divorce wording. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Hindi): the class reads Resolution 102 (2/24) of the 24th seminar of the Islamic Fiqh Academy of India on the rights of children in Islam: custody, education, early marriage, child labor, discipline, juvenile detention, and care of the needy and disabled child.

A Correction Before the Lesson: "If You Go, You Are Three"

A question had come the previous week about a man who told his wife "if you go (out), you are (divorced) by three," asking whether it was void speech or an implicit divorce (kinaya). The teacher had answered from 'Umdat al-Mufti wa'l-Mustafti that it is void; he then found the commentator's note on the next page saying it is a kinaya, and checked al-Tuhfa and al-Nihaya: it is not void but an implicit divorce, whether conditional or immediate, with the number stated clearly, so if he intended divorce, a triple divorce takes effect (the teacher explained the implied expression "you are [divorced], by three"). He mentioned this to avoid the error in future, noting the lesson that one must not close a book after the first page.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah

1. Pillars of Marriage and the Wording

Background: the pillars are four: the spouses, the guardian, two witnesses, and the wording. The author starts with the wording because of the extent of disagreement about it, which requires a long discussion.

The offer (ijab): marriage is valid only by an offer, even from someone who speaks another language. The wording: the contracting party (the bride's guardian or his deputy) says "zawwajtuka" or "ankahtuka" ("I have married you") "my ward so-and-so", naming or indicating her by pointing.

2. Present Tense in the Offer

  • Some said "uzawwijuka" ("I will marry you") is valid when it is stripped of the meaning of a promise. Ibn Hajar queries this: the sound view is that the rule from the end of the chapter on guarantee applies here (a "promise" to pay is not a guarantee), and given the extra caution in marriage, a promise should not be tolerated at all. He then saw that al-Bulqini rejected it outright but accepted it if the promise meaning was removed by saying "now," which supports his view.
  • The teacher explained the position as: if accompanied by an explicit indication (such as "now") it is valid; otherwise not. He also asked whether a situation (guests present for the wedding) counts as an indicator and said probably not, since the wording needs a verbal indicator, as the example "now" shows; and he noted the real custom in Tarim for centuries: the one leading the contract first says "uzawwijuka" with the blessing formula ("according to what Allah commanded: keeping with kindness or releasing with grace") then "zawwajtuka," where the latter is what makes it binding and the first is a promise.

3. The Acceptance (Qabul)

  • The husband (or his deputy) says "tazawwajtuha" or "nakahtuha" ("I married her"): it must include something referring to her: a name, pronoun or pointing. Just "tazawwajtu" is not enough (the teacher added that in the previous Q&A the author is using the form with the pronoun, or her name: "I married so-and-so").
  • Or "qabiltu nikahaha / tazwijaha," "radiitu nikahaha / tazwijaha." The meaning of "qabiltu nikahaha" is "I accept her being married to me" (matching the offer), since "marriage" itself is the combination of offer and acceptance; the teacher noted al-Bajuri's remark that "'aqd al-nikah" is redundant for the same reason. 'Ali said "radiitu nikahaha" at his marriage to Fatima. A claim that the reference could be to an earlier marriage is dismissed since the context makes plain it is the acceptance of what was offered.
  • Text: "wherever it says the husband accepts what the wali or his deputy offered, it is understood to be what was offered to him." On vocalization the class noted that the active reading ("awjaba") is preferred over the passive ("ujiba").
  • Not enough: "qabiltu" alone, or "qabiltuha," or "qabiltuhu" alone, except in the case of the intermediary (mutawassit), which is not relied upon. Direct address is not required: if the husband's deputy says "marry off your daughter" and the guardian says "I married her" (it is agreed address is not required in the intermediary case), but some insist he must say "zawwajtuhu" or "zawwajtuha," then he says "I accept her marriage." The teacher said one who says "I married" with nothing to refer to it is a statement of a report (as in al-Baghawi's note), and that is why the pronoun is needed; whereas in sales "yes" suffices.
  • The "or" in the text gives free choice between the accepted wordings; the two wordings need not match. A suggestion that "qabiltu" should come first as the "real" acceptance is rejected: all are real acceptance by the Shari'a, and the order may serve a point of emphasis.
  • Contested wording: "tazawwajtu" or "nakahtu" alone might be a report; Ibn Hajar notes the sounder view requires no more than the pronoun and that a statement is treated as a performative in the Shari'a.

4. Pronunciation Errors

  • It does not harm for a layperson to open the ta' of the first person ("zawwajtu" with fatha instead of damma), to swap zay and jim ("jawwaztuka" for "zawwajtuka," as in the dialect where "jawz" means "spouse"), or to turn kaf into hamza ("ankahtuka" as "anhatu'"); also, in some early fatwas, "ankahtuk" as in some Yemeni dialects.
  • Al-Ghazali: the mistake of "zawwajtu lak" or "ilayk" does not harm if the meaning is clear, like a grammatical error or one of gender. Abu Shukayl: opening the ta' is a solecism that does not harm the meaning, so the expression does not leave its sense; and Ibn al-Sharaf said that in the local custom, if the meaning is understood it is valid even from someone who knows better.
  • Dispute: one note says it harms from the knowledgeable (Ibn Hajar, favored by Shabramallisi), against al-Ramli who says "even from one who knows"; the Khatib leans to the former. The teacher reconciled them through al-'Abbadi/Sayyid 'Umar's gloss: it does not harm the layperson wherever it is not his language, and it does not harm the learned where it is the local usage or his language (Egyptian doctors who say "z" for "dh" are an example); so there is no real dispute.
  • This differs from prayer, where a pronunciation that changes the meaning matters, since in contracts the criterion is common usage. Those who infer from al-Ghazali's tolerance of gender mistakes (as in divorce, slander and manumission) that opening the ta' is acceptable overlook that "zawwajtuki" (feminine) for the husband is also fine.
  • Dialect asides: the teacher and students discussed Levantine and Egyptian uses such as "ilak" and the added "li" before an object.

5. Husband's Acceptance Before the Offer

It is valid for the husband's or his deputy's wording to come first ("qabiltu nikaha ibnatika fulana," then the guardian says "zawwajtuka," with a pronoun), as the two scholars (al-Nawawi and al-Rafi'i) said, against those who distinguished. The claim that an acceptance cannot precede the offer is rejected, since it can mean "I accept what will come from you," and the past tense is used for the future to show confidence in it, which is common in language and custom. The teacher stressed that the bride must be specified: "qabiltu" alone with no reference is not valid; if only one daughter is meant and both know, it is valid. The question of a father with several daughters ("I marry you one of them," or a different name than hers) is left to the next lesson.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi (Islamic Fiqh Academy of India)

Resolution 102 (2/24) — The rights of children in Islam

(24th Fiqh Seminar, March 2015.)

Custody (hadana): obligatory by Shari'a and primarily the mother's. If she is absent, one woman qualified for it holds an individual obligation; if several are qualified, a collective one. The interests of the child and the custodian are both considered. The mother is not forced in ordinary cases but is if no one else exists and the child is at risk, and then she is entitled to payment (the teacher: if a volunteer exists, the volunteer takes it unless the father agrees to pay). The boy remains in her custody until he can manage his basic needs (eating, drinking, cleaning himself); the seminar estimates age 7 for boys and until puberty or close to it for girls. The teacher noted this varies by person and that the criterion is the child's ability, not understanding of speech. The custodian must be sane, adult, trustworthy and capable, and if a woman, not married to a non-mahram; if she marries one who has no right of custody, her custody lapses and passes to her mother, but marrying a relative with a right (such as the child's cousin) does not drop it. Custody lapses where harm is feared for education, upbringing, body or psyche.

Education: parents and guardians must provide the education enabling the child to perform the religious duties, from the child's property if any, otherwise from the supporter. The teacher added that on the school's view modern education and technology are not obligatory; the seminar adds "as needed, within Shari'a limits," which returns to necessity. He mentioned a previous question of whether a guardian must hire someone to help the child with ablution: it was concluded not obligatory beyond maintenance. Only the religious duties (the obligations of prayer, knowing Allah and the Prophet, and al-Fatiha) are required; Arabic and the rest of the Qur'an are not. He noted that a judge fixing support for state schooling (clothing, books) means the school is treated as necessary in practice, and if the state makes schooling compulsory, free of anything contrary to Islam and affordable, it must be followed. The resolution says that sex education of the kind some quarters demand has no place in Islam, and moral education should be given early.

Marriage: marriage after puberty should not be delayed excessively since delay brings bodily, spiritual and social harm; early marriage before puberty is permitted for some interests but after puberty is better and preferred. The teacher recalled a teacher saying delay affects even an upright, chaste person physically, and that in earlier generations in Hadramawt males married young (even at 14 in some accounts), though generally girls did; delay then was tied to financial ability.

Child labor: Islam does not view child labor favorably and they deserve pity; they should be educated and raised according to ability and given proper chances of growth. Parents and guardians may give them tolerable work that helps in upbringing and learning life skills or vocational work useful for their future. The teacher added that work is good even for the children of the wealthy, since otherwise they grow lazy. It is the government's responsibility to support fathers suffering economic decline and issue grants; the teacher said this is not limited to fathers and that such fathers would be eligible for Zakat.

Discipline and punishment: a child before puberty is not subject to the hudud or retaliation for theft, killing or zina; he is disciplined. Parents, guardians and teachers may discipline but not with cruel or painful punishment, within Shari'a limits. The teacher: the beating must be light, for discipline and not vengeance, and not three strokes unless needed (the relied-upon view: not fixed, only as needed), and never severe (breaking a bone, drawing blood). He cited verses from the Maliki school (by 'Abd Allah b. al-Mukhtar) that a teacher who exceeds three strokes is punished for the excess, noting that for the Shafi'is this is ta'zir, not retaliation.

Juvenile detention: children may be detained in juvenile facilities but not treated harshly, not given the severest punishment or exhausting work, and they should be educated in prison, since the aim is to discipline.

Care of needy children: responsibility for the custody, upbringing and education of destitute children falls first on relatives, then the government, then society (the Muslims at large). The teacher said this order is not the Shafi'i position: for the Shafi'is it falls on ascendants and descendants only, then public funds, then the Muslims; the Hanafis' circle of relatives is wider (extending to heirs). The state and society should not cut ties with a child once entrusted to others, since they might be exploited.

Disabled children: parents and guardians should watch over physically or mentally disabled children, at home and in hospital in emergencies, treat them as far as possible, be patient, and hope for reward. The teacher added that teaching such children the Qur'an is a good way to offset their disability and win people's respect.
