Kitāb al-Waṣiyya: Executor Buying Damaged Property & Symposium Rulings on Humor, Tourism, Sports - July 28, 2026

📅 2026-07-28 🕐 15:57

Overview

The session opens Kitāb al-Waṣiyya (the chapter on bequests). A single long fatwa consumes the first half: an executor (waṣī) instructed to buy real estate with 500 riyāl for endowment instead bought a dilapidated jointly-owned property, then demolished and rebuilt it, then died before completing the endowment; his son continued the work and now the property is worth 1,100 riyāl (600 and 500 for the two houses). The Shaykh works through the jaʿāla-like structure to conclude that the property belongs to the waṣī and prescribes a clean path forward. The session then returns to the Sixth Indian Fiqh Symposium's resolutions on Entertainment and Tourism (Resolution 88), covering humor (muzāḥ), tourism, sports (with detailed Shāfiʿī corrections on prize-money and the modern athletic-career case), and closing recommendations on integrating sports with education.

1. The Executor Who Bought a Damaged Property for the Bequest

Background / Question

A man makes his brother his executor (waṣī) with 500 French-silver riyāl, instructing him to purchase property and real estate with this sum, endow it (yuqifah), and disburse its yield to the poor and the needy — with an option to allot half the yield to students of religious learning residing in the town and half to the poor. The executor had two dilapidated houses jointly owned 50/50 with the muwaṣṣī. The executor bought the muwaṣṣī's half from the heirs "using the aforementioned dirhams — with some of them", intending to demolish and rebuild them into two proper houses, one of which would be set aside for the endowment. Before completing this, the executor died. His eldest son then took over — obtained rushd (competency/authorization) from the town's judge, completed the demolition and rebuilding, and now the two houses are valued in the market at 600 riyāl and 500 riyāl respectively. The questions:

  • Is this arrangement (the executor's initial purchase of the deceased's half from the heirs, then the demolition and rebuilding) valid?
  • If invalid, what happens to the houses and to the 500?
  • If valid, may the son use a qurʿa (lot) to determine which house goes to the endowment — with adjustments so the smaller house-plus-100 riyāl matches the value of the larger?
  • If the lot brings the larger house to the endowment, may he sell half of it on ʿuhda (with recourse) to make up the difference?

Ruling

The Shaykh clarifies (in dialogue with a student) that the muwaṣṣī did not have two houses; he shared one house (in shuyūʿ — undivided joint ownership) with his brother. The brother-executor bought out the deceased's half from the heirs, then demolished the whole and rebuilt as two houses. Now: is this purchase valid?

  • The executor's purchase is void as an act on behalf of the bequest, regardless of whether it was made:
  • with the very cash of the estate (bi-ʿayn māl al-tarika) — this variant is rare — the sale is bāṭil;
  • or on the executor's own dhimma (the usual case) — the sale is valid as a personal purchase for himself, and the property belongs to the executor, not to the estate.
  • Reason: the executor's absolute charge was to buy property — the unqualified command carries the presumption of soundness (salāma). Damage (kharāb) to a house is a ʿayb (defect). This mirrors the rule that when someone appoints an agent (wakīl) to buy a specific thing, the agent must buy sound property. The purchase of a damaged property by the executor is a muqhālafat al-muwaṣṣī.
  • From the Tuḥfa: if a bequest reads "buy a sheep and give it," the absolute word covers small, large, sound, or defective; but where the absolute is a command — "buy a sheep for the poor" — the presumption of soundness kicks in, exactly as in wakāla.
  • Reinforcing point: buying with fewer dirhams than the executor was directed to spend also violates the specific 500-riyāl instruction. From al-Qalāʾid on the parallel Buy Zayd's slave for 1,000 and free him case:
  • If the executor buys Zayd's slave for 500 and frees him:
  • If the slave's value is 1,000 → remainder (500) to the heirs.
  • If value is 500 → remainder to Zayd as muḥābāh (intended favor).
  • If between → excess above value to Zayd.
  • Same principle applies to "perform Hajj on my behalf for 1,000" and the executor engages someone for 500 (unbeknownst to the muwaṣṣā-lahu).
  • Contrast: order to buy an unspecified slave for 1,000: if a 1,000-worth slave is available and executor buys a lesser one, the purchase is invalid for the bequest and the slave becomes the executor's own; if no 1,000-worth slave is available, the executor buys the best available.
  • Applied to the present case: the executor's purchase for the waṣiyya is not valid. The two houses belong to him (or to his heirs) since the dhimma purchase carries into personal ownership. He remains liable for the 500 — it must still be spent as directed.
  • He cannot purchase from himself, even through an agent (a wakīl is legally the same person). The clean solution:
  • Way out (makhraj): the ḥākim (or his deputy) formally buys the smaller house from all heirs of both the waṣī and the muwaṣṣī with the 500. Because we do not know whether the original acquisition was with the estate's cash or on the dhimma, this purchase — from all heirs of both parties — clears every possible claim and cleanly effects the endowment.

Referenced scholars / texts

Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī (Tuḥfa); al-Qalāʾid on the parallel slave and Hajj-by-proxy cases; the rule of aḥkām al-tawkīl.

2. Sixth Indian Fiqh Symposium: Resolution 88 on Recreation and Tourism (Continued)

Background / Question

Continuing from prior weeks: this session covers the Symposium's resolutions on humor (muzāḥ), tourism, and sports. Photography, caricature, and dramatic representation had been covered previously.

Ruling — Humor (Muzāḥ)

  • Humor is permitted when free of lying, obscenity, mockery, and injury.
  • Humor programs that damage religious or worldly interests — whether in prose or verse — are impermissible.
  • It is not fitting for a Muslim to make his livelihood from jokes and levity (spoken or written).
  • Programs constructed for pure diversion and boisterous laughter are inconsistent with the character of the sharīʿa, except where they are used to treat the ill.
  • Anecdote: at a family wedding a comedian was hired for entertainment; a Yemeni relative who had been following his country's crises said afterwards, "May Allah reward you — the laughter released everything I was carrying about the situation in Yemen." The Shaykh treats this as an example within the permissible margin: within a limited stretch of humor amid a legitimate gathering.
  • From Muḥarrāmāt al-Lisān: excessive humor is included among the sins of the tongue, because it "lā yuʾminu ʿādatan an tanshaʾ ʿanhā fitan" — one cannot guarantee it will not lead to fitna and the loss of gravitas. Small amounts, aimed at softening a moment, are mandūb. Between the two — not for softening, just idle joking — is at least makrūh and madhmūm sharʿan.

Ruling — Tourism (Siyāḥa)

  • Travel between cities or countries for recreational purposes is permitted, with avoidance of extravagance (isrāf, tabdhīr).
  • Sites containing serious dangers to life or dignity (mountain climbing without safety gear, hazardous valleys) may not be visited alone or with family unless proper safety measures are taken.
  • Places where unlawful acts are rampant may not be visited for mere recreation — but travel remains permitted when the visit has a legitimate purpose (studying the land, its nature, or another matter that would be lost if not undertaken, without significant customary alternative).
  • No difference between Muslim and non-Muslim countries in this ruling: the rule attaches to the presence of unlawful acts, not to the identity of the state.
  • The Shaykh's threshold: it is not enough that a place have some maʿṣiya; the person's presence must not be construed as being of that group in customary understanding. Sitting far away from the unlawful acts, so that one is not customarily associated with them, is acceptable.
  • Modern application to beaches: the same test applies. If a family goes to a section customarily attended by those doing what is impermissible, and the person is customarily associated with them or would be forced to see what is impermissible, it becomes forbidden. If they can be at a location distanced from the maʿṣiya — where they neither see it nor are associated with it — it is permissible.
  • Providing services (transport at fare, food and drink stalls) at such sites for those who wish to go is not itself iʿāna ʿalā l-maʿṣiya; but the Shaykh notes the mixed reality where "vendors" at leisure sites are effectively tourists themselves in disguise.
  • Travel companies for lawful purposes are permitted; travel that strengthens religious and cultural links, preserves the maʿāthir of the salaf, and provides historical/religious information is encouraged.

Ruling — Sports (Alʿāb)

  • Physical sports that benefit humanity — strengthening the body, sharpening the mind, adding vitality — are permitted so long as they:
  • Are free of munkarāt sharīʿa (impermissible acts);
  • Do not distract from religious or worldly duties;
  • Do not harm animate creatures.
  • Face-punching sports are prohibited even when the athlete wears protective gear, unless the gear ensures no impact reaches the face at all.
  • Modesty rules must be observed during play, for men and women alike, as in ordinary life.
  • Encouraged sports: those the ḥadīth encouraged — ramy (archery / marksmanship), jary (running), sabq al-khayl wa-l-ibil (horse and camel racing) — because they train for combat and can be pressed into meritorious service (rescue, protection).
  • Prize money (ʿiwaḍ):
  • The Symposium's Ḥanafī-leaning position: "prize money on victory or defeat is permitted if it comes from one of the contestants or from an outsider, but not from all the contestants." (This is the Ḥanafī view.)
  • The relied-upon Shāfiʿī position (the Shaykh's correction): "no sabq except in a khuff, ḥāfir, or naṣl" — i.e., prize money is valid only in the sports of preparation for war (mounts, arrows, spears, and modern equivalents). Outside these, prize money — from any source — is not permitted.
  • Anecdote from a Kulliya student: a group had a rule that the loser walks around the college. The Shaykh's view: this is caught by the same rule — the "prize" for the winner is exemption from the walk, so it is a compensated contest outside the sharʿī categories, thus impermissible. If the walking is merely a waʿd (promise) between them, and neither considers it a binding condition, then it is fine.
  • Chess: subject to karāha because of the disagreement over its permissibility. Three conditions for playing chess in the Shāfiʿī school:

1. Not with someone who believes it forbidden;

2. No money staked from either side (stated explicitly in al-Asnā);

3. Not leading to loss of prayer.

  • From Jordan's Dār al-Iftāʾ (read into the record): the same conclusion — the jumhūr of Shāfiʿī scholars, and the muʿtamad of the four madhāhib, prohibits taking prize money on any sport not on the war-preparation list. al-Jumal is quoted on the definition of sabq: contests over things prepared for combat (mounts, arrows, spears, stones by hand or sling). Some Mālikīs allow prize money in non-war-preparation contests if it comes from a non-participant.
  • Wagering by non-participants on match outcomes is maysir — clear ḥarām.
  • Tickets for permitted matches: purchasing tickets to watch a permitted match is permissible if the payment is genuine payment for admission.
  • Sports careers:
  • Sports may only be a temporary recreation. They may not be one's life-goal.
  • It is not fitting for a Muslim to devote his life exclusively to sports, neglecting education and lawful earning.
  • The Shaykh's Shāfiʿī extension: employing professional athletes — paying them salaries to play — is not permitted, because the "employer" receives no manfaʿa the sharīʿa recognizes. The manifest benefit accrues to the athlete himself (his skill, his health, his fame). What the "employer" gains — the entertainment of spectators, national pride in a team's victory — is not recognized as manfaʿa to which a fee may be attached under the Shāfiʿī understanding of ijāra.
  • The subject is left open for continued discussion the next session, since the counterexample of revenue-generating leagues (Egypt–Jordan matches, television rights, ticket sales) also deserves engagement.

Ruling — Time and Priorities (Concluding Recommendations of Resolution 88)

  • Time is the capital of life. Any sport that consumes the time of players or spectators is not free of karāha, even if it avoids explicit prohibitions of dress or conduct.
  • The Symposium urges the Indian government to be cautious about heavily promoting sports competitions in ways that channel creative youth talents away from national development.
  • Sports competitions should be avoided during periods when students are preparing for examinations, to avoid setbacks; competition times should be limited so as not to waste the time of enthusiasts.

Referenced scholars / texts

The Sixth Indian Fiqh Symposium (Resolution 88 on Recreation and Tourism); Muḥarrāmāt al-Lisān; the ḥadīth lā sabaqa illā fī khuffin aw ḥāfirin aw naṣl; al-Jumal on the definition of sabq; al-Asnā on the conditions for chess; Jordan's Dār al-Iftāʾ contemporary fatwa summary (read in as illustration); the fatwa of Shaykh Sālim (unpublished, as noted by the Shaykh) on card games as prohibited (qimār).
