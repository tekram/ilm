Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, the marriage wording and its conditions): the class covers the invalidity of temporary marriage (nikah al-mut'a) and whether "for the length of your life" is temporary, then nikah al-shighar (exchange marriage) with its reasons and the cases that differ, then the requirement that the husband know the bride is lawful for him, how the bride must be identified, and what is required of guardian, bride and husband (including no state of ihram). The class stops before the witnesses. The class ends with a short discussion of when an angry man's divorce does not count.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah

1. Temporary Marriage (Nikah al-Mut'a)

Ruling: a marriage limited to a known or unknown period is void, because of the prohibition. It was first permitted as a concession for the needy, then forbidden at Khaybar, permitted again in the year of the Conquest (some say the Farewell Pilgrimage), then forbidden permanently by an explicit text.

  • Ibn 'Abbas held it remained lawful (and Ibn al-Zubayr threatened to stone him over it). The teacher said it is not permitted to follow him, as the view is anomalous and contrary to all the scholars. But no hadd is applied for the doubt he created, and the report that Ibn 'Abbas retracted is not established. Some of the Salaf agreed with him that it is lawful but held that the rulings of marriage do not attach to it.
  • Under the school (as in Fath al-Mu'in), if it is done with a guardian and two witnesses, the dowry, lineage and 'idda attach to it, though no inheritance between the pair. Where the hadd applies, none of dowry, lineage or the like attach. Al-Zarkashi disputed the claim of consensus, saying the disagreement was real.
  • "For the duration of your life" or "of mine": al-Bulqini held it valid, as it merely states what is true of every marriage. Ibn Hajar rejects it since death does not remove all marriage effects (the text's statement that it is not "temporary" if so worded was queried in class, with the teacher noting the wording needs the opposite sense: the text says it is "not temporary" because its effects remain after death). Fath al-Mu'in adopted al-Bulqini; Ibn Hajar and most did not.
  • Ibn Hajar compares it with "I gave you this to use for the duration of your life" in gifts, where the criterion is the soundness of the hadith, and notes the extra caution here. Zufar (Hanafi) held the marriage valid with the time limit voided.

2. Exchange Marriage (Nikah al-Shighar)

Ruling: invalid, per the hadith in the two Sahihs; named from "shaghara al-kalb rijlahu" (the dog raising its leg to urinate: "do not lift my daughter's leg until I lift yours"), or from a town that is "shaghir" (empty) of its ruler. The definition at the end of the hadith (whether from the Prophet or from Ibn 'Umar or Nafi') is: "I marry you my daughter on condition that you marry me your daughter (or my son), each one's private part being the dowry of the other," and the other accepts: "I married and I marry you."

  • Reason for invalidity: shared interest in the private part, since each makes his ward's private part the subject of the marriage and the other's dowry, as if marrying her to two men. Al-Rafi'i objected, and others gave other reasons (conditionality, no dowry), which Imam al-Haramayn weakened, so the ruling rests on the hadith and is a matter of worship.
  • If the private part is not made the dowry, and he only says "I marry you my daughter on condition you marry me your daughter," the sounder view is both marriages are valid with the dowry of the like, since there is no sharing. A condition of a contract inside a contract does not void marriage (unlike sales). The teacher said in gifts, vows and the like a stated condition harms but an implied one does not, by the analogy of the gift. The text's "on condition you marry me" counts as a request in place of "marry me," otherwise acceptance would be needed.
  • If the private part is the dowry of only one: only that one is void: "I marry you [my daughter] on condition you marry me your daughter, and your daughter's private part is the dowry of my daughter": the first is valid; in the reverse, only the first is void (as explained in the class with examples).
  • If both name money as well as making the private part the dowry ("and a thousand as the dowry of the other"), it is void on the sounder view since the sharing remains.
  • If the groom only says "I married your daughter" or the guardian only says "I married you my daughter" without the other part, the first case is probably void (no offer) and the second valid.

3. Knowledge of Lawfulness and Identifying the Bride

  • The husband must know (here: think) the woman is lawful for him; if he is unsure (e.g. she may be a relative), the marriage is not valid, as a precaution. Ibn Hajar addresses an apparent contradiction with the marriage of a missing man's wife who marries thinking him dead, or a man who marries thinking his relative alive and he is dead: the knowledge is a condition for undertaking the contract, not for validity in reality; if the conditions are in fact present the marriage is valid inwardly, though judged invalid outwardly and the man sinful. The difference from a brother marrying off his sister while unsure she is an adult is that doubt there concerns guardianship, whereas the lawfulness of the bride must be established. Ibn Hajar says what he gave is the sounder, since not knowing the husband's death is even clearer than not knowing 'idda has ended, and they still validate it if he turns out dead.
  • Guardian: must not be a slave, minor, woman or hermaphrodite (or other conditions to come). Bride: free of marriage and 'idda, and not wholly unknown.
  • Identifying her: al-Mutawalli said the way is knowing her name and lineage or seeing her; "I marry you this one" while she is veiled or behind a screen and the husband knows neither her face, name nor lineage is void, as testimony cannot be borne on her. Al-Adhra'i and al-Zarkashi qualify this: the earlier scholars' statement "if he pointed to a bride present: valid" and "the one in the house" applies where he knows her lineage or identity; so there is no real conflict with the scholars who allowed pointing. Ibn al-'Imad held the witnesses need not know her, since their duty is to be present and record the wording; the ignorance of the husband renders the contract pointless, whereas the witnesses' ignorance leaves its use.
  • Ibn Hajar's conclusion: whenever it is known that she is the one pointed to at the contract, the marriage is sound, even after the contract session; otherwise not. (Al-Ramli differs; the gloss notes it is a disagreement with him.) The teacher said marriage has no "session" as sales do. Al-Mutawalli's statement is read as applying to someone who despairs of ever knowing her.
  • Absent bride: if the guardian is not father or grandfather, her lineage must be given (father's father) to avoid sharing the name; the father alone suffices if no other of the name in town. In the three (husband, guardian, bride) identification is required, except in "one of my daughters," and choice is required (except for the compelled bride), and no ihram (obligatory or voluntary) for any of the three.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi (Islamic Fiqh Academy of India)

The 25th Fiqh Seminar

Held at the Arabic Islamic university Dar al-Hadith (India), 25-27 Rabi' II 1437 (5-7 February 2016), with delegations from Iran, South Africa and elsewhere. Topics: matters concerning the People of the Book, the rights of the elderly and disabled, divorce in anger, unity of the Ummah, and interfaith dialogue, principles and etiquette.

Resolution 106 (1/25) — Matters concerning the People of the Book

1. "People of the Book" is a Qur'anic and hadith term for Jews and Christians, as held by the majority of scholars including later Hanafis. The opinions about the Sabians vary and no single view is chosen. Jews and Christians are People of the Book as long as they claim belief in the Torah and Gospel and their messengers. Atheists, Babis, Baha'is, Sikhs and Qadianis are not People of the Book. The teacher noted that for marriage the school looks to lineage, not just claim, and that the Magians were treated as a People of a Book for some purposes; the seminar's marriage conclusion is different.

2. Though marrying a Kitabiyya is lawful in itself, it is rarely free of problems today and Muslims should avoid it. The teacher mentioned Ibn Hajar's remark that a man strong in knowledge and faith marrying a Kitabiyya is better than marrying a Muslim woman who neglects prayer (to take account of the Hanbali view that one who leaves prayer is a kafir).

3. The status of a book as divinely revealed or a person as a prophet is a matter of belief requiring certain proof; there is none for other religions' scriptures or figures, so one does not say they are revealed or prophets, merely because of similarities.

4. Muslim leaders should set up high-level modern institutions that give religious and moral education with modern education; until these exist, parents may place children in modern secular institutions out of necessity while taking precautions for their faith. The teacher said his own teachers did not forbid even girls from state schools.

5. Kitabiyya wives have the same rights as Muslim wives (housing, maintenance); it is not permitted to withhold them merely because she is a Kitabiyya, though one must avoid what harms one's religion. If she wishes to perform her worship, the resolution says the husband should allow her unless he or the children are harmed; the teacher questioned this: he has no right to authorize disbelief, though he may not prevent her from going to a church.

6. Muslims should be careful in using the services of non-Muslim charities; if a Muslim employee is made to do missionary work, it must be rejected and the service not used, and Muslim organizations should set up alternatives.

Rights of the Elderly and the Disabled

Islam calls for respect and care of the elderly and disabled. The teacher said "fard" here means "ought," since the school does not oblige supporting everyone: one with money supports himself; the wife's maintenance is on her husband if she is not nashiz; poor parents are supported by their children and children may not force them to earn even if they can (this is also the school's view); a son is supported until he can earn something suitable and a father may require him to seek work if it benefits him without harm. Nursing and serving parents is an obligation on both sons and daughters, and a husband should allow his wife to serve her parents if no one else can; children may not stop the father from marrying again, and if he cannot support the new wife the wealthy sons must; they may not demand their inheritance during the parents' lives; and one may not travel for more wealth leaving parents needing care unless they agree and someone looks after them. The resolution calls serving the elderly and appointing a servant obligatory; the teacher repeated that this matches only what they need for essentials, and not, for example, someone to assist with ablution daily, as discussed earlier. Putting parents in a care home without their consent is not permitted; if done in necessity and with consent, the child must visit and care for them. A person may not take government aid for the elderly before reaching the stipulated age.

Divorce in Anger (Talaq al-Ghadban)

Marriage is a solemn covenant and divorce the most hated of lawful things, used only in necessity; a husband must control himself in anger. Divorce in anger takes effect, except where anger reaches the level of madness and he loses mental balance and knows not what he says or does: he is treated as insane and the divorce does not take effect.

  • Proof: if he was known to lose his reason in anger, his oath that he was in that state suffices; if not, witnesses (doctors) are needed. In class: the wife, if she believes him, may take the position that nothing happened, but as the teacher held, if she believes he lost awareness, she may keep silent and not accept him as husband without announcing it; her acceptance does not apply outwardly. The doctors need not have been present: it suffices that their examination shows he was unaware over that period (e.g. ten hours); the condition is only that they be consulted.
  • The teacher noted that this resolution is a disputed one and said it was one of the points he would keep in view.

The class closed with the teacher noting the next topics (unity of the Ummah and interfaith dialogue) were not read.
