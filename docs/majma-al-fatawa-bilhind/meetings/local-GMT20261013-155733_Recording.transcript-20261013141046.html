Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, Fasl al-Khitba): the class continues the section on the engagement proposal: the ban on proposing over another man's proposal and its conditions, what counts as an explicit acceptance, when the ban lifts, a suitor who proposes to several women, being asked for advice about a suitor (and the permitted forms of backbiting), plus a tangent on inheritance co-owners using shared property. Book 2 (Fatawa al-Hindi): the class begins the resolutions of the 24th seminar of the Islamic Fiqh Academy of India (March 1-3, 2015), reading Resolution 101 (1/24) on writing and translating the Qur'an, up to the point on Braille for the blind. A Q&A closes the session: distributing 50 oath expiations, and leaving the prayer for food burning on the stove.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, Fasl al-Khitba

1. Proposing Over a Proposal

Ruling: it is forbidden to propose to a woman who has already been proposed to, if the following conditions are met:

1. He knows of the existing proposal.

2. He knows of the acceptance.

3. The acceptance was explicit.

4. He knows that proposing over a proposal is forbidden.

5. The first suitor is a person whose proposal was permitted (even if disliked), and is "respected" (muhtaram), even a non-Muslim. This excludes a harbi non-Muslim. The teacher left open whether it also excludes someone whose death penalty is mandatory (a married adulterer, one who abandons prayer after being sentenced), concluding it may revert to the basic rule since such a person is not respected.

  • The hadith says "brother" but this is the usual case; the reasons are the harm and the breaking of ties.
  • The teacher noted that a proposal that is disliked (e.g. the proposal of someone in ihram, or one lacking the means or having a defect) still counts, provided the acceptance was explicit; and that acceptance may come from the suitor's own side or his representative.

2. What Counts as Explicit Acceptance

  • By the compelling guardian (mujbir); by a master for his slave woman (not a mukataba); by the ruler for an adult insane woman with no father or grandfather; or by the woman together with the guardian; or by a non-compelled woman alone (in a case with no wali, or a guardian who authorized her); or by a woman who has authorized the guardian to accept, even without naming a man ("marry me to whom you wish"). Ibn Hajar says this is the sound implication of the earlier scholars' words, though some disputed it. That she cannot contract the marriage alone does not stop her answering a proposal alone.
  • The silence of a virgin who is not compelled counts as explicit; the claim that she must say she is not shy is rejected.
  • Wording: "I accept you" (ajabtuka) is explicit. Some held "I am pleased with you as a husband" (radiituka zawjan) is only allusion, but Ibn Hajar says the sounder view is that it is explicit. (The class also spent time on the vocalization of "radiituka" and the habit of marking only long vowels in print.)

3. When the Ban Lifts

  • With the first suitor's permission, given without fear or embarrassment (fear and shyness do not make the forbidden lawful).
  • If the first suitor leaves it, or the woman or her side turns away, or he does, including when a long time passes after the acceptance such that circumstances show he has turned away. The teacher stressed the delay alone is not enough: a suitor busy with intensive study (law, Shari'a sciences, memorizing the Qur'an, medicine) may not have turned away. Also a distant travel with no contact, where "cut off" means cut off from the fiancee, not from all news; the teacher added it was not allowed in the first place to correspond privately with her in a way that resembles khalwa (such as on WhatsApp).
  • If the first suitor was not given an answer, or the answer was only an allusion, or neither acceptance nor rejection was clearly stated, the ban does not apply. Likewise if the acceptance was explicit but the second suitor did not know of it, did not know it was explicit, or did not know it was forbidden; or the proposal was abandoned; or the first man married someone whom it is forbidden to combine with the fiancee; or the first suitor was a harbi or an apostate (an apostate cannot marry).

4. A Man Who Proposes to Several Women

If one proposes to five women, together or in succession, none of them may be proposed to by another man until he turns away from some or contracts with four (the teacher: the same applies to ten or twenty; the point is that they exceed what he can marry). Likewise if a man has three wives and is proposed to by a fourth, or it is known he wants only one, another woman may not propose to him on the conditions above. If he has fewer than four wives and has not shown he wants only one, there is no ban, since combining is possible. The teacher noted this sets aside the harm to the wife, since a man's right to marry more is recognized.

5. The Recommended Proposal

It is recommended to propose to people of merit. This is the starting point for the following.

6. Being Consulted About a Suitor

  • Ruling: whoever is consulted about a suitor, or someone someone wishes to deal with or work with (is he suitable?), must mention his faults truthfully, and also when not asked, just as someone who knows of a defect in goods must tell the buyer. Ibn Hajar says honor is more sacred than property, and the harm is greater here (exposure of the private parts, disgrace); this is stated as obligatory in al-Adhkar, al-Riyad, Sharh Muslim, and by Ibn al-Salah.
  • Faults mentioned: both religious and customary (the latter by analogy with the hadith about Mu'awiya who "has no money"). The teacher said to name the faults most likely to deter, and to be specific only as needed: e.g. if the family does not mind that he does not pray, mentioning it is pointless. A faults list is a necessary measure; what is permitted by necessity is limited to its extent. He noted that customary faults include being already married, which many women reject, even though it is lawful.
  • The Prophet's own advice: when asked about Abu Jahm (he "does not lay down his stick from his shoulder", meaning frequent beating or frequent travel) and Mu'awiya (no money). Ibn Hajar: this does not contradict the rule of stating specifics, because the Prophet knew the woman would accept less. The teacher said the Mu'awiya here is not Mu'awiya b. Abi Sufyan. On the stick: the teacher preferred reading it as travel rather than beating, and added that even a lawful beating of a wife is better left, because the man tends to do it for himself, may exceed the limit, and each excess is counted against him; certainty, not just probability, is needed that the situation permits it. For children, discipline for the sake of the Shari'a is the better case but still within limits. Rule: when torn between punishing and forgiving, forgive.
  • Permitted backbiting: this is one kind of allowed backbiting. The text lists others: complaining to someone who can help, seeking a fatwa (the questioner's and opponent's case, with the mufti named if needed), and exposing someone who openly flaunts sin or innovation (only that sin).
  • If the suitor consults about himself: he must say "I am not suitable for you" if he has faults, and if they accept him anyway, fine; otherwise he must withdraw or disclose what the family would reject, religious or customary. Al-Adhra'i's view that one need not mention faults when the family has not asked is rejected: disclosure is obligatory even without being asked.
  • Is mentioning a high price obligatory in sales? The teacher raised it as a question for the chapter on sales: the text says defect, so a high price is outside it, and the hadith "let people be provided for by one another" suggests it is not allowed to point out. A student reported from his work in the chapter on forbidden sales that the scholars treat the buyer's overpaying as his own fault and give no option, and that their silence is close to a text. The teacher: this fits the rules, and the exact point can be checked.

7. Tangent: Inheritance Co-Owners Using Shared Property

The teacher raised a point from the 'Umdat al-Mufti wa'l-Mustafti, whose compiler's comment (in the Jami' al-Fatawa) says an heir living in a share of the inherited house owes nothing, because the default is freedom from liability. The teacher disputes this as an author's preference rather than the relied-upon view:

  • The known rule: no heir may use any part of the estate without the others' consent, until there is a division or a rotation (muhaya'a) or the judge rents it out. An heir who lives in the house for his fifth share is using what is jointly owned in every part.
  • An old text is cited: if one co-owner abstains from using the shared property without being prevented, he does not claim rent (Sayyid 'Abd al-Rahman b. Sulayman and al-Wa'ili). The compiler argues rent applies only when someone uses the whole house; using only his own share is not liable. The teacher said this is flawed since every part of an undivided house belongs to all the heirs in proportion.
  • A student asked about plots of equal parts being used by one co-owner. The teacher: even partition by separation (qismat al-ifraz) must happen first; using it before is disobedience, and the rent owed is for the share used. The texts of al-Tuhfa say if co-owners refuse rotation the judge compels them or rents it out for a year.
  • The page referenced was p. 99 of the second volume.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi (Islamic Fiqh Academy of India)

The 24th Fiqh Seminar

Held at Dar al-'Ulum Islamiyya, Azirat (India), March 1-3, 2015, chaired by Shaykh Muhammad al-Rabi' al-Hasani al-Nadwi. Scholars attended from across Indian states and from Saudi Arabia, Britain, South Africa, Mauritius and elsewhere. Topics: writing and publishing the Qur'an and its translation, teaching the Qur'an to the blind by Braille, children's rights in Islam, halal and haram rules in food products, standards for halal certification, and donating human organs. Representatives of South African and British halal bodies and a university food-research team presented, as did liver-transplant surgeons and a South African institute for the blind. The Academy also issued a statement on spelling and sign conventions in Indian versus Arab-world printed Qur'ans, and an official statement on attempts to give a sectarian coloring to the Indian education system.

Resolution 101 (1/24) — Writing and translating the Qur'an

1. The Qur'an was sent as the final book of guidance, and since people speak many languages, trustworthy translations should be published in the world's languages. (The teacher: translations of explanation, in various languages.)

2. Publishing a translation of the Qur'an's meanings stripped of the Qur'anic text is not permitted in any language, hence it is not valid to buy, distribute or give it as a gift. The class questioned the reason; the teacher guessed it is to allow verification, and for something like a tafsir without the text, this is their choice and he does not think it forbidden.

3. Writing and printing mushafs is permitted only in the 'Uthmani script (rasm).

Class discussion on non-Muslims and the Qur'an:

  • A student asked about giving a non-Muslim books of Shari'a sciences, including tafsir. The teacher: publishing is one thing; owning is forbidden to a non-Muslim and sale is invalid. Even a loan is not allowed if disrespect is feared. Per Hashiyat 'Abd al-Hamid (Ibn 'Abd al-Salam): a non-Muslim is barred from putting a hand on a mushaf even for binding it, but may be allowed to read it if there is hope for his Islam. The teacher corrected his own earlier statement and concluded: such translations may be given only as access, not ownership; only if his Islam is hoped for; and with a sign he will not disrespect it (ideally in a Muslim's presence). The sale is invalid in all cases.
  • If a non-Muslim copies a mushaf or hadith books, he is ordered to dispose of his ownership. Al-Ramli permitted writing the Qur'an in Indian script (and by analogy Turkish), so non-Muslim ownership of a Qur'an in English translation is the same.
  • A student asked about a booklet of Qur'anic themes rather than a direct translation, and about da'wa letters like the Prophet's to Khosrow. The teacher: if it contains the name of Allah or a great name it may be forbidden; he quoted al-Tuhfa and the glosses: books containing the sayings of the early scholars (even stories of the righteous and names of the prophets) may not be given to disrespectful non-Muslims, and by Hashiyat 'Abd al-Hamid also not to hateful innovators (he named the Rafida and the "Wahhabis," since their mockery of the saints' miracles is worse than the non-Muslims'). A Muslim writing for a dhimmi should not write the name of Allah (the gloss warns this error is common). The teacher then criticized sweeping takfir in some recent fatwas on a statement that Allah is "everywhere."
  • On rasm: the teacher said writing a mushaf in 'Uthmani rasm is clearly preferred (e.g. "zaqqum" with an open ta, "bi-aydin" with two ya's), and cited Shaykh 'Abd al-'Azim al-Zurqani: Malik was asked whether a mushaf should be written in newly introduced spelling, he said no, only the original; al-Dani said no scholar contradicts Malik; Ahmad forbids departing from 'Uthman's mushaf; for the Shafi'is the glosses of al-Manhaj say "riba" is written with waw and alif as in the 'Uthmani rasm; the Hanafis say it ought to be so. The other view, that rasm is conventional rather than fixed, is reported (Ibn Khaldun; al-Baqillani). The teacher: all agree it is preferable; for the Shafi'is it is possibly mandatory. Outside a mushaf, writing the same verse in modern spelling is fine.

4. Every Muslim, man or woman, must learn the Qur'an and qualify to recite it, and teach his children and dependents, or be asked about neglect.

5. The Qur'an is to be printed in Arabic script, but when necessary the Qur'anic text may be printed in other scripts if: the order of the Qur'an is not changed, the articulation points are observed as much as possible, and comprehensive symbols marking the features of the 'Uthmani and Arabic rendering are provided. The teacher read this as transliteration for pronunciation only, so that the reader utters the Arabic word.

6. The blind and disabled deserve the utmost care and sympathy; Braille is a great invention and Muslims must give the blind facilities to advance in Shari'a sciences through it. The teacher said this is also compensation: e.g. his usul teacher Shaykh Mahmud Faraj al-Sayyid Sulayman was blind and had memorized the Qur'an perfectly; engaging the blind with the Qur'an is among the best ways to compensate, instead of leaving them unproductive. The class stopped before item 7.

Q&A

1. Fifty oath expiations (kaffarat yamin): may they be divided among 10 families on one day? Yes. Each recipient family receives for 10 expiations; even in one hour. The teacher: give to the poor and needy only (not those in debt or travelers, since this is not Zakat). Must he intend "a tenth of each expiation to this person"? Not required: he intends them as expiations for these ten. (The teacher compared the Zakat al-Fitr given for himself, wife and children.) If a recipient would exit poverty by getting a share, give only what keeps him poor or needy; the portion beyond may not be valid (partial validity by separation of the deal). Who is "poor/needy" today: look at his earnings. Someone who earns enough daily is not eligible; someone with irregular daily work who cannot ensure it may be given a lifetime's portion (the usual life expectancy of 60 years); someone with a monthly salary that doesn't last may be given at the start of the month, because all his money to age 60 would not cover his need.

2. Breaking the prayer for food burning on the stove: permitted, as it is fear of loss of property, which is among the excuses (like fear for oneself, honor, or property permitting tayammum). The teacher noted it is also an excuse for the Friday prayer and congregation, even though the person is sinful if he caused it to avoid the prayer; the Shari'a dislikes wasting money. He noted a dispute: Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli hold that fear for property is an excuse even if one has no obligation to defend it (like another's crop catching fire), while the Khatib limits it to a living being or a deposit. The ruling for a loaf in the oven is the same, provided he needs it and fears its loss if he does not bake it, and did not intend to skip the prayer.
