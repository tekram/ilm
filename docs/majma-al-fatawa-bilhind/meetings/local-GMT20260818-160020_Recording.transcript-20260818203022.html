Desirable Qualities in a Bride, Fiqh Academy Symposium on Elections & Bay' al-Wafa - August 18, 2026

📅 2026-08-18 🕐 16:00

Overview

This session covers the Shafi'i fiqh of Kitab al-Nikah regarding the qualities recommended in choosing a prospective bride, followed by the resolutions of the 22nd Fiqh Symposium (Nadwa Fiqhiyya) of the Islamic Fiqh Academy — covering political participation/elections, sukuk, and women's protection — and closes with a brief return to the topic of bay' al-wafa (conditional sale with right of redemption).

1. Recommended Qualities in a Prospective Bride: Religiousness (Din)

Background / Question

What is the first and most important quality recommended when choosing a wife, and what exactly does "diyana" (religiousness) mean in this context?

Ruling

  • It is recommended that a prospective bride possess diyana — not merely being Muslim, and not merely chastity from fornication, but full uprightness ('adala): not committing major sins and not persisting in minor ones, such that her good deeds outweigh her transgressions.
  • Basis: the hadith "choose the one with religion, may your hands be in dust" (taribat yadak) — a phrase either meaning "you will be enriched if you comply" or "you will become impoverished if you don't," explained as either a literal or a figurative (antithetical) usage, similar to calling a desert "mafaza" (place of triumph) or a very fair-skinned man "al-Zanji" (the black one).
  • There was scholarly hesitation over whether a Muslim woman who neglects prayer or a scriptuary (Christian/Jewish) woman is preferable. Some said the scriptuary is preferable since her marriage's validity is agreed upon by consensus, while the prayer-neglecting Muslim's status is disputed (the Hanbalis consider her an apostate under certain conditions). However, the relied-upon (mu'tamad) position is that the sinning Muslim woman is absolutely preferable to the scriptuary.
  • Clarification on the Hanbali position: even within the Hanbali school, a woman is not automatically ruled an apostate merely for neglecting prayer — this ruling only applies if a judge/ruler formally commanded her to pray and she still refused. Since this formal process essentially never happens in practice, the marriages of most prayer-neglecting Muslim women today remain valid even by Hanbali standards.
  • Ibn Hajar (al-Haytami) suggested nuance: if the man himself has strong faith and knowledge, the scriptuary may be preferable for him (safer from her being a source of temptation, and there's hope she may embrace Islam), while for others the reverse holds — though the shaykh noted the fatwa relied upon (per Shibra Malisi/others) treats the sinning Muslim woman as preferable absolutely, regardless of the husband's own strength of faith.

2. Second Quality: Virginity (Bikr)

Ruling

  • It is recommended that the bride be a virgin, based on the Prophet's ﷺ instruction along with the reasoning that virgins have "sweeter mouths" (understood either literally — youth generally correlating with fresher breath — or figuratively as "softer speech"), "bear more children" or are "more eagerly receptive" (or both), and "are content with less" (i.e., less demanding, whether regarding intimacy or otherwise), and possess "ghirra" (guilelessness/innocence — meaning less attuned to worldly mischief, unlike a previously-married woman who has more experience of men and is therefore more wary).
  • A previously-married woman (thayyib) is preferable for a man who is not up to managing a virgin, or one who has children needing someone experienced to raise them, as the Prophet ﷺ advised Jabir.
  • From Ihya 'Ulum al-Din: it is recommended not to marry one's virgin daughter except to a man who has never been married before, since people naturally become attached to their first experience — this applies to daughters generally (virgin or previously married), distinguished from the separate recommendation (for the groom) to prefer marrying a virgin.
  • A side discussion clarified that a difference in complexion/beauty related to loss of virginity, while not always perceptible, is sometimes a real, observable effect — partly attributable to virgins generally taking more care with personal grooming and cleanliness than married women.

3. Third Quality: Good Lineage (Nasab) and Avoiding Disreputable Origins

Ruling

  • It is recommended she be of known, reputable lineage (traceable to scholars and righteous people).
  • It is disliked (makruh) to marry the daughter of fornication, the daughter of an open sinner (fasiq), a foundling of unknown parentage, or one whose father is unknown — based on a hadith (graded sound by al-Hakim, though its underlying rationale — that the child will be born frail — was disputed by some, since the Prophet ﷺ himself married 'Ali to Fatima despite a degree of close relation, and the actual concern is only about very close consanguinity, i.e., first-degree maternal/paternal cousins; more distant relations, like Fatima's relation to 'Ali, are not discouraged).
  • The Prophet's ﷺ marriage to Zaynab bint Jahsh, despite her being his paternal cousin, served the specific purpose of establishing the permissibility of marrying a divorced adopted son's wife — explicitly addressed in the Qur'an ("...so that there is no discomfort for believers regarding the wives of their adopted sons when they have fulfilled what they wanted of them...").
  • Discussion of the maxim "beware the greenery of the dunghill" (khadra' al-diman) — a weakly-attested but instructive saying warning against marrying a beautiful woman from a corrupt/disreputable family background.

4. Further Recommended Qualities

Ruling

  • Affectionate (wadud) and fertile (walud) — fertility in a virgin is inferred from her female relatives' childbearing history; a saying is cited that "a fertile dark-skinned woman is better than a barren fair one."
  • Abundant in intellect ('aqlaha wafira) — meaning intelligence exceeding the ordinary threshold required for legal responsibility, though scholars cautioned against a wife whose intelligence significantly exceeds her husband's, since this can lead to her dominating him rather than the reverse (though her greater intelligence can still benefit worldly affairs).
  • Good character (husn al-khuluq) — discussed at length with the observation that harsh language/cursing tends to run in families whose environment normalizes it (e.g., certain trading/mediating households), while in other communities (the shaykh's example: Tarim) such language is essentially never heard; a broader tangent addressed the modern problem, especially in Syria, of casual cursing involving the divine name, which the shaykh attributed to a lack of admonition from religious teachers.
  • Not already having a child from another man, except where there is a clear benefit to doing so anyway (such a woman is called lafut).
  • Beautiful (hasna'), per one's own natural inclination (Ibn Hajar's position) or per common convention/'urf (al-Ramli's position) — since the underlying purpose is chastity (iffah), which requires attraction.
  • However, extreme beauty (jamal bari') is actually disliked, for three reasons: it may lead her to become prideful/vain; it invites the gaze of immoral men; and it exposes her (and by extension her husband) to being followed/stalked or afflicted by the evil eye — though extreme beauty is still given some weight as a tiebreaker between otherwise equal candidates.
  • Modest dowry (khafifat al-mahr).
  • Not fair-skinned in the specific sense of "shaqra" — defined as a reddish complexion that has overtaken (rather than merely blended with) natural fairness, resembling the glow of a burning flame; mere fairness blended with a touch of red is not disliked and is in fact considered one of the best complexions, since it was the Prophet's ﷺ own natural complexion. The concern with the "overtaken" reddish type relates to psychological effects: self-consciousness about one's complexion is observed to correlate with poorer temperament (similar to how conditions like vitiligo/leprosy can cause psychological strain leading to bad temper).
  • A tangential discussion arose about a hadith (of uncertain strength) that a man whose wife has bad character and does not divorce her will have his supplications against her (for her harm/destruction) go unanswered — since he had the option to simply divorce her instead.
  • Not the object of another's unrequited affection (i.e., avoid marrying someone still emotionally attached to a prior suitor, or vice versa).
  • No dispute over her lawfulness to the groom — e.g., no history of fornication or intimacy involving her mother, ascendants, descendants, or uncertainty regarding breastfeeding-based kinship (rida').

4b. Undesirable Types (Based on a Hadith Cited by al-Daylami/al-Khattabi)

Ruling

Four disliked archetypes are named with wordplay on rhyming Arabic terms: al-shahbara (the vulgar/quarrelsome one, described via the association of "blue" with rudeness), al-lahbara (tall but emaciated — height itself is not a defect, but excessive emaciation is), al-nahbara (short and unattractive, or alternatively an elderly woman in decline, or one excessively talkative/wasteful in speech), and al-handara (an elderly woman in decline, or one who talks excessively). If these traits conflict, the order of priority when choosing is: religion (absolutely first), then intellect and good character, then fertility/childbearing capacity, then nobility of lineage, then virginity, then beauty, then whatever seems most beneficial by one's own judgment — though Sayyid 'Umar al-Basri argued beauty should be prioritized higher (even above virginity) since it better serves the underlying purpose of chastity (a husband attracted to his own wife is less likely to look at other women). An anecdote was shared of a family in Egypt where suitors, choosing between a divorced stepmother mistaken for the bride and the actual (virgin) candidate, ended up preferring beauty even knowing one candidate had been previously married — illustrating that in practice, people often weigh beauty even above virginity.

5. Second Topic: Resolutions of the 22nd Fiqh Symposium (Nadwa Fiqhiyya) — India

Background

The 22nd Fiqh Symposium (of an India-based fiqh academy) was held 25–27 Rabi' al-Thani 1434 AH (corresponding to March 9–11, 2013 CE) at the Jamia Islamia Arabia congregational mosque in Amroha. It brought together scholars, muftis, and specialists from across the country as well as some resident scholars from Algeria, South Africa, and Turkey. Three main topics were discussed: election participation, short-term conditional sale contracts (bay' al-wafa'), and sukuk (Islamic bonds) — their sale, purchase, and use for investment — alongside issues submitted by the UN Commission on the Status of Women regarding women's protection.

Resolution No. 92/1/22 — On Election Participation

Ruling (as issued by the symposium)

1. Given the importance of elections in a democratic system, Muslims must exercise this right with care and precision.

2. It is permissible, and commendable, for qualified and competent individuals to nominate themselves for elections.

3. Muslim representation in legislative and decision-making bodies must be secured to protect public interests, though elected members must strive to prevent the passage of any law that contradicts Shari'ah or genuine human welfare.

4. Muslim members must also strive to amend existing laws that conflict with Shari'ah rulings or human welfare.

5. There is no harm in elected members taking an oath of allegiance to the constitution.

6. Muslim participation in elections is a necessity in India and other democratic countries; joining political parties is permissible as long as the party's charter is not built on sectarianism.

7. Muslim women may vote, while observing the relevant Shari'ah rulings (e.g., appropriate conduct/dress).

Discussion: The Local (Hadramawt/Tarim) Tradition of Non-Participation in Elections

  • The shaykh explained that the historic and still-prevailing local practice among the scholarly community (the Sadat/'Alawi tradition) is to abstain entirely from voting and elections, based on a foundational principle of avoiding entanglement in politics (as opposed to religious knowledge and da'wa), traced back to al-Imam al-Faqih al-Muqaddam, who "sheathed the sword" and redirected the community toward Sufism/spiritual pursuit rather than political/military power, reportedly saying he would not even wish political involvement upon his own servants, let alone his descendants.
  • The shaykh personally recounted once voting under a colleague's insistence, seeing his candidate lose, and concluding there was no real benefit — voting participation risks shifting communal focus and energy away from knowledge and da'wa toward electoral mobilization, a pattern the shaykh associated with Wahhabi-oriented groups locally, who reportedly usually win local Hadramawt electoral seats specifically because the traditionalist community abstains (were the traditionalists to participate, they would likely outnumber them).
  • A specific historical anecdote: the well-known Sufi scholar 'Ali al-Habashi reportedly once refused a Yemeni president's offer of political backing during a visit to Dar al-Mustafa (Tarim), explicitly stating they do not participate in politics — illustrating the community's principled distance from state politics, which in turn earned relative freedom to teach without state interference, since remaining apolitical is itself understood as a form of prudent political strategy (the state remains reassured that gatherings of students pose no political threat).
  • A student pressed further, asking whether this is a principled (categorical) position or merely one based on assessing lack of practical benefit (maslaha) at any given time — the shaykh clarified it is fundamentally principled (avoiding politics as inherently unsuited to their path), though it has also historically coincided with an assessment that there was no compelling benefit; if genuine benefit were clearly established in some future context, individual guidance might differ privately even while the community would not publicly call for participation.
  • Broader discussion touched on: the danger of some regimes deliberately including Islamic movements in elections merely to lend legitimacy to their own rule while marginalizing or later suppressing them once participation weakens their outsider standing (citing the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood's post-2011 experience as an example — participation arguably enabled their eventual removal from power, whereas remaining outside the system as observers might have preserved their standing); the view that political power tends to corrupt/alter the convictions of those who hold it over time (citing the late Shaykh Fadlallah's saying that "power changes the minds of its holders"); and the shaykh's assessment, based on firsthand experience in Syria, that certain hardline Salafi/Wahhabi currents there have moderated somewhat after gaining a stake in the new order (post-Assad), partly through inter-scholarly agreements ("charters of unified religious discourse") establishing disciplinary committees under the Ministry of Endowments to hold preachers accountable for extremist rhetoric — described as an unexpected positive development attributed to divine providence rather than deliberate planning, alongside continued concern that some such groups remain fundamentally excommunicative (takfiri) in outlook toward other Muslims, including Sufis, even while currently exercising restraint out of political calculation.
  • A related anecdote concerning the recent funeral of a local shaykh (Ali Salim Bakir Baghaithani) — led in prayer by Sufi-oriented scholars despite the deceased personally leaning Wahhabi in life — was discussed as exposing an inconsistency in strict takfiri positions (if Sufis were truly considered non-Muslim "grave-worshippers," attending their-led funeral prayers would itself be inconsistent), which the shaykh suggested is gradually becoming apparent to wider audiences, tempering perceptions of Wahhabi positions as excessive.

6. Brief Return to Bay' al-Wafa' (Conditional Sale with Right of Redemption)

Ruling (Quick Recap, requested by a student)

  • The Hadrami customary practice of bay' al-wafa' is described as a talfiq (patchwork) between the Shafi'i and Maliki schools: structurally, per the Shafi'i school, it is executed as an unconditional, valid sale (since an explicit stipulated condition — e.g., "I sell to you on condition that you return it to me once I repay the price" — would render the sale invalid/corrupt under pure Shafi'i doctrine).
  • However, per accepted local custom, once the seller offers repayment of the equivalent price, the buyer becomes obligated to return the sold item — this obligation is derived not from stipulating a condition within the sale contract itself (which would void it under the Shafi'i view), but by treating it as a promise binding on the buyer, drawing on the Maliki position that a promise (wa'd) is obligatory to fulfill.
  • Beyond this core borrowing from the Maliki school (obligation to fulfill a promise), further legal details/branches of the doctrine are worked out using Shafi'i principles from the law of pre-emption (shuf'a) and defect-based rescission (radd bi'l-'ayb).
  • Because of the underlying tension/ambiguity in combining doctrines this way, many past scholars, even while permitting the practice out of necessity/dire need (darura/haja), privately still harbored some discomfort with it.
