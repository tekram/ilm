Contraception Timing, Is Marriage Worship, Prophetic Infallibility & Multiple Friday Prayers - August 11, 2026

📅 2026-08-11 🕐 16:00

Overview

This Tuesday session continues Tuhfat al-Muhtaj's opening of Kitab

al-Nikah, focusing on when it is lawful to interrupt an early pregnancy,

followed by an extended treatment of whether marriage itself counts as an

act of worship, a lengthy digression on the Prophet's ﷺ infallibility

triggered by a student's question, and rulings on when marriage becomes

disliked. No Indian Fiqh Academy resolutions were read this particular

week — the session ran long on the Tuhfa material. It closes with a wide

Q&A: family reunion customs, death-anniversary gatherings, and multiple

Friday prayers in one town.

1. Interrupting an Early Pregnancy (Nutfa/'Alaqa Stage)

Background / Question

Once semen/an embryo has settled in the womb, is it permissible to induce

its expulsion at the nutfa (sperm-drop) or 'alaqa (clot) stage, before any

recognizable human form or ensoulment?

Ruling

  • After ensoulment (nafkh al-ruh), termination is prohibited by

unanimous agreement — this case is not in dispute.

  • Before ensoulment, scholars differ: Abu Ishaq al-Marwazi held it

permissible to cause expulsion of the nutfa or 'alaqa (and this was also

reported from Abu Hanifa, with the Hanafis reportedly extending

permissibility further, though not to after ensoulment, which is agreed

prohibited by all). Ibn Hajar al-Haytami treats it as prohibited once the

matter has "settled in the womb," since it is already progressing toward

the form suited for ensoulment — comparing it unlike coitus interruptus

('azl), which precedes any implantation.

  • A tension was noted between two of Ibn Hajar's own formulations —one

ties the prohibition to mere settling in the womb, another (in the

chapter on blood-money for a fetus, ghurra) ties it to the embryo

having "begun to take shape" — resolved by treating the second, more

specific/qualified wording as governing the first, more general one:

the correct position is prohibition once implantation and the beginning

stages of formation have occurred, which in practice follows shortly

after implantation.

  • Al-Ramli held termination is not prohibited until ensoulment itself,

which he applied without restriction to necessity — though the class

agreed this Shafi'i-school view about coitus/family planning differs from

a separate ruling (in the chapter on blood-money, ghurra) that

liability (a slave's value in kaffara terms, or blood-money) attaches to

destroying a fetus once physical form has begun to appear (head, limbs)

even before ensoulment — suggesting some scholars would still hold a

woman liable for expiation in that case if she acted without genuine need,

though not if she acted defensively against harm to herself (analogous to

self-defense, which carries no liability).

  • Practically, both the Ibn Hajar and al-Ramli positions are considered

valid expressions of the mu'tamad (relied-upon) Shafi'i position and

may be given as fatwa without restriction to necessity, unlike weaker

minority opinions (which may only be relied upon when genuine need

exists) — though the class noted a pastoral norm among some past teachers

of only mentioning al-Ramli's more permissive view to women with a

specific medical need (e.g. fear of complications from closely spaced

pregnancies) rather than publicizing it broadly.

  • Whether a wife may act on this without her husband's consent: since the

embryo resulted from intercourse that was itself lawful (not a wrong done

to her), the husband is understood to have an interest in it, and it is

not clearly permissible for her to unilaterally terminate absent his

consent — except where she acts to prevent harm to herself, treated

analogously to self-defense.

Referenced scholars / texts

Ibn Hajar al-Haytami (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj), Shihab al-Din al-Ramli (and his

Nihayat al-Muhtaj), Abu Ishaq al-Marwazi, Abu Hanifa, al-Ghazali's Ihya

(cited on coitus interruptus).

2. Whether Marriage Itself Is an Act of Worship

Background / Question

Is the marriage contract itself considered "worship" ('ibada), or is it

merely permissible/beneficial unless accompanied by a devotional intent?

Ruling

  • The class revisited the earlier discussion that "the state of dedicated

worship is definitively better than the state of marriage" — but

clarified that the specific comparison in the text is between

devoting oneself fully to worship (for someone able to do so) and

marriage — not a blanket claim that worship in general outranks marriage

in every case, since one who cannot devote himself to worship may find

marriage preferable to idleness.

  • Some held marriage is not "worship" in itself unless undertaken with the

intent of having good offspring; this was answered by noting that its

validity for a disbeliever does not negate its being worship for a

Muslim, just as maintaining mosques or manumission are also acts

commanded by the Lawgiver and hence classified as worship. The author's

own fatwa: marriage becomes an act of the Hereafter meriting reward when

undertaken with an intent such as seeking righteous offspring, kinship,

companionship, or aiding acts of worship; otherwise it remains simply

permissible (mubah).

  • The general principle affirmed: an act is rewarded whenever it is

recommended and no factor negates the reward (such as showing off), or

when undertaken with an intent of obedience (e.g. hoping for good

children) even if not independently recommended — parallel to how

reciting a Qur'anic verse, or giving charity, is rewarded even absent an

explicit intention to seek reward, so long as no factor (such as reciting

merely to quote someone, not as Qur'an) cancels that reward.

  • The Prophet's ﷺ own marriages are unconditionally an act of devotion,

since they served to transmit dimensions of his praiseworthy character

not otherwise visible to men — hence he was permitted more wives than

others, so that each of his wives could preserve what the others could

not, given how many aspects of his character there were to transmit.

Referenced scholars / texts

Al-Mawardi, al-Subki (on the specific point of comparison being with

"devoting oneself to worship"), Ibn Hajar al-Haytami.

3. Extended Digression: The Prophet's ﷺ Infallibility (Ismah)

Background / Question

A student's question about the incident where the Prophet ﷺ delayed

Companions from tending to date-palm pollination after advising against

artificial pollination — and the crop subsequently failed — led to a

broader discussion: can the Prophet ﷺ be mistaken in matters of ordinary

worldly knowledge, distinct from matters of religious legislation?

Ruling

  • The Prophet ﷺ is infallible (ma'sum) from error and sin in what he

conveys as legislation, per "he does not speak from desire; it is only

revelation revealed" and his own statement to Ibn 'Umar, "write, for by

God nothing comes from this mouth but truth." This ismah covers both

religious and worldly statements he makes as binding guidance — it is not

limited to religious rulings alone, contrary to some contemporary claims

that he is only infallible in religious matters, not worldly ones.

Exceptions exist only for ordinary human forgetfulness (as in his own

statement "I do not forget, but I am made to forget," referring to

forgetting the number of prayer cycles on isolated occasions) — this is

a different category from religious/legal error and does not affect

legislative authority.

  • The pollination incident is explained as the Companions having, out of

excessive literalism, taken as a binding ruling something the Prophet ﷺ

intended only as a suggestion toward trusting in God's providence (not

an assertion that pollination has no agricultural effect) — the fault

lay in the Companions' own hesitant, overly literal understanding, not

in any factual error on the Prophet's ﷺ part. "You know best your worldly

affairs" means the Companions possessed accurate operational knowledge of

their own affairs, not that they were more knowledgeable than the Prophet

ﷺ himself, who as the most complete of all people in intellect and

knowledge could not have been ignorant of something as basic as

pollination.

  • When a scenario admits of an interpretation upholding the Prophet's ﷺ

full honor versus one that diminishes it, the interpretation upholding his

honor is to be preferred without hesitation, as it best accords with the

plain sense of the relevant texts on his infallibility.

Referenced scholars / texts

The hadith of Ibn 'Umar ("write, for nothing comes from this mouth but

truth"), the hadith "I do not forget, but I am made to forget," the Qur'anic

verse "he does not speak from desire" (53:3-4).

4. When Marriage Becomes Disliked (Makruh)

Background / Question

Under what circumstances does marriage shift from recommended/neutral to

actively disliked?

Ruling

  • For a man: disliked if he has no desire for intercourse (whether due to

temperament or a transient preoccupation) and lacks the financial means

(dower, seasonal clothing, daily maintenance) — since he would be

obligating himself to something unneeded, without necessity (unlike

someone hungry who borrows to eat).

  • If he has the means but lacks desire, marriage is not disliked, since he

incurs no unmet obligation, and marriage's purposes are not limited to

intercourse — some scholars (Ibn Hajar leaning to accept this, al-Ramli

rejecting it absent actual desire) extend recommendation to marrying for

companionship, kinship ties, or household help/support in worship.

  • If he has both desire and means but suffers from a chronic condition

(old age, permanent illness, impotence) that would harm the wife by

leaving her un-satisfied and thus prone to moral risk, marriage is

disliked (though not obligatory to divorce an existing wife on this

basis, absent clear textual support for such an obligation) — as

distinguished from someone whose incapacity is merely occasional (no

dislike attaches).

  • Whether these conditions, if they arise after the marriage contract

(rather than existing at its outset), retroactively affect it: the

stronger view is no — an existing marital bond, and the affection/care

built over time, outweighs treating it the same as at the point of

initial contracting.

  • For women: the same five-fold analysis is understood to apply by

extension (contra the literal wording of the text, which appears to

address only men) — recommended for a woman who desires marriage,

needs financial support, or fears falling into sin without it; scholars

differ on whether it is recommended unconditionally for a devoted

worshipper (al-Zanjani: yes, since marriage burdens her with no

additional duty; others: no, since a wife carries substantial

obligations toward her husband that she may not be able to fulfill,

citing severe warnings against neglecting such duties) — the class

favored the latter, adding that a woman convinced she will not be able

to fulfill her marital duties, without a genuine need to marry, is

religiously prohibited from marrying.

  • Scholars also disputed whether fasting is prescribed for a woman lacking

the means to marry, the same way it is prescribed for men to curb desire

— the stronger view treats the general hadith ("O youths...") as

including women too, by analogy, notwithstanding a passage in some

texts suggesting fasting has "no role" for her.

Referenced scholars / texts

Al-Zanjani, al-Adhra'i, Ibn Hajar al-Haytami, Shihab al-Din al-Ramli (and

al-Nihaya).

5. Closing Q&A

Family Reunion Gatherings

A multi-day annual family reunion custom (held around the end of a lunar

month, chosen partly to counter old superstitions about "unlucky" days

tied to the Prophet's ﷺ final illness and death) is not religious

innovation — it is a social gathering for kinship and recreation, without

any specifically religious ritual attached, comparable to a long-observed

local custom in Tarim of scheduling a recreational outing on a day

traditionally considered inauspicious, specifically to counteract that

superstition.

Death-Anniversary Gatherings (Hawliyyat)

Annual gatherings marking someone's death, while not a traditional practice

in the speaker's home region and resembling a practice associated with

innovators in some other communities, are judged by intention: gatherings

centered on reviving grief/mourning are blameworthy, but ones centered on

Qur'an recitation or remembrance for the deceased's benefit are

unobjectionable — illustrated by the large annual gathering some local

scholars attend in Say'un marking the death anniversary of Habib Ali

al-Habshi (author of the Simt al-Durar mawlid), one of the largest such

gatherings in Hadramawt. The operative principle: actions are judged by

intentions, and there is no need to treat commemorative gatherings as

inherently tainted by association with innovation.

Multiple Friday Prayers in One Town

Where a town now has more than one Friday congregation (a departure from

older practice, driven by the arrival of newer, Wahhabi-influenced

mosques), the class reviewed differing bases for judging which

congregation counts as the (single) valid Friday prayer where multiplicity

is not due to genuine necessity: by who actually attends the largest

congregation, by who is formally obligated to attend, or by the more lenient

view — endorsed here as the practically preferred position, though not

necessarily the doctrinally strongest — of counting whichever congregation

consists of those for whom Friday prayer is valid at all, since this is the

most inclusive and workable standard. Where multiple congregations exist

without established necessity, the default assumption is that no genuine

necessity exists, rendering the (extra) Friday prayer invalid and Dhuhr

obligatory on those who did not pray in the original, sole historical

mosque — though the speaker noted his own past practice of praying both

Friday and Dhuhr afterward as a precaution, before settling on relying

solely on the Friday prayer at the original congregation to avoid

normalizing doubt about its validity. A newer congregation is treated as

not fulfilling Friday prayer at all if it is credibly charged with an

actual, non-trivial innovation (such as omitting the pre-Friday four-cycle

prayer and beginning the sermon the instant the Dhuhr call sounds), which

would make that congregation's "Friday" ruled invalid outright — placing

the burden of the multiplicity, and the resulting obligation to also pray

Dhuhr, on that newer congregation's attendees rather than the original one.

Referenced scholars / texts

Al-Khatib al-Shirbini (commentary on Tuhfat al-Muhtaj), Ibn Hajar

al-Haytami, the Sharh al-Yaqut commentary on al-Manthumat al-Waraqat

(cited by a student on the criterion for counting Friday attendees),

Shaykh Muhammad Ba'awdhan (a local teacher's earlier practice of praying

both Jumu'ah and Dhuhr).
