Marriage Sermons, Contract Etiquette & Intimacy (Tuhfa) + Qur'an on Phone Screens & Mushaf Printing (Fatawa al-Hindi) - October 20, 2026

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, the sunnas around the engagement and the marriage contract): the class covers the recommended sermons (khutba) before the proposal and before the contract, the opening wording, the conditions for a valid acceptance (continuity, answering by the addressee, being heard, matching the offer), recommended timing and place of the contract, congratulations and supplications, and the etiquette of intimacy, including when it is disliked or forbidden. Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah

1. The Sermon Before the Proposal

Ruling:

  • It is recommended for the suitor or his representative to be brief in the proposal and to propose explicitly, not by allusion (as al-Jalal al-Balqini noted and it is evident), since a proposal accompanied by a sermon shows desire for marriage and is only permitted when explicit proposals are permitted. Shabramallisi: making the sermon with an allusive proposal turns it into an explicit one, so it would be forbidden then (for example a woman in 'idda).
  • Precede the proposal (khitba) with a sermon (khutba): praise of Allah and thanks, then blessings on the Prophet, then the advice of taqwa, then: "I have come to you [or: my principal has sent me] seeking your noble daughter/young woman for so-and-so." The teacher said these exact words are not fixed worship; any wording of this meaning is fine.
  • The guardian or his deputy replies the same way (praise, blessings, taqwa), then says "he is not a man turned away from" (i.e. he is welcome), or any wording showing no refusal.
  • The teacher described the custom in his circle: the suitor himself usually does not attend; his brothers and elders go, a meal is served, and someone else speaks.

2. The Sermon Before the Contract

  • A second sermon is recommended before the contract when about to utter it, by the guardian, the husband, either deputy, or a third party. The text calls it more emphasized than the first; the teacher said in his tradition it is typically a respected person (such as a descendant of the Prophet's family) who gives it, as opposed to a registrar in other settings.
  • Third sermon: the teacher noted that after this a person leads the pair through the contract with "Praise be to Allah and blessings on the Prophet, O so-and-so son of so-and-so, I marry you..." but it is not a full khutba, and he quoted al-Habib Muhammad b. Hasan al-Haddad that this is not sunna and no major scholar such as al-Ghazali mentioned it.
  • Validity: if the guardian opens with praise and blessings, says "I have married you," and the husband says "Praise be to Allah and blessings on the Prophet, I accept," the contract is valid on the sounder view, since the praise is a short introduction that is not foreign to the contract.
  • Recommended or not? Al-Nawawi in the text: the sounder view is it is not recommended; indeed leaving it is recommended to avoid the view that it invalidates (also in al-Adhkar). Ibn Hajar notes that in al-Rawda and its source it is recommended with the added advice to taqwa, and that al-Adhra'i argued for it at length. The teacher: the relied-upon position of al-Nihaya and al-Mughni, and the practice in his circle, is that it is not recommended, so the groom should say "I accept" directly as people do; Ibn Hajar seems to lean to al-Rawda but the practice is against him.
  • Al-Mawardi's report that the Prophet and 'Ali both gave a sermon at the marriage of Fatima: Ibn Hajar answers (referring to his own book al-Sawa'iq al-Muhriqa) that the Prophet married her to him in his absence, and when 'Ali came the Prophet told him Allah had commanded it and he said "I am pleased"; if the report is as al-Mawardi says it was repeated to please him, otherwise it is among the Prophet's specific privileges to marry anyone to anyone, since he has more right over believers than they themselves.

3. Length and Continuity

  • Al-Adhkar: the sermon before the contract is better longer than the sermon before the proposal. If the separating remembrance is long, the marriage is invalid for certain, as it shows turning away. The teacher noted the permitted gap is the amount of praise and blessings; al-Qaffal's measure is that if they had stayed silent for that time it would no longer count as an answer. Al-Ramli and al-Khatib: this is by custom.
  • Separation by a stranger: Ibn Hajar, by analogy with sales: an outside speech from the one whose answer is awaited harms even if short, but from one who has finished speaking harms only if long. Al-Ramli and al-Khatib: it harms either way. The saying "I marry you, so treat her well" followed by acceptance is invalid; silence harms if long.
  • Other conditions: the answer must come from the person addressed, not his deputy (if the deputy is addressed, the principal does not answer); it must be heard by those nearby; the initiator must not withdraw; the capacities of the parties (and the guardian's permission, if required) must last through the contract; the acceptance must match the offer (except in the dowry, where a mismatch only reverts to the proper dowry, unlike a sale price); the initiator must finish his speech, including the dowry and its description (Ibn Hajar queries this since the dowry need not be stated).
  • Microphone question: a student raised the objection from the students about whether the pair are "heard by those nearby" when the contract is amplified or recorded. The teacher: the shaykhs did not treat the microphone as a problem, since it carries the voice immediately and with certainty, like the wind carrying the sound; a modern amplified signal differs from an old horn, but they followed the reason, not the form. (The connection broke up during this part.)

4. Timing, Place, and Congratulations

  • Month: marrying and consummating in Shawwal is recommended, based on 'A'isha's report rebutting those who disliked it ("Which of his wives was more favored by him than I?").
  • Place and time: the contract in a mosque, on Friday, and at the start of the day (hadith: "O Allah, bless my Umma in its early day"). Ibn Hajar rejects the custom of doing it right after the Friday prayer, unless delayed to allow more people, especially scholars and the righteous, to attend; then it is better. The teacher: early in the day is best if people will attend; if the practical attendance is after Friday prayer, then after it.
  • Before the contract: it is sunna for the guardian to say "I [will] marry you in accordance with what Allah commanded: keeping with kindness or releasing with grace," followed by the contract. The teacher noted the textual point that "uzawwijuka" (present tense, "I wish to marry you") is the right reading in the text, as the printed "zawwajtuka" would make an acceptance invalid, and that this is possibly a printer's error.
  • After the contract: "May Allah bless you and bless upon you and unite you in goodness." The teacher said men say it to men and women to women or mahrams; a stranger woman greeting the groom this way is not suitable. It remains recommended for someone who missed the contract to say it when he meets the groom, even later, as long as the occasion for congratulation still applies.
  • "How did you find your family?" Al-Adhkar says this is also sunna, based on the Prophet's wives asking him after his marriage. Ibn Hajar: it is not a real question (the Prophet did not answer); it means "I hope you found her as you like". It should only be said to someone who knows the sunna, because ordinary people might take offense.
  • "Bi'l-rafa' wa'l-banin" (the pre-Islamic wedding wish "with harmony and sons") is disliked, though it is a supplication, since it betrays a preference for boys and is the old custom of those who disliked girls. The general blessing above covers everything.

5. Etiquette of Intimacy

  • Take her by the forelock at first meeting and say "May Allah bless each of us in the other"; before relations cover themselves with a garment. Precede with cleanliness, perfume, kissing and whatever stimulates. The teacher noted that a wife dressing alluringly is even more important than perfume, and cited Ibn 'Abbas ("I like to adorn myself for my wife as I like her to adorn herself for me," per the verse "they have rights similar to those against them").
  • Say "In the name of Allah, O Allah keep Satan away from us and from what You grant us" even with no hope of a child. Al-Nihaya: this is an individual sunna for each of them; al-Mughni: for the husband only. The teacher compared this with the sunna kifaya and noted the ruling differs by whether there is hope of a child.
  • At the moment of ejaculation, bring the sincerity of the invocation to heart (and Qur'an 25:54 silently). Bughyat al-Mustarshidin adds that remembering the righteous has a blessing on the child.
  • Not disliked: relations facing the qibla, even in the open; talking during relations is disliked. No manner is disliked as long as it avoids the anus and what harms (as a trusted doctor judges), and menstruation or impurity. The teacher explained that "harm" is a matter of probability, not of the judge's reliability: if harm is suspected it is disliked, and if likely it is forbidden, per the same principle as with sun-heated water.
  • Speaking about details: it is forbidden to relate the details of relations (some say a major sin), if it is something one is ashamed of and she dislikes it. If she consents or has been shown to consent (e.g. women telling each other what is not disliked), it is not forbidden.
  • Imagining another woman: discussed earlier (see the Sept 29 session).
  • Timing: it is said to be better to avoid the first, middle and last nights of the month (as Satan attends), but nothing is established; if the husband ejaculates first, it is recommended to wait for her; the pre-dawn (sahar) time is recommended since hunger and satiety are both absent, and as a time of night worship. Al-Ramli and doctors' limits: do it when the desire arises naturally, not from forced thought. If a man sees a woman who attracts him he should go to his wife, so as not to keep the attachment. It is also recommended on Friday before going (to reduce desire when passing women in the street), and not leaving it when back from a journey. Strengthening aids that are lawful and medically safe are fine if for chastity or children; many people err and harm themselves.
  • Intercourse with a pregnant or nursing woman is discouraged, disliked if there is fear of harm to the child, and forbidden if harm is likely. Ibn Hajar says "if he is sure of harm"; the teacher said per the earlier principle, thinking it probable is enough for forbidden (al-Nihaya: "if it overwhelms his thinking" with regard to the child's death, even if he fears zina), and that those who say "not disliked" mean when no harm is feared.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi (Islamic Fiqh Academy of India)

Resolution 101 (1/24) — Qur'an writing and translation (continued)

(The 24th Fiqh Seminar, Azirat, March 2015; the class read from p. 259. Item 7 was not read in this recording.)

8. Writing and printing the Qur'an in Braille is permitted given the blind's need, since Braille is a symbolic language rather than a script; but as it is used as a symbol for the Qur'an in writing a mushaf it must be respected, and the blind must learn the Qur'an from a teacher who is fully competent in it, by the soundest methods.

9. Verses of the Qur'an appearing on a phone screen should not be touched without purification (tahara).

10. A phone's body and similar electronic devices are in the ruling of a detached cover; therefore if Qur'anic verses are displayed on the screen, wudu is not required merely for carrying the device.

  • Note: Muftis Junayd b. Muhammad al-Baqi and Muhammad Shahd al-Qasimi hold it is not permitted to write the Qur'an in non-Arabic script even with the Arabic text attached; Ustadh Muhammad 'Uthman Thawban A'zam al-Qasimi also holds Braille transcription is not permitted.

Class discussion:

  • On the Braille note, the teacher said the third view (permitted by need, with the Arabic text present if possible) seems preferable, noting this is akin to istihsan among the Hanafis.
  • Phone screen: the wording "should not touch" and then "wudu is not required for carrying" prompted the teacher to read it as: not obligatory but disliked in etiquette, not forbidden (otherwise they would have said forbidden). A student pointed out the Hanafi usage of "la yanbaghi" can mean disliked-forbidden (makruh tahriman); the teacher said the authors are not strictly following the Hanafi school, and that the screen is a barrier, as in the Hanafi view that one may touch a mushaf through a barrier. In his own view a Qur'an on a phone is not truly a mushaf: one may say "I did not read from the mushaf, I read it from the phone," which is a valid denial; the display is pixels, not writing touched directly, as if seen through glass. The question of a verse typed in a chat remains to be studied. He leaned that it is not forbidden, as the default is no prohibition.

The Seminar's Statement on Mushaf Printing Conventions

The statement says the Qur'an is the final guidance, and its preservation includes the preservation of the writing method dictated by the Prophet in the 'Uthmani rasm; the signs added later (vowels, diacritics) are not part of the text. The method used in India and Pakistan follows the established tradition via Abu 'Amr al-Dani (d. 444 AH) and has been verified by local scholars; altering it to the Arab-world symbols is pointless and could lead to division, so people should continue the printing conventions they have.

Class discussion:

  • The teacher accepted most points but queried the claims: he said the Prophet dictated the Qur'an while hadith was written down at times; and the older Indian mushafs on which he and his teachers memorized (and which Shaykh Muhammad Ba Sudan said troubled one's memorization) had small errors and weaker marking of pauses and signs (e.g. the circle mark on "wa mala'ihi"), unlike the modern Madinah print. He said he had to struggle to relearn his memorization when he switched to the newer print.
  • "Pointless" cannot be right because pointless changes would be forbidden as play; the changes actually serve teaching (marking idgham, ghunna, imala). Only decorative drawing in the margin would be forbidden.
  • On division: the teacher said the Indians are very strict in religious matters (e.g. someone seeing a book placed on the ground may react harshly), and while this is sometimes praised, correction of a first-time mistake should be gentle, because it may be inadvertence. Harsh rebuke is only for a stubborn or heedless person.
