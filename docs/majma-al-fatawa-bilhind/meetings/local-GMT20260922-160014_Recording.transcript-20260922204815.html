Kitab al-Nikah: Gaze & Fisq Rulings (Tuhfa) + Istisna' & Citizenship Fatawa al-Hindi - September 22, 2026

📅 2026-09-22 🕐 16:00

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah): the session opens with rulings on what may or may not be looked at or touched between men and women, non-mahrams, and disbelieving women, then moves into an extended discussion on the threshold of fisq (open sinfulness) and its effect on testimony — interrupted by two live questions on accepting a workplace gift and on underpaid teacher contracts, and closing with a note on the ruling on price-fixing (tas'ir). Book 2 (Fatawa al-Hindi): the class reads resolutions on manufacturing contracts (istisna') and citizenship/nationality (al-muwatana), followed by a set of live fatwa questions.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah

1. Looking at and Touching Non-Mahrams

Ruling:

  • The more correct view (al-asahh): it is forbidden for a Muslim woman to look at a disbelieving woman beyond what is visible during normal work (face, neck, hands to the upper arm, feet to the knee) — based on "aw nisa'ihinna" (Qur'an, restricting women's exposure to "their [Muslim] women") and the fear that a dhimmi woman may describe the Muslim woman to non-Muslim men; supported by 'Umar's practice of barring dhimmi women from entering baths with Muslim women or visiting the Mothers of the Believers.
  • A woman known for fisq via lesbian acts (sihaq) or procuring (qiyada) is treated like a disbelieving woman for this purpose. The text extends this to a woman known for zina (fornication), but the teacher noted that al-Khatib al-Shirbini did not adopt that extension — such a woman keeps the normal ruling for Muslim women so long as she remains Muslim.
  • Extended discussion on the threshold of fisq: a single or occasional sin does not make someone "fasiq." The accepted view (al-mu'tamad) is that habitual persistence (israr) in minor sins only makes a person fasiq once such sins outweigh or equal one's good deeds — not by mere repetition. Practical application: a well-known singer engaged in public immodesty, mixing with men, and physical contact with fans cannot be formally labeled "fasiqah" if her acts of worship and other good deeds likely outweigh those sins; the label of fisq is properly tied to testimony-rejection by character-witnesses (muzakkin) — who judge by outward reputation, not to a general public moral verdict. Mere public "mujahara" (open display) of sin removes a person from the state of general divine pardon (kull ummati mu'afa) but does not by itself elevate a minor sin to a major one, unless it amounts to open defiance of God.
  • A woman may look at an unrelated man's body (other than between navel and knee) absent fear of temptation (fitna) or desire, per the view adopted in Safinat al-Najah/al-Rafi'i and followed practically today; men and women are treated differently here because a man is understood to typically initiate desirous gazing, while a woman's gaze is treated as usually incidental — hence it is not deemed forbidden for a man to appear bare-chested or bare-headed in public (his exposure is not the "usual" object of desire-driven gazing), while the reverse is forbidden for women.
  • Touching rules for mahrams: kissing/touching a mahram's hand, cheek, or head (not 'awra) is agreed permissible absent desire, whether for need or affection (shafaqa) — e.g., an adult sibling or aunt's hand may be kissed as a mark of respect even without a specific need. Kissing the mouth of a mahram is discouraged unless customary (e.g., kissing a young child). Cheek-to-cheek greeting customs among men (mu'anaqa, touching cheeks, shoulders, or foreheads) were discussed as regionally variable — the teacher noted that cheek-touching (not really kissing) is customary and tolerated in some regions (e.g. Yemen, Egypt) but discouraged in others in favor of a shoulder/neck embrace, and that the authentically sunnah greeting for a returning traveler or long-absent relative is a kiss on the cheek or shoulder (documented in Sharh al-Muhtaj, citing the Prophet's kissing Zayd ibn Haritha's shoulder), not a full chest-to-chest embrace.

Live question — Accepting a gift from an unrelated company

A government employee was offered a small in-kind gift by a company that has no current or planned business relationship with his employer. Ruling: permissible to accept, since there is no existing official relationship between the giver and the recipient's position (analogous to a gift given to a person before he is appointed judge, or outside his jurisdiction) — though declining is still preferable (al-wara' awla). If accepted and later deemed improper, it must be returned to the giver, not redirected to charity, unless the giver can no longer be reached.

Live question — Underpaid teacher contracts

Some private schools in Jordan issue two contracts: an official one at the legal minimum wage (~300 dinars) for compliance purposes, and a real one under which the teacher actually receives far less (e.g. 100 dinars). Ruling: since these are private schools not bound by government-employee pay scales, the wage-floor regulation is a matter of siyasa (state policy) rather than a shar'i obligation — outwardly (zahiran) one must not openly flout the ruler's order, but inwardly (batinan) the underlying private wage agreement is valid and binding once both employer and employee freely consented to it, since Shari'a places no floor on a fair wage (ujrat al-mithl) beyond mutual consent; it is comparable to ordinary bargaining in a sale. The ruler's wage-floor is analogous to price-fixing (tas'ir), discussed next.

Note — Price-fixing (Tas'ir)

Price-fixing by the ruler/imam is forbidden even during scarcity, though a merchant who defies it is excused from liability (though selling above the fixed price still stands validly, so long as it is not done in open defiance, mujahara). Obedience to such an order is obligatory outwardly only (zahiran, to avoid open confrontation with authority) but not inwardly (batinan) when the order serves a narrow interest rather than a genuine public good — unlike orders that serve the public interest generally, which must be obeyed both outwardly and inwardly.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi

Resolution 89/3/20 — Manufacturing contracts (Istisna')

  • Istisna' is in reality a sale (bay'), valid for any manufacturable item, movable or immovable, subject to conditions: the item must be capable of manufacture; specifiable by kind, description, weight, size, and design; the raw materials must be supplied by the maker himself (not the client — otherwise the contract becomes an ijara, a hire of labor); and all specifications must be stated clearly at the time of contracting to avoid ambiguity (jahala/gharar).
  • The contract binds both parties once concluded; neither may unilaterally rescind without the other's consent. Discussed in light of COVID-era construction-material price spikes (30-50%+): on the resolution's own principle the contract stands as agreed regardless of the spike, though the class noted a contemporary view (Shaykh Ali al-Qaradaghi) that distributes the loss between the parties when the spike stems from war/crisis (treated as a jaiha, a calamity), but not from ordinary market movement.
  • The manufacturer may make the item himself or via an agent; the client may not resell the item before actually taking possession of it.
  • A manufacturer's genuine losses from a client's cancellation may be offset (e.g., via a forfeited deposit/'urbun); a late-delivery penalty clause tied to elapsed time and cost (as some modern jurists apply) was rejected by the teacher as impermissible — it resembles a jahiliyya-style penalty and risks becoming riba, since tying a fixed extra payment to elapsed time on what is functionally a debt obligation is not valid absent a genuine, itemized loss.
  • Note: the Shafi'i school has no distinct istisna' contract as such; the closest valid structures are salam (advance payment for a described, fungible item — but not valid for real estate/construction), a combination of ijara/ju'ala contracts for labor and materials separately, or a non-binding promise-to-purchase once the item is completed (as with a tailor who owns the cloth and is asked to sew a garment).

Resolution — Citizenship (al-Muwatana)

  • Islam is one religion and Muslims are one Ummah bound by unity, not fragmented by origin — a principle so basic it is definitional (not merely "the default, overridden by exception," as the resolution's wording loosely implies).
  • The Ummah has been unable to fully escape the modern Western-derived nation-state system that divides Muslims territorially into separate legal nationalities; accepting this system in the current international order is tolerated as a necessity of the times, for identification purposes (comparable to tribal identity), not as an endorsement of division.
  • A Muslim facing serious threats to religion, life, wealth, honor, or mind in his home country must be granted citizenship by another Muslim country if he requests it — discussed as potentially obligatory to grant when the threat is to religion, life, wealth, honor, or mind, but a Muslim seeking citizenship merely to avoid a lesser harm (e.g. a residency fine) is only permitted, not obligated, to pursue it, since Shari'a does not obligate protecting mere wealth at the cost of a serious step like changing nationality (analogous to the debate over whether fear for another's property excuses missing Friday prayer).
  • A Muslim country must grant full citizenship rights to Muslim refugees fleeing to it under duress.
  • Naturalizing in a non-Muslim country: forbidden where religion, life, wealth, or mind would be endangered there; forbidden if motivated by admiration for a civilization that contradicts Islam (referring to the applicant's own motive, not the host country generally); disliked (makruh) if sought merely to raise one's standard of living; permitted for economic hardship, medical need, or educational purposes; and recommended (mustahabb) if undertaken for da'wah purposes.

Live Fatwa Questions

  • Inheritance transfer without formal division: a man died leaving parents, a wife, and two daughters; the grandparents wished to give their inherited share to the widow and daughters without formally itemizing each person's portion. Recommended mechanism: each grandparent makes a binding vow (nadhr) — "I vow what is due to me from my son's estate to his wife and daughters, according to their legal shares" — since a vow, unlike a gift (hiba), validly applies to an unspecified or even not-yet-possessed amount, avoiding the formal possession/transfer procedures a hiba would require. Because the two daughters (ages 2 and 3 months) are legally under their paternal grandfather's guardianship (not their mother's, regardless of what any state authority names her), any withdrawal from their share requires his authorization; the mother may act as his delegated agent for withdrawals made for the children's benefit.
  • Attending a gathering where alcohol is present: merely being present where alcohol is served (without drinking) is disliked/impermissible in principle, since one is not "honoring" a forbidden act by one's presence, but it is excused under a genuine, non-trivial need (hajah shadidah) — treated as equivalent to necessity for attendance only, not for participating in the sin itself — e.g., missing an award ceremony one is heavily invested in. The same reasoning was extended by analogy to a prior discussion on car insurance, which some contemporary scholars (e.g. Shaykh 'Atiyya Saqr) have treated as approaching necessity given the legal and financial risk of going without it, though the teacher declined to give a final verdict on that question.
