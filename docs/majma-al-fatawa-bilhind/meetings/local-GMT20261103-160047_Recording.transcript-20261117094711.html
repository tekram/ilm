Marriage Wording: Mute, Foreign Language & Conditions (Tuhfa) + Food Halal, Certification & Organ Donation (Fatawa al-Hindi) - November 3, 2026

Overview

Book 1 (Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah, the marriage wording): the class continues the wording of the contract: that it is valid only with the words of marrying (zawwaja/ankaha), why not with words of permitting, gifting or owning; the contract of a mute person; contracts in other languages; why implicit wording (kinaya) is invalid; errors in naming the bride; request forms such as "marry me your daughter"; and why conditional wording voids it. A student also raises a real case of a contract where the officiant only prompted "qabiltuhu." Book 2 (Fatawa al-Hindi): the 24th seminar of the Islamic Fiqh Academy of India: the rules of halal and haram in food products (Resolution 103 (3/24)), halal certification standards (104 (4/24)), and organ and blood donation (105 (5/24)). The class ends with a Q&A on the pagan origins of weekday and month names.

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah

1. Only the Words of Marrying

Ruling: the contract is valid only with a wording derived from "tazwij" or "inkah." The text's "uzawwijuka" and similar derived forms (such as the active participle "mu zawwijuka") are not repetitions of earlier rulings but are accepted. Ibn Hajar differs from al-Bulqini on an active-participle form: the participle is a real expression of the present, so it does not suggest a promise, unlike the present-tense verb, though the dispute over each is famous and the preference in their combination is by the weight of the scholars versed in the language.

  • Evidence: the hadith "fear Allah regarding women; you took them by Allah's trust and made their private parts lawful by the word of Allah," and "Allah's word" means what appears in His Book, which has only these two terms. Analogy is barred since marriage has an element of worship, so words of permission, gift, ownership or service do not work.
  • The Prophet's own marriage "by gift" is among his specific privileges ("exclusively for you, not for the believers"). The hadith in al-Bukhari "malliktuka bi ma ma'aka min al-Qur'an" is either a slip by one narrator (Ma'mar), since the majority narration is "zawwajtuka" and the group is more reliable than one; or a narration by meaning, assuming synonymy; or the Prophet combined both words to signal the force of the husband's right, as if he were an owner.

2. The Mute and the Writer

  • The mute man's marriage is valid by his clear gesture (understood by clever and ordinary people alike) and by writing, without dispute (as in al-Majmu'). A criticism: the author takes writing as only an implicit divorce, so how could marriage, which is graver, be valid by it? Answer: it applies where he has no understandable gesture and cannot appoint a representative. A gesture that only the clever understand is treated like writing.
  • The teacher discussed whether a mute person can "write": he must write letters, not draw; Shaykh Fadl said a person who cannot hear cannot speak, so some mutes are really deaf. The mute's writing is like his gesture, but unlike a speaker's, a speaker must pronounce the contract in nikah, unlike sales.

3. Other Languages

  • A contract is valid in a language other than Arabic on the sounder view, even if he knows Arabic, since meaning governs and there is no inimitability at stake. Conditions: he uses a wording the speakers of that language consider explicit; each party understands his own and the other's speech (even through a trustworthy person telling him the meaning beforehand); and the two witnesses must also understand it.

4. No Implicit Wording; Naming the Bride

  • Implicit wording (kinaya) such as "ahlaltuka ibnati" ("I made my daughter lawful for you") is invalid even if he says he intended marriage and circumstances support it, since the witnesses cannot know his intent. This differs from sales, where witnesses are not required.
  • Naming the bride: the witnesses' ignorance of the bride's identity does not harm, but ignorance in the wording itself does. If a father of several daughters says "I marry you one of them" or "my daughter Fatima," intending a particular one, it is valid even if the name is not hers: the intention governs (he said "Fatima" but meant Zaynab; the same for a father marrying a son and giving the wrong name). If the two parties intended different women, the contract is void. If the guardian dies and the groom claims "I meant Fatima" while she says otherwise, she is believed on oath since she has knowledge of what was intended. The teacher noted that mistakes in naming happen with crowding and confusion, and mentioned naming practices among Indonesians.
  • "Qabiltu" alone is insufficient; he must say "qabiltu nikahaha / tazwijaha" (the text's "qabiltuhu" in the intermediary case is not relied upon). The "madhhab" does not accept it (al-Kurdi: whether in the mutawassit case or not).

5. Request Forms

  • "Zawwijni ibnataka" followed by "zawwajtuka" is valid; likewise the guardian saying to the husband "tazawwajha" and he says "tazawwajtuha," with no need for "qabiltu" (as in the Prophet's marriage of the woman who offered herself: "zawwijniha," and "zawwajtukaha," with no further acceptance reported).
  • Not valid are forms lacking firmness: "tuzawwijuni," "zawwajtani," "zawwajtuha minni," "yatazawwajuha," "tatazawwajuha," since they are questions; unless a second statement of offer or acceptance follows. Also invalid: "qul tazawwajtuha" (an order to say words), "zawwajtu nafsi aw ibni min bintik" (the husband is not the object of the contract, though he receives its ruling in "I am divorced" with intent), and "zawwajtu bintī fulanan" then writing or sending to him (this is accepted in sales, which are more lenient).

6. Conditional Wording

  • The contract cannot be made conditional, or it is void (as in sales, more strictly in marriage). Cases: told of a birth, "if it is a girl, I have married you" (void); "if my daughter has been divorced and completed her 'idda, I marry you to her" (void even if it turns out true); one with four wives saying "if one of them has died I marry you my daughter" (void).
  • It is valid if it merely states a fact he already believes: told of a girl's birth and he says "if the informant is truthful, I marry you" (in effect "since it is a girl," "in" meaning "idh," like "remind, if reminder benefits"); or told of his wife's death and says "if the informant is right, I have married your daughter."
  • Al-Bulqini: where the conditional wording is simply what the situation implies (e.g. "I marry you my daughter if she is alive," where her death was only rumored), it may be valid; Ibn Hajar doubts this since "in" there does not mean "since" (life is only presumed). Others allowed "if so-and-so is my ward, I have married you to her"; read as applying where he knows or thinks she is his ward; and "I marry you if you wish" is not meant as a condition but is not compared with sales, since marriage is more strict.

7. A Case: a Contract Where the Officiant Prompted Only "Qabiltuhu"

A student attended a marriage in which the officiant had the husband say only "qabiltuhu" (accepted it). He spoke to the officiant afterward but the officiant was not persuaded and did not repeat the contract; others advised that those families were of ordinary religiosity and a correction might cause trouble. The teacher: the relied-upon position requires "qabiltu nikaha/tazwijaha"; there are views among the Hanafis that are easier, and given the circumstances the caution is to repeat the contract if no discord results, and it costs little (only "I marry you, my daughter"); but if the contract was invalid, there may be a dowry-of-the-like owed for the intercourse plus a dowry for the new contract. If the parties are ordinary people and will not accept advice, the student has done his duty by raising it with the officiant.

8. A Question on Present-Tense Wording in English

A student noted that contracts in English use the present tense generally. The teacher: the criterion is not usage but whether the wording leaves open a promise or a future sense; if in that language it has only the sense of the performative (that it is done), it is valid, but if it still carries the sense of a promise it is not.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi (Islamic Fiqh Academy of India)

Resolution 103 (3/24) — Rules of halal and haram in food products

  • Food products relate to health, and some producers neglect health standards or adulterate; the seminar calls on producers and government to apply health-care standards and effective laws, and says it is not forbidden (in the reading, as corrected in class) to take measures to increase crop yield, but forbidden to use fertilizers and drugs that gravely harm health, or chemicals that ripen fruit early or enlarge it unnaturally. The teacher recalled Shaykh al-Bouti's saying that the Islamic awakening is not the crowd in mosques but giving up fraud in dealings, and that he chose small tomatoes over large ones.
  • It is permitted to use artificial means to increase an animal's milk, but not if it harms the animal or makes the milk harmful.
  • It is forbidden to deliberately feed animals whose meat is eaten with impure things without need; but the meat is not disliked until the impurity shows in the body. The teacher noted that on the school's relied-upon view eating a "jalla" animal is disliked (not forbidden), while al-Rafi'i held forbidden.

Resolution 104 (4/24) — Conditions and standards for halal certification

  • The Shari'a has clear rulings on halal and haram; neglecting them is a grave sin and can cancel other good deeds. Bodies that issue halal certificates must act with full verification.
  • Meat products are permitted provided the animal's lawfulness and slaughter are confirmed; products containing forbidden substances may not be certified except by those with knowledge of the Shari'a's rulings and specialists who are trustworthy Muslims; the word of a non-Muslim or one unaware of the rulings is not counted. Muslims should set up their own laboratories, but may rely on a reliable lab run by non-Muslims where none exists, with Muslim oversight as far as possible. Certification is among the most delicate responsibilities, to be done only by a body made of specialist scholars and muftis and God-fearing Muslim specialists, who supervise the slaughter themselves.

Resolution 105 (5/24) — Donating human organs and parts

  • Human blood is vital; donating it to someone who needs it, on a skilled doctor's prescription, is permitted regardless of religion. The teacher cited Shaykh Fadl that donation is permitted as it is like surplus (not a sale, since a free person cannot be sold in whole or in part), and said an elder had once insisted the donor be a pious Muslim. A Muslim may give blood to a blood bank that gives it free; blood camps and banks are permitted as a humanitarian service. The resolution calls giving blood among the most important duties if one's blood type matches; the teacher said "most important duty" is not literally an obligation in the school, and the donor must not be harmed (a friend told of being harmed by being bled while ill, which he linked to a bank's wish to collect the largest amount).
  • Part of a liver may be given to a friend or relative since it regrows; selling and buying it is forbidden in all cases (Shaykh Fadl: a free person owns neither all nor part of himself). The teacher asked whether a slave's organs could be sold since the master owns the slave, and answered no.
  • It is not permitted to set up a human milk bank, and if one exists it is not permitted to supply it or cooperate with it (the milk bank discussion was in the previous class). Banks storing semen of a man or woman's reproductive material, and sales of it, are forbidden. A student asked about freezing eggs for an unmarried woman wishing to preserve fertility: the teacher said keeping them may be fine if no uncovering of the awra is needed beyond necessity, but fertilization later by a husband would be a problem; and whether the egg is covered by the ruling on reproductive material is a question for research, noting Shabramallisi's view that the mother is the egg's owner.
  • A cornea may not be transferred from a living person; the seminar postponed the ruling on the cornea of the dead, whereas the International Academy allowed it (if bequeathed or the heirs permit); the teacher said this still needs long study, including whether heirs control it.

The class stopped before the seminar's statement on protecting education from sectarian coloring.

Q&A: Names of Weekdays and Months With Pagan Origins

A student in America asked about English weekday and month names that come from Greek and Norse gods, which no one has in mind today. The teacher gave two principles: (1) speaking a foreign phrase whose meaning you do not know and have not seen trusted Muslims use is forbidden, as it might be a statement of disbelief; (2) permitted: addressing someone who bears a name like that (such as "Abd Manaf," a name in the Prophet's lineage, or 'Abd al-Masih); not permitted: calling on or addressing the deity itself ("O god of such") since that gives it divinity. For weekday names like "Thursday" (from Thor) used merely as names of days, the teacher said there is no addressing or worship, and no effect, even if a name were "Satan," just as the Arabs named days.
