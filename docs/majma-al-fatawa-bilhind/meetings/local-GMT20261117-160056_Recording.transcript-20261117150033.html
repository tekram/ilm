Overview

Book 1: Tuhfat al-Muhtaj, Kitab al-Nikah

1. The Witnesses: What They Must Hear and Why

Ruling: the marriage is valid only in the presence of two witnesses, whether or not they were sought out (by chance is enough). They must hear the offer and the acceptance, meaning the parts on which validity depends ("I married you so-and-so / my ward" and "I accept her marriage"), not the mention of the dowry.

  • Evidence: "no marriage without a guardian and two upright witnesses; any marriage otherwise is void."
  • Reason: caution regarding the private part and protecting marriages from denial. The teacher noted this wording is also in al-Mughni. A method with no safeguard against denial is invalid: so overhearing from behind a wall is not enough, even if certain the two heard, and even if the guardian and husband are known to be alone in the room; likewise a contract by video link (Skype, Zoom and the like) is not valid, since two witnesses must be at the place of contract, both present together. If the contract was done and one witness informed then the contract repeated for the other, it is invalid.
  • It is recommended to have a group of righteous people present, in addition to the two.

2. Conditions of the Two Witnesses

  • Agreed conditions: complete freedom, verified maleness, and being human (not jinn), per Ibn al-'Imad: so a marriage is not valid with a slave or a jinn, unless the jinn's outward uprightness is known, as with leading prayer and counting toward the forty for Friday.
  • Not valid with a woman or a khuntha, unless the khuntha later proves a man (as for the guardian). The difference from marrying a bride who later proves female or male: the witness and guardian are secondary to the contract (they exist for another's sake), whereas the bride is the object of the contract, a basic pillar.
  • By the same reasoning, if someone marries a woman while doubting whether she is a forbidden relative (thinking she is his foster sister) and she later proves not to be, the marriage is not valid (as the two scholars said, against al-Rawyani). The teacher noted the gloss says this is relied upon, even though people may not notice it, as the "reality" is normally the criterion in contracts.
  • Also conditions: being Muslim, accountable, upright, hearing (since the testimony concerns a statement, which requires actual hearing), sight (statements are only proven by seeing and hearing), speech, maturity of judgment (rushd), no demeaning trade that harms dignity, not forgetful, and knowing the language of the two parties.
  • The blind: one view accepts him as qualified to witness in general; the sounder view is no, even if he knows the spouses; likewise someone in thick darkness; and the deaf. The reason: seeing is needed to bear testimony of the speakers.
  • Language: one view says it suffices that he retains the wording and has it translated afterward. Per the relied-upon view the witness must understand at the time of speaking, so a later translation does not suffice, even before the other side's acceptance. This differs from the earlier rule about a guardian who does not know the language and has it translated, since there the condition is that the husband accepts what he knows, whereas here the condition is that the witness understands at the time of bearing testimony.

3. Sons, Enemies and Close Relatives

  • The marriage is valid with the sons of the two spouses (each one's son, or both) outwardly and inwardly, though it is better that they not attend. The teacher said "better not" may refer to the case where they are the only two witnesses.
  • Also valid with the spouses' enemies and with their grandfathers (the bride's father and father's father are guardians and cannot be witnesses since they are the contracting party); a witness who differs in religion from the bride's guardian is accepted as a witness but cannot be her guardian. If she is a slave and her father cannot marry her off, he may be a witness if upright.

4. Hidden Uprightness (Mastur al-'Adala)

  • The marriage is valid outwardly with two whose uprightness is hidden: those about whom nothing sinful is known (as relied upon by a group), or those whose outward uprightness is known but who were not vouched for ("tazkiya"), which is the author's chosen view, which he said is the truth. Their hiddenness is nullified if an upright person impugns them; a sinner who repented at the contract is not equated with the hidden. It is recommended to seek the hidden, as the sounder view holds, since marriage is common among ordinary and sinful people; requiring knowledge of inner uprightness would be burdensome.
  • The author preferred in Nukat al-Tanbih (as did Ibn al-Salah) that if the contracting party is a judge, inner uprightness is certainly required, since he can easily consult those who vouch for people; al-Mutawalli held there is no difference. Ibn Hajar's synthesis (adopted in al-Tuhfa, against al-Nihaya and al-Mughni): the judge may not carry out the contract except in the presence of two whose uprightness he has verified, but this is a condition for proceeding, not for validity: if he contracted with two hidden persons who prove upright, it is valid; and if another person contracted with two who prove immoral, it is not, since reality is the criterion.
  • Does the husband have to investigate the guardian and witnesses? Al-Haniti (and in effect the school) says no; some later scholars made it obligatory ("Kanz al-Raghibin": he is sinful for leaving it even if the marriage is valid) because proceeding while doubting a condition is not permitted. Ibn Hajar answers that the doubt that blocks proceeding applies only to the spouses, who are the object of the contract and need more caution; the guardian and witnesses are secondary, so he may proceed unless a defect is known; if a defect turns out, the marriage is void.
  • A witness whose Islam or freedom is hidden: the marriage is valid if he proves to be Muslim or free, even in a place where everyone is Muslim or free, since the reality governs.
  • Class Q&A on usury: a student asked whether a man who takes an interest-bearing loan loses his uprightness as a witness. The teacher: taking an interest-based loan from a bank when not forced is a major sin, so his testimony at the marriage would not be accepted as upright (unlike the hidden); he said "the corruption has spread" does not change the ruling, and for hidden uprightness it suffices on the relied-upon view that no immorality is known of him, even if no outward uprightness is known. Whether a small amount of interest is minor or major he left for research, noting that the hadith are severe even about a single dirham, and that theft of a small amount is lesser because the hadd falls.

Book 2: Fatawa al-Hindi (Islamic Fiqh Academy of India)

Resolution 109 (4/25) — Unity of the Ummah

  • Unity of the Ummah is an urgent necessity today; the greatest corruption of the age is the harmful disagreement it suffers from. There are natural, praiseworthy disagreements that do no harm, but those that exceed Shari'a limits become a slow poison.
  • Jurisprudential disagreement (over what is preferable or not, or lawful and unlawful, as matters of ijtihad): believing one's own view alone is true and the other false is incorrect; condemning or declaring wrong the other's ijtihad is not right; and such disputes should not be spread among the general public. There is no objection to someone explaining his own school with evidence, even recommended at times, and if other schools are mentioned the evidence should be given with fairness; calm in speech and respect for dissenting scholars are required.
  • Creedal matters: it is valid to establish one's creed with evidence, avoiding inflammatory language; avoid insulting other schools even when the other side speaks improperly.
  • Critiquing misguided ideas: a big difference between criticizing a misguided but non-kufr belief and criticizing a belief that necessitates kufr and declaring its holders kafirs. In the latter, avoid declaring holders kafir as far as possible and take caution; cooperation with them is permitted for religious, social and necessary interests. Criticism aims only at establishing truth and refuting falsehood, with no personal motives; avoid inflaming the opponent's zeal; show justice and tolerance; advise gently, not harshly; and no impugning of intentions (the teacher stressed this principle, even where the outward Shari'a might seem to allow).
  • Sunnis and Shi'a: disagreement has become severe, with war, displacement, sedition and mass killing, and enemies have exploited it; killing one another without mercy is not a legitimate jihad but corruption on earth. Peaceful efforts and dialogue are needed; Sunnis and Shi'a living together in shared communities should live peacefully on shared values, avoid cursing and insulting each other's sacred figures (the teacher added: to curse the Companions is a burden on the one doing it), and scholars and leaders of both should play a role against divisiveness.

Class Discussion: Takfir and Who Is Fighting Whom

A student asked about the danger when one Shi'i branch aids a sect (the Nusayris) whose disbelief is not in doubt, in war against Sunnis, referring to the recent war in Syria. The teacher's points, in summary:

  • Much fighting among Muslims is political rather than creedal, and people are often moved by politics and desire while presenting it as defending faith; as an example, parties on opposite sides in Yemen each cast the other as "the Magians" or "the Muslims." Caution is required where blood is concerned, and where uncertain it is better to extend the disagreement to find points of agreement than to reach a final verdict.
  • Aiding unbelievers against Muslims is a sin and does not reach kufr in itself.
  • Declaring killing lawful: it does not amount to kufr if they believe their victims deserve death for something (such as not believing in "wilaya" or for being deviant or disobedient) and not simply for being Muslim; killing a Muslim for being a Muslim would be kufr. He called this "a great doubt that removes kufr," as one doubt can remove a thousand proofs when it comes to the sanctity of blood and the ruling of Muslim versus kafir. They are disobedient sinners, but not outside Islam on this ground.
  • The Nusayris: a student noted that the ruling that the Nusayris as a sect are disbelievers is long established among scholars living near them (Ibn 'Abidin and earlier, and in Ottoman-era fatwas), since their beliefs were gathered and known. The teacher said that as a sect described in books the ruling stands, but to apply it to a particular person one must hear from him or know his state, and being unsure of someone's disbelief is better than being categorical and finding there was a doubt that removes it; one who comes as a Muslim and says he does not hold those beliefs is accepted. Another student replied that the sect's declared beliefs are widely documented, including their own statements and the "esoteric" teaching of concealment; the teacher said the ruling on an apostate sect that inherited apostasy is that whoever comes in is accepted, but for an individual one must know him individually, and that in any case caution is required for the sake of the Ummah.
  • The teacher ended by advising the student that his words carry weight as someone people refer to, to be careful with each statement, and to try to bring factions together rather than side with one, even one that is right.

The class stopped before the section on dialogue between religions.
